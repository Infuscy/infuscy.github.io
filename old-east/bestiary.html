The vampire’s paper trail

  • The word: a Novgorod priest signed himself “Upir Likhyi” in a colophon dated 1047, often called the first written vampire. The colophon survives only in 15th–16th-century copies, and “Upir” may simply be a personal name. Firmer is the anti-pagan Word of St Gregory (11th–13th century): it says the Slavs first sacrificed to “upyri and bereginy”, later to Rod and the Rozhanitsy, then to Perun.
  • The spade: at Sozopol, Bulgaria, archaeologists found two men buried about 700 years ago with iron rods driven through the chest (2012). Bulgarian historians count over a hundred such burials and say the practice survived in some villages into the 1900s. At Pień, Poland, a 17th-century young woman was found in 2022 with a sickle across her neck and a padlock on her big toe.
  • The paper: on 26 January 1732 Austrian military surgeons signed Visum et Repertum, their report on the “vampires” of Medveđa in Serbia. It caused a sensation across Europe and made the Serbian word vampir international. Emily Gerard’s 1885 essay passed the Romanian remedies, stake, garlic and burned heart, to Bram Stoker.
  • The present: in 2004, in Marotinu de Sus in Dolj County, relatives exhumed a 76-year-old man who had died the previous December. They cut out and burned his heart, and a sick relative drank the ashes in water. Six men were convicted of illegally exhuming a corpse and given six-month sentences that they did not have to serve.
