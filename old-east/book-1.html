Book I

Charms against the eye, fright and fever

These are the workhorses of village magic: short healing charms that every family knew someone able to say. Two of them, the fever-sisters and Avestița, have the longest documented pedigree in this book.

I.1 Descântecul de deochi — lifting the evil eye

Romania, with twins in Serbia, Bulgaria and Greece. For a sudden headache, yawning and listlessness, or a baby crying after being admired.

You need: a cup of unbegun water, three or nine live embers from the hearth (burnt matches will do), a knife with a black handle or a sprig of basil.

  1. At dawn, drop the embers into the water one by one, counting down from nine. If they sink, the eye is on the person.
  2. Stir the water crosswise with the knife while saying the words three times.
  3. The sufferer takes three sips. Wet the forehead, temples, chest and palms with the sign of the cross.
  4. Throw what is left at a fence corner or tree root where no one walks.

Keeping it off: a red thread on the wrist; a dot of soot on a baby’s forehead or behind the ear; after praising a child, spit lightly three times and say să nu-l deochi (“lest I eye him”). Ashkenazi Jews of the same towns said keyn ayin-hore (“no evil eye”) and tied the same red thread.

Twins: in Serbia the rite is called gašenje ugljevlja, “quenching the coals”. Greek xematiasma drops olive oil into the water instead; if the drops spread, the eye is present.

Age: Medieval / early modern

A Romanian evil-eye charm invoking Saints Cosmas and Damian was copied into the Codex Sturdzanus between 1609 and 1618, and it ends with the “clean, bright” formula still used today. The belief itself is ancient: Plutarch’s Table Talk (5.7) already debates how a glance can harm.

I.2 Turnatul cerii — pouring out the fright

Ukraine, Belarus, Romania, Hungary. For fright (Romanian sperietură, Ukrainian pereliak): a child who stammers, startles or wets the bed after a dog attack or a fall.

You need: beeswax (stubs of church candles), a small pot set in hot water, a bowl of unbegun water, a cloth.

  1. Seat the sufferer facing east and cover the head with the cloth.
  2. Hold the bowl over the head and pour in the melted wax.
  3. Read the shape that sets: a dog, a man, a snake, fire. That is the shape of the fear.
  4. Repeat at dawn on three days, until the wax sets smooth. Bury the wax or give it to running water.

Safety: Hungarian ólomöntés and some Romanian charmers poured molten lead. Lead fumes are toxic and molten metal burns badly: use beeswax, melted in a water bath.

Age: Folk-recorded, widespread

Known from Finland to the Balkans, but written down mostly from the 19th century. Anything older is a guess.

I.3 The child who will not sleep — Muma Pădurii and the Dawn-maidens

Romania, Russia, Ukraine. For a baby who cries all night.

In Romania, Muma Pădurii (the Forest Mother) steals a child’s sleep and leaves her own wailing in its place. In Russia, the culprits are the kriksy, the night-criers. The cure in both is a trade: give back the crying, ask for the sleep.

  1. At dusk or first light, carry the child to the threshold or an open window.
  2. Face the forest (Romania) or the dawn (Russia) and say the words three times.
  3. Spit lightly three times over the left shoulder and go in without looking back.

Age: Folk-recorded

Russian charms to the Dawn sisters appear in L. N. Maikov’s 1869 collection. The personified Dawns (Zorile) are also sung to at funerals in south-western Romania. A link to an Indo-European dawn goddess is often claimed; it is plausible and unprovable.

I.4 Bone to bone — the sprain charm

Romania, Russia, Belarus; the same words in German, Gaelic and Sanskrit. For sprains and wrenched joints.

  1. Warm a little oil or goose fat and rub it into the joint, always toward the heart.
  2. Russian and Belarusian healers then “sew” the sprain: they stitch a needle and thread through a cloth laid over it, without touching the skin. The patient asks “What are you sewing?” and the healer answers “Torn flesh and sinew I sew.”
  3. Bind the joint and repeat on three mornings.

Safety: if you cannot bear weight on it, it may be broken. Get an X-ray.

Age: Ancient root

This is the most famous charm formula in Europe. The Second Merseburg Charm (10th-century manuscript) ends ben zi bena, bluot zi bluoda, lid zi geliden: “bone to bone, blood to blood, limb to limb.” The Sanskrit Atharvaveda (4.12) joins “marrow to marrow, joint to joint”, and Carmichael recorded the same words in a Gaelic sprain charm. Scholars still argue whether this is shared Indo-European inheritance or a medieval Christian charm that travelled.

I.5 The fever-sisters — St Sisinnius and the twelve shakers

Russia, Bulgaria, Serbia, Romania. For fevers and chills.

Saint Sisinnius (in other versions the Archangel Michael) sees twelve wild-haired women rise out of the sea: the Fevers, daughters of King Herod. He seizes them and beats them until they confess their names and swear that wherever their names are read, they will not come. The Russian list Afanasyev gives (names vary by manuscript): Tryaseya the Shaker, Ogneya the Burner, Ledeya the Freezer, Gneteya the Crusher, Grynusha the Chest-presser, Glukheya the Deafener, Lomeya the Breaker, Pukhneya the Swelling, Zhelteya the Yellowing, Korkusha the Cramper, Glyadeya the Watcher, Neveya the Deadly.

  1. Write the legend and all twelve names on paper.
  2. Read it over the sick person three times at dawn, or fold it and hang it round the neck.
  3. When the fever breaks, burn the paper.

Age: Medieval, with a late-antique root

The Slavonic Sisinnius prayer was translated from Greek no later than the 12th century. The 11th-century Glagolitic Euchologium Sinaiticum already holds a cycle of prayers against fever, and Slavonic lists of forbidden books condemned such “false prayers”. The idea that naming a demon disarms it is older still: the Testament of Solomon (early centuries CE) has Solomon interrogate demons for their names.

I.6 Avestița — the childbed amulet

Romania, with Greek, Slavic and Jewish twins. To guard a mother and newborn in the forty days after birth.

Coming down from the Mount of Olives, Saint Sisoe sees the Archangel Michael seize Avestița, “the Wing of Satan”, a demon sent to stop the birth of Christ. She harms women in childbed and infants. Michael forces her to reveal her tricks and her names, and sends her back to the depths of hell until the Last Judgment. Wherever her names are written, she cannot enter.

Her names. Usually nineteen. The manuscripts include Avezuha, Navadariia, Samca, Solomiia, Viștița, Scormela, Huluba, Grapa, Muha, Puha and Zlaia.

  1. Copy the legend and all nineteen names onto paper (a zapis). Some copies add a drawing of the demon, which keeps her off too.
  2. Fold it and hang it at the mother’s bed, or sew it into the baby’s swaddling.
  3. Keep it in place through the forty days after the birth.

Age: Ancient root, and the best-documented chain in this book

In “Two Thousand Years of a Charm against the Child-Stealing Witch” (Folk-Lore, 1900), Moses Gaster traced the Romanian text back through Greek charms of St Sisinnios against the demon Gylou, the child-stealing Gello of classical Greece. Jewish amulets against Lilith, naming three guardian angels, are the same story; Ashkenazi families across Eastern Europe hung a kimpet-tsetl (childbed note) for the same purpose. A 6th-century painting at the Coptic monastery of Bawit in Egypt shows Sisinnios on horseback spearing a female demon. In Romanian, the Sisoe legend is in the Codex Sturdzanus (1590–1602); the oldest dated Avestița copy is from 1752.
