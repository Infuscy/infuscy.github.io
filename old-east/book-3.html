The Old Book of the East

Book III

House, herd and field

This is communal magic: rites a whole village performed against plague, cattle-death, witches and hail. Several are dated by chronicles and church bans, which makes this the best-attested book of all.

III.1 The plague shirt — cămașa ciumii

Romania (Oltenia, Făgăraș), Serbia’s Timok valley, Bulgaria. Against plague and cholera; later, to protect soldiers going to war.

  1. Gather a group of old women, often widows: three, seven or nine. Some villages wanted them all to share a name, such as seven Marias near Făgăraș.
  2. Between dusk and dawn, they spin, weave, cut and sew one whole shirt. When plague broke out in Serbia in 1837, Prince Miloš Obrenović had nine old women do it naked and in secret, by hearth-light, without a candle.
  3. Carry it to the crossroads with food, singing, shouting and banging dishes. Throw the shirt to the wind and leave the food “for the Plague”.
  4. Alternatively, hold it open on the road and have everyone crawl through it, children first.

Bulgarian twin: in eastern Bulgaria, “pure women” wove a cloth (chumino) in a single night from the wool of a black ram. At night the household crawled through it naked outside the village, men and women in separate groups. Ethnographer Dimitar Marinov recorded it in the early 20th century.

Age: Medieval / early modern

The shirt is first mentioned in the Romanian lands in Anton Maria Del Chiaro’s Istoria delle moderne rivoluzioni della Valachia (Venice, 1718); Prince Miloš’s was made in 1837, and Tudor Pamfile recorded the rite in Oltenia in 1916. It is still alive: men of the Timok valley carried threads from such shirts to the Yugoslav wars in 1991 and to Kosovo in 1999. The Russian twin is the “one-day church” (obydennaya tserkov’), raised from fresh logs by a whole town in 24 hours. The Novgorod chronicle records the first in 1390, and Vologda still raised one in October 1654.

III.2 Ploughing the circle — opakhivanie

Russia, Ukraine, Belarus; Bulgaria, Serbia. To lock an epidemic out of the village.

  • The Russian form: at midnight the village women, often naked or in white shifts with hair unbound, drew a furrow round the whole village. One of them pulled the plough while the rest beat pans and carried icons and brooms. In the accounts, any creature they met was taken for “Cow Death” in disguise.
  • The Bulgarian form: twin blacksmiths forge the ploughshare in a single night, naked and silent, stopping when the cock crows. Twin brothers yoke twin oxen and plough one or three furrows round the village.

Age: Medieval, with an 8th-century hint

Dimitar Marinov recorded the Bulgarian rite in the early 20th century, already rare and only in eastern Bulgaria. A 2012 study cites naked men ploughing round Tarnovo in the Black Death of 1347 and round Lyaskovets in the cholera of 1837. A Frankish list of pagan practices from about 743, the Indiculus superstitionum, already condemns “furrows around farmsteads”.

III.3 Living fire — focul viu, zhivoy ogon’, nodfyr

Romania, the Slavic lands, Germanic and Gaelic Europe. Against cattle plague.

  1. Put out every fire in the village.
  2. Make new fire by friction only: a dry pole spun against a plank, or a rope sawn round a post. In Serbia a boy and a girl did it, naked, in a dark room. In Bulgaria two naked men rubbed branches together in the forest.
  3. Drive the cattle through the smoke or over the embers, and scatter the ashes on the fields.
  4. Every household relights its hearth from the new fire.

In the Rhodopes, a tree set alight by lightning counted as living fire: every hearth in the village was put out and relit from it.

Safety: treat it like any bushcraft fire: bare ground, water at hand, animals kept at a sane distance.

Age: Early medieval

The same Indiculus (c. 743) condemns “fire made by rubbing wood, that is, nodfyr”. The rite ran from Scotland to Russia into the 19th century.

III.4 St George’s Eve — guarding the milk

Romania (Sângeorz), Bulgaria (Gergyovden), Serbia and North Macedonia (Đurđevdan). The night of 22–23 April.

The threat: before dawn, witches go naked into other people’s pastures and drag a towel or rope through the dew. They are gathering the mană, the fruitfulness of cows and fields. Next day your cow gives water instead of milk.

  1. At dusk on the eve, set green branches (willow, beech, birch) and sods of green turf on the gate, over the doors and on the cowshed.
  2. Rub garlic on the cows’ horns and udders, and on the doorposts.
  3. In Maramureș, sheep going out to pasture are passed over a chain, an axe and a small fire.
  4. At dawn, wash your own face in the dew.

A tale told across the region: a farmer hiding in his field hears a witch chant “I take all, I take all!” as she drags her towel. He mutters, “And I take half”, and next morning his cow fills every bucket in the house. Irish and Scottish May Eve lore tells of witches “skimming the dew” the same way. On this night buried treasure also burns with a bluish flame: throw a garment over the spot and dig there later.

Age: Folk-recorded, widespread

The same dew-witch appears from Ireland to Macedonia, on May Eve in the west and St George’s Eve in the east. Such a spread suggests medieval roots at least.

III.5 The house spirit — moving the Domovoi, sparing the house snake

Russia, Ukraine, Belarus; Romania, Lithuania.

The Domovoi (“house one”, or simply “Grandfather”) lives behind the stove. Leave him bread and salt, milk or porridge there, and never curse in his hearing.

  1. When you move, scoop embers from the old stove into a pot, or invite him into an old bast shoe.
  2. Bow at the old threshold and say: Dedushka domovoy, poydyom s nami v novyy dom, “Grandfather Domovoi, come with us to the new house.”
  3. At the new house, let the cat in first, then tip the embers into the new stove.

In Romania the guardian is the house snake (șarpele casei), living under the threshold or in the foundation. Never kill it: its death foretells the master’s. Leave it a saucer of milk. Lithuanians kept the grass snake (žaltys) in the same way.

Age: Folk-recorded

Described in detail only from the 18th–19th centuries; most scholars see an ancestor spirit behind him.

III.6 The shadow in the foundation — the builder’s rite

Romania, Bulgaria, Serbia, Greece, Hungary. To make a building stand.

In the ballad, Meșterul Manole walls his own wife into the monastery of Curtea de Argeș so that it stops collapsing. Serbia’s Building of Skadar, Greece’s Bridge of Arta and Hungary’s Kelemen the Mason tell the same story, in some 700 local variants.

The lore: masons “stole” a passer-by’s shadow by measuring it with a reed and walling the reed into the foundation. The victim died within forty days and became the building’s unseen guardian, its stafie. Passers-by were warned not to let their shadow be taken. Emily Gerard (1888) heard of professional “shadow-traders” who supplied builders with victims.

The kindly version, still common: under the cornerstone, put a coin, bread and salt, and a sprig of basil, and pour out a little wine. In many Balkan villages a rooster is killed over the foundation.

Age: Folk-recorded

Vasile Alecsandri first printed the Manole ballad in 1852–53. The shadow looks like a softened form of foundation sacrifice, which is found worldwide, but the order of development is guesswork.

III.7 Driving off the hail — Solomonari, bells and willow

Romania; the Hungarian garabonciás, the Serbian zduhać. When a hail cloud comes.

The lore: the Solomonari are wandering scholars trained at the Șolomanța, a school beneath a lake or at the end of the world. They ride dragons through the clouds and bring hail where they are offended. One may come to your door dressed as a ragged beggar: refuse him at your peril.

  1. Ring the church bell. Western bells were often cast with the words fulgura frango, “I break the lightning”.
  2. Burn a few of the willow catkins blessed on Florii (Palm Sunday) and kept by the icons, and set the Easter candle in the window.
  3. Drive an axe into the yard, blade up, to cut the cloud.
  4. Feed the next beggar who knocks.

Age: Folk-recorded, with a medieval background

The name links them to Solomon, master of demons in medieval lore, and Moses Gaster wrote the classic study of them (1884). Emily Gerard’s 1885 essay “Transylvanian Superstitions” turned their school into the “Scholomance”, where the Count of Bram Stoker’s Dracula (1897) learned his black arts.
