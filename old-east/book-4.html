The Old Book of the East

Book IV

Divination

Telling the future was the most everyday magic of all: a woman in Brodina, Suceava, recalled her mother casting beans before deciding whether to set out on a journey. Two of these methods descend from books that the Church banned.

IV.1 The onion calendar

Romania, Hungary. New Year’s Eve. To forecast the rain of the coming year.

  1. Cut an onion in half and peel off twelve cup-shaped layers.
  2. Name each for a month, January to December, and line them up.
  3. Put a pinch of salt in each and leave them overnight on the table or windowsill.
  4. In the morning, the cups holding water mark the wet months; the dry ones mark drought.

Age: Folk-recorded

The idea behind it, that the twelve days of Christmastide foretell the twelve months, is medieval and widespread.

IV.2 Casting the 41 beans — datul în bobi

Romania, Moldova, Bosnia (bacanje graha), Russia; the Turkic steppe. For any yes-or-no question: a journey, a match, a lost animal.

  1. Take 41 dried beans or corn kernels. Mix them in a circle on a sieve or a cloth while saying your question.
  2. Split them at random into three heaps. From each heap remove four at a time until one to four remain.
  3. Lay the remainders in a row of three, then gather the rest and repeat twice more. You now have a three-by-three grid.
  4. Read it: Romanian practice reads the number and position of beans on the three lines. Many readers treat the top row as the head (thoughts), the middle as the heart, the bottom as the road.

There was never one fixed key: each bean-reader read in her own way.

Age: Folk-recorded; origin unknown

The Kazakh and Kyrgyz kumalak uses the same 41 counters, the same removal by fours and the same nine-square grid. That points to an old Eurasian route, probably through Turkic or Persian hands, though no one has traced it.

IV.3 Sieve and shears — finding a thief

Pan-European, England to Russia. To name who took something.

  1. Stick the points of an open pair of shears into the rim of a sieve.
  2. Two people balance the shears’ handles on their fingertips, so the sieve hangs free.
  3. Ask: “By Saints Peter and Paul, did (name) take it?” for each suspect.
  4. At the guilty name, the sieve turns or falls.

Age: Ancient root

Theocritus (3rd century BCE) mentions a country-woman “sieve-diviner” (koskinomantis) in his third Idyll, and Artemidorus lists sieve-diviners among the fortune-tellers of the 2nd century CE. Whether they used shears is not recorded.

IV.4 The thunder book — Gromovnicul

Romania, Bulgaria, Serbia, Russia. To read the year from its first thunder.

The gromovnic (from Slavonic gromŭ, “thunder”) is a manuscript book of omens, arranged by the signs of the zodiac. Thunder heard under a given sign, by day or by night, foretells war, famine, disease or the fate of rulers. Many copies add earthquakes, lightning and hail.

  1. Note the sign of the zodiac, and whether it is day or night, when you hear thunder.
  2. Look up the entry and read the year’s fortune.
  3. At that first thunder, villagers rubbed their backs against a wall or tree, or touched a stone to the head, so as to be strong all year.

Age: Medieval, with a classical root

Romanian gromovnics survive from 1636 (Sărata Oltului). The most widespread version is ascribed to the Byzantine emperor Heraclius (“Iraclie împărat”), probably printed at Govora in 1639. A Romanian index of forbidden books from 1667–1669 lists the genre among heretical books. It continues the Greek brontologion. John Lydus (6th century) preserved a Roman thunder calendar by Nigidius Figulus (1st century BCE), who claimed Etruscan sources.

IV.5 The body’s omens — the twitching book

Romania, Bulgaria, the Slavic lands.

  • Right palm itches: money is coming. Left palm: money is going.
  • Right eye twitches: joy, or a dear face. Left eye: tears.
  • Ears ringing: someone is speaking of you.
  • Hiccups: someone is thinking of you.

Age: Medieval

Slavonic trepetniki (“twitching books”) listed what each tremor of the body foretold, and the Church banned them with the thunder books. Their model is a Greek treatise on twitching ascribed to Melampus, from the Hellenistic period.

IV.6 Choosing your Babă — the first nine days of March

Romania, Moldova; Bulgaria’s Baba Marta. 1–9 March.

Baba Dochia drives her sheep up the mountain in March, shedding one of her nine sheepskin coats each warm day, until the frost turns her to stone.

  1. Before 1 March, secretly pick one day between the 1st and the 9th: that is “your Babă”.
  2. Watch its weather. Sunny and mild means a good year for you; rain or sleet means a sad or troubled one.
  3. Wear the red-and-white mărțișor until you see the first stork or a tree in blossom, then hang it on a branch.

Age: Medieval root, with a 19th-century myth on top

The name almost certainly comes from St Eudokia (Evdochia), whose feast falls on 1 March. The romantic story of Dochia as a daughter of the Dacian king Decebal, fleeing the emperor Trajan, was put into verse by Gheorghe Asachi after an 1838 trip to Mount Ceahlău. B. P. Hasdeu already rejected attempts to read “Dochia” as “Dacia”. The 1 March customs of Bulgaria, North Macedonia, Moldova and Romania were inscribed together on UNESCO’s intangible heritage list in 2017.

IV.7 Drawing down the moon

Bulgaria (Strandzha, the Rhodopes), Greece (Epirus). Witch-lore to know rather than a rite to perform.

On certain nights the magyosnitsi (witches), or in some villages “wise men”, pull the moon down from the sky. On earth she turns into a cow or a calf. They milk her, and moon-milk cures any illness or makes their own cows yield more. Older Greek versions from Epirus have women in white play music, dance, offer the moon food and invite her down to tell them the future. South Slavic lore even explains the moon’s dark spots as stains from her time on earth.

Age: Ancient root

Aristophanes’ Clouds (423 BCE) jokes about hiring a Thessalian witch to pull the moon down, and Plato’s Gorgias mentions the Thessalian women who draw down the moon. The Frankish Indiculus (c. 743) condemns the belief that women “command the moon” and can “take away men’s hearts”. Few motifs in this book span 2,400 years so clearly, though no one knows how it travelled.
