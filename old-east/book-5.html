Book V

Curses, bindings and their undoing

Folk tradition was blunt about curses: they work only when earned. The curse of a wronged mother, orphan or widow “catches”; an undeserved one comes back on the person who cast it. That is why most of this book is about undoing.

V.1 The art of the blestem — a formulary of curses

Romania, Bulgaria, Serbia, Russia.

Romanian curses run on the optative “-ar”, “may it…”. The heaviest is a wish that the earth refuse the body, so that it never rots and walks as a strigoi. A curse said kneeling, with hair unbound, or before an icon with a candle was thought heavier still. In many villages only the one who laid a curse could lift it, by forgiving aloud.

Romanian folk song even has a genre for it, the abandoned girl’s curse on the lover who left her.

Age: Folk-recorded

The formulas were written down in the 19th century; the habit of formal cursing is as old as writing, as the more than a thousand surviving Greek and Roman curse tablets show.

V.2 The wedding padlock — legatul

Romania, the Balkans, the East Slavs; the “ligature” of medieval Western Europe.

The binding: while the couple is led three times round the table in church, an enemy snaps a padlock shut or ties knots in a thread, then throws the key into a well or river. The groom is “bound”: impotent, or the marriage barren, until it is undone.

Guarding a wedding:

  1. Kinswomen watch the guests for anyone fumbling in a pocket during the ceremony.
  2. Bride and groom carry garlic or a pin in their clothes.
  3. In Russia a trusted wedding-sorcerer or the best man went ahead of the procession to “close” the road against spoiling.

Undoing (dezlegare): find the lock and open it, or the knots and untie them. Failing that, a charmer ties nine knots in a red thread and unties them one by one, counting down from nine to none, then burns the thread.

Age: Early medieval

The Frankish Indiculus (c. 743) already condemns “ligatures”, though there the word means amulets tied on the body. Hincmar of Reims discussed impotence by sorcery in the 860s divorce case of King Lothar II, and the theme runs through European witchcraft writing for the next eight centuries.

V.3 Turning back the făcătură

Romania (făcătură, făcut), Bulgaria (magiya), Russia (porcha). When something has been “done” to you.

The signs: objects where none should be. A knot of hair under the threshold, a bundle of rags, needles, eggshells, grave soil, or (in Russia and Ukraine) a wreath of feathers grown inside a pillow. Then sudden illness, quarrels in the house, dying animals.

  1. Never touch the thing with bare hands. Lift it with tongs or two sticks.
  2. Burn it in the stove with incense, or give it to running water.
  3. Then speak the counter-charm. A Romanian unbinding recorded before 1912 runs: Cine-a făcut cu unu, / io desfac cu doi; / cine-a făcut cu doi, / io desfac cu trei (“Who made it with one, I unmake with two; who made it with two, I unmake with three”).
  4. A common Romanian cleansing follows: at dawn, at a crossroads, wash the afflicted with water from nine wells and nine sprigs of basil. Throw the water backward over the shoulder and go home without looking back.

The Orthodox route is still in use: a priest reads the exorcism prayers of St Basil the Great (Molitfele Sfântului Vasile) over the afflicted.

Age: Ancient root

Plato (Laws 11, 933b) describes Athenians alarmed by wax figures left at their doors, at crossroads or on their parents’ graves. The village forms are folk-recorded.

V.4 Footprints, hair and nails

Romania, the East Slavs.

What has touched you stays bound to you. Soil from your footprint (urma), dug up and dried in a pot of basil or above the stove, makes you “dry up” with longing and return. The same soil can be used to harm. Russians called it “taking out the track” (vynut’ sled).

  1. Burn or bury your hair combings and nail clippings; never leave them where a bird or a neighbour could take them.
  2. Do not leave clothes outside overnight.
  3. Keep a lock of a beloved’s hair yourself, and you hold the same power.

Age: Folk-recorded

James Frazer called this “contagious magic” and found it on every continent; its age is beyond reckoning.

V.5 The twisted ears — the zalom

Russia, Ukraine, Belarus, Poland. A curse on a field.

A sorcerer twists and knots a handful of standing grain, the zalom or zakrutka, in an enemy’s field. Whoever reaps it falls ill, and the harvest spoils.

  1. Do not touch it or reap it.
  2. A healer or priest pulls it out with an aspen stick (the aspen being deadly to evil) and burns it on the spot with prayers.

The mirror image: at the end of harvest, reapers left the last ears standing and tied them into “Volos’s beard”, an offering. Same gesture, opposite intent.

Age: Folk-recorded

The name Volos may preserve the pre-Christian god Volos (Veles), “god of cattle”, by whom the Rus’ swore in their 10th-century treaties with Byzantium.
