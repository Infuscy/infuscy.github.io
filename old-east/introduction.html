The Old Book of the East

Before you begin: what “old” means here

If you suspect that most spellbooks on the shelf are younger than they claim, you are right. Most descend from Wicca, a religion assembled in England in the 1940s–50s, not from anything the Celts did. The magic in this book was taken down from village charmers between about 1850 and 1950, and parts of it can be traced back a thousand years or more.

The modern shelf. Gerald Gardner launched Wicca publicly with Witchcraft Today (1954). He blended ceremonial magic (the Golden Dawn, founded 1888; Aleister Crowley), Freemasonry, Charles Leland’s Aradia (1899) and Margaret Murray’s long-discredited “witch-cult” theory (1921). The eight festivals of the “Wheel of the Year” were assembled in the 1950s–70s; only four of them have Celtic names. Ronald Hutton’s The Triumph of the Moon (1999) documents the whole story.

Real Celtic charms look like Eastern ones. Alexander Carmichael’s Carmina Gadelica (1900), gathered in the Scottish Highlands and Islands, is the best record of genuine Gaelic folk charms. Like the Romanian descântece in this book, they call on Christ and the saints, tell a short story and order the illness out. They look nothing like a Wiccan ritual.

Where the Eastern material comes from:

  • Folklorists’ fieldwork: Simion Florea Marian (1886, 1893), Tudor Pamfile (1911, 1916) and Artur Gorovei (1931) in Romania; the Bulgarian Sbornik za narodni umotvoreniya (from 1889); L. N. Maikov’s Great Russian Incantations (1869); Oskar Kolberg in Poland; Vuk Karadžić in Serbia.
  • Older written witnesses: charm manuscripts of the 17th–19th centuries, Byzantine amulets, medieval Slavonic sermons and lists of forbidden books, and church bans. The Council in Trullo (692) outlawed the Kalends feasts, masks, men dressed as women and the 1 March festival. All of them survived in Balkan villages anyway.
  • Ancient echoes: Greek writers describe rites strikingly close to ones still recorded in the 19th century. In Plato’s Charmides (4th century BCE), Socrates learns a healing charm from a Thracian physician of Zalmoxis, god of the Getae on the lower Danube, and explains that “these charms are fair words”.

The honest caveat. A charm recorded in 1890 is not automatically 2,000 years old. Claims of a pure “Dacian” or pre-Christian core are mostly 19th-century romantic nationalism and cannot be proven. The Christian layer (the Virgin Mary, St Sisinnius, the Archangel Michael) is not a later disguise: it is itself medieval, and part of what makes these texts old.

How the charms are given. Words appear in the original language with an English translation. Most are composite renderings built from formulas that recur across many recorded variants. Every village had its own version; there was never one “correct” text.

Safety. The Romanian mătrăgună is deadly nightshade, and some rites used molten lead or open fire; each is flagged where it appears. Healing charms were village medicine where there was no doctor, so keep them as heritage, not treatment. Folk belief itself warned that an undeserved curse returns to whoever cast it.
