# The Old Book of the East

*A grimoire of charms, curses and rites from Romania, the Balkans and the Slavic lands — with the evidence for how old each one really is.*

Nov 23, 2026 · @Florin

## Before you begin: what "old" means here

If you suspect that most spellbooks on the shelf are younger than they claim, you are right. Most descend from Wicca, a religion assembled in England in the 1940s–50s, not from anything the Celts did. The magic in this book was taken down from village charmers between about 1850 and 1950, and parts of it can be traced back a thousand years or more.

**The modern shelf.** Gerald Gardner launched Wicca publicly with *Witchcraft Today* (1954). He blended ceremonial magic (the Golden Dawn, founded 1888; Aleister Crowley), Freemasonry, Charles Leland's *Aradia* (1899) and Margaret Murray's long-discredited "witch-cult" theory (1921). The eight festivals of the "Wheel of the Year" were assembled in the 1950s–70s; only four of them have Celtic names. Ronald Hutton's *The Triumph of the Moon* (1999) documents the whole story.

**Real Celtic charms look like Eastern ones.** Alexander Carmichael's *Carmina Gadelica* (1900), gathered in the Scottish Highlands and Islands, is the best record of genuine Gaelic folk charms. Like the Romanian *descântece* in this book, they call on Christ and the saints, tell a short story and order the illness out. They look nothing like a Wiccan ritual.

**Where the Eastern material comes from:**

- **Folklorists' fieldwork:** Simion Florea Marian (1886, 1893), Tudor Pamfile (1911, 1916) and Artur Gorovei (1931) in Romania; the Bulgarian *Sbornik za narodni umotvoreniya* (from 1889); L. N. Maikov's *Great Russian Incantations* (1869); Oskar Kolberg in Poland; Vuk Karadžić in Serbia.
- **Older written witnesses:** charm manuscripts of the 17th–19th centuries, Byzantine amulets, medieval Slavonic sermons and lists of forbidden books, and church bans. The Council in Trullo (692) outlawed the Kalends feasts, masks, men dressed as women and the 1 March festival. All of them survived in Balkan villages anyway.
- **Ancient echoes:** Greek writers describe rites strikingly close to ones still recorded in the 19th century. In Plato's *Charmides* (4th century BCE), Socrates learns a healing charm from a Thracian physician of Zalmoxis, god of the Getae on the lower Danube, and explains that "these charms are fair words".

**The honest caveat.** A charm recorded in 1890 is not automatically 2,000 years old. Claims of a pure "Dacian" or pre-Christian core are mostly 19th-century romantic nationalism and cannot be proven. The Christian layer (the Virgin Mary, St Sisinnius, the Archangel Michael) is not a later disguise: it is itself medieval, and part of what makes these texts old.

**Evidence ratings, given on every entry:**

| Rating | What it means |
| --- | --- |
| Ancient root | A source older than about 1000 CE describes the same motif or rite. Continuity is plausible, rarely proven. |
| Medieval / early modern | Attested in writing between about 1000 and 1800: church bans, manuscripts, chronicles, travellers. |
| Folk-recorded, widespread | First written down 1850–1950, but found among several unrelated peoples. A sign of age, not proof. |
| Local or revived | Known from one region, or reshaped in modern times. |

**How the charms are given.** Words appear in the original language with an English translation. Most are composite renderings built from formulas that recur across many recorded variants. Every village had its own version; there was never one "correct" text.

**Safety.** The Romanian *mătrăgună* is deadly nightshade, and some rites used molten lead or open fire; each is flagged where it appears. Healing charms were village medicine where there was no doctor, so keep them as heritage, not treatment. Folk belief itself warned that an undeserved curse returns to whoever cast it.

## The anatomy of an Eastern charm

Almost every charm from the Carpathians to the Volga is built from the same six parts. Learn them and you can read any Romanian *descântec*, Russian *zagovor* or Bulgarian *baene*, and compose your own in the old manner.

1. **The threshold.** Work at dawn or dusk, before speaking to anyone. Use "unbegun water" (*apă neîncepută*), drawn first from the well, in silence. Crossroads, thresholds and riverbanks are the usual places: points where this world touches the other.
2. **The rising.** Many charms open with the speaker getting up and setting out. Romanian: *Sculatu-m-am, mânecatu-m-am* ("I rose, I rose early"). Russian: *Vstanu ya, blagoslovyas', poydu perekrestyas'* ("I shall rise, blessed; I shall go, crossed"), through the door and gate into the open field, to the white stone Alatyr.
3. **The story.** A short myth carries the cure. The sufferer sets out "on the road, on the path" (*pe cale, pe cărare*), meets the illness and is struck down. He weeps; no one hears him but the Mother of God, who comes down and sends him to the charmer. Scholars call this device a *historiola*. It appears in the Second Merseburg Charm (a 10th-century German manuscript) and in Egyptian and Mesopotamian spells far older.
4. **The banishment.** The illness is ordered out of every part of the body and sent "where the rooster does not crow, where the dog does not bark, where the priest does not strike the *toaca*" (the wooden semantron). Other Romanian charms send it "into the whirlpool of the seas, into the beating of the winds". Russian ones send it to the mosses and bogs "where people do not walk".
5. **The countdown.** "Nine, eight, seven… one, none." The trouble shrinks with the numbers until nothing is left.
6. **The seal.** Romanian: *Să rămână curat, luminat, ca argintul strecurat, cum Dumnezeu l-a lăsat* ("May he stay clean and bright as strained silver, as God left him"), then *De la mine descântecul, de la Dumnezeu leacul* ("From me the charm, from God the cure"). A Romanian charm copied between 1609 and 1618 already ends with the "clean, bright" formula. Russian charms lock themselves: "Be firm, my words, firmer than stone; I lock them and throw the key into the sea."

**After the words.** Leftover water is thrown where no one walks: a fence corner, a tree root, running water. The charmer spits lightly three times, and nobody looks back on the way home.

**The charmer.** Usually an older woman (*descântătoare*, *baba*, *znakharka*, the Polish *szeptucha* or "whisperer") who learned from a relative. The word *descântec* comes from the verb *descânta*, which is inherited from Latin: it goes back to an unattested *discantare*, "to sing away".

## Book I — Charms against the eye, fright and fever

These are the workhorses of village magic: short healing charms that every family knew someone able to say. Two of them, the fever-sisters and Avestița, have the longest documented pedigree in this book.

### I.1 Descântecul de deochi — lifting the evil eye

*Romania, with twins in Serbia, Bulgaria and Greece. For a sudden headache, yawning and listlessness, or a baby crying after being admired.*

**You need:** a cup of unbegun water, three or nine live embers from the hearth (burnt matches will do), a knife with a black handle or a sprig of basil.

1. At dawn, drop the embers into the water one by one, counting down from nine. If they sink, the eye is on the person.
2. Stir the water crosswise with the knife while saying the words three times.
3. The sufferer takes three sips. Wet the forehead, temples, chest and palms with the sign of the cross.
4. Throw what is left at a fence corner or tree root where no one walks.

**The words** (composite of recorded variants):

> *Deochi, deochetor, ieși din creștet, din cap, din ochi, din sprâncene, din inimă,*\
*du-te unde cocoșul nu cântă, unde câinele nu latră, unde popa nu toacă.*\
*De te-a deocheat bărbat, să-i plesnească ochii; de te-a deocheat muiere, să-i sece țâțele; de te-a deocheat fată mare, să-i cadă cosițele.*\
*Să rămână (numele) curat, luminat, ca argintul strecurat, cum Dumnezeu l-a lăsat. De la mine descântecul, de la Dumnezeu leacul.*

> Eye, eye-caster, come out of the crown, the head, the eyes, the brows, the heart;\
go where the rooster does not crow, where the dog does not bark, where the priest does not strike the *toaca*.\
If a man cast it, may his eyes burst; if a woman, may her breasts run dry; if a maiden, may her braids fall out.\
May (name) stay clean and bright as strained silver, as God left him. From me the charm, from God the cure.

**Keeping it off:** a red thread on the wrist; a dot of soot on a baby's forehead or behind the ear; after praising a child, spit lightly three times and say *să nu-l deochi* ("lest I eye him"). Ashkenazi Jews of the same towns said *keyn ayin-hore* ("no evil eye") and tied the same red thread.

**Twins:** in Serbia the rite is called *gašenje ugljevlja*, "quenching the coals". Greek *xematiasma* drops olive oil into the water instead; if the drops spread, the eye is present.

**Age: Medieval / early modern.** A Romanian evil-eye charm invoking Saints Cosmas and Damian was copied into the Codex Sturdzanus between 1609 and 1618, and it ends with the "clean, bright" formula still used today. The belief itself is ancient: Plutarch's *Table Talk* (5.7) already debates how a glance can harm.

### I.2 Turnatul cerii — pouring out the fright

*Ukraine, Belarus, Romania, Hungary. For fright (Romanian sperietură, Ukrainian pereliak): a child who stammers, startles or wets the bed after a dog attack or a fall.*

**You need:** beeswax (stubs of church candles), a small pot set in hot water, a bowl of unbegun water, a cloth.

1. Seat the sufferer facing east and cover the head with the cloth.
2. Hold the bowl over the head and pour in the melted wax.
3. Read the shape that sets: a dog, a man, a snake, fire. That is the shape of the fear.
4. Repeat at dawn on three days, until the wax sets smooth. Bury the wax or give it to running water.

**The words.** East Slavic healers say, in many versions, *Ne ya vylyvayu, Maty Bozha vylyvaye*: "It is not I who pour it out, it is the Mother of God." A Bukovina charm for fright, recorded in Vicov de Sus, orders the terror away from the child and "to the Black Sea" instead. The charmer threatens to sweep it out with a broom, rake it with a rake and throw it into the sea, then spits three times.

**Safety:** Hungarian *ólomöntés* and some Romanian charmers poured molten lead. Lead fumes are toxic and molten metal burns badly: use beeswax, melted in a water bath.

**Age: Folk-recorded, widespread.** Known from Finland to the Balkans, but written down mostly from the 19th century. Anything older is a guess.

### I.3 The child who will not sleep — Muma Pădurii and the Dawn-maidens

*Romania, Russia, Ukraine. For a baby who cries all night.*

In Romania, *Muma Pădurii* (the Forest Mother) steals a child's sleep and leaves her own wailing in its place. In Russia, the culprits are the *kriksy*, the night-criers. The cure in both is a trade: give back the crying, ask for the sleep.

1. At dusk or first light, carry the child to the threshold or an open window.
2. Face the forest (Romania) or the dawn (Russia) and say the words three times.
3. Spit lightly three times over the left shoulder and go in without looking back.

**The words** (composite):

> *Muma Pădurii, na-ți nesomnul, dă-ne somnul.*\
Forest Mother, here is your sleeplessness; give us back the sleep.

> *Zarya Mar'ya, zarya Marina, voz'mi bessonnitsu, otday sonnitsu.*\
Dawn Maria, Dawn Marina, take the sleeplessness, give back the sleep.

**Age: Folk-recorded.** Russian charms to the Dawn sisters appear in L. N. Maikov's 1869 collection. The personified Dawns (*Zorile*) are also sung to at funerals in south-western Romania. A link to an Indo-European dawn goddess is often claimed; it is plausible and unprovable.

### I.4 Bone to bone — the sprain charm

*Romania, Russia, Belarus; the same words in German, Gaelic and Sanskrit. For sprains and wrenched joints.*

1. Warm a little oil or goose fat and rub it into the joint, always toward the heart.
2. Russian and Belarusian healers then "sew" the sprain: they stitch a needle and thread through a cloth laid over it, without touching the skin. The patient asks "What are you sewing?" and the healer answers "Torn flesh and sinew I sew."
3. Bind the joint and repeat on three mornings.

**The words.** A Romanian charm has the Mother of God rebuild the sufferer piece by piece: *păr la păr, inimă la inimă, sânge la sânge și os la os* ("hair to hair, heart to heart, blood to blood and bone to bone"). Russian charms say *kost' k kosti, zhila k zhile*: "bone to bone, sinew to sinew."

**Safety:** if you cannot bear weight on it, it may be broken. Get an X-ray.

**Age: Ancient root.** This is the most famous charm formula in Europe. The Second Merseburg Charm (10th-century manuscript) ends *ben zi bena, bluot zi bluoda, lid zi geliden*: "bone to bone, blood to blood, limb to limb." The Sanskrit *Atharvaveda* (4.12) joins "marrow to marrow, joint to joint", and Carmichael recorded the same words in a Gaelic sprain charm. Scholars still argue whether this is shared Indo-European inheritance or a medieval Christian charm that travelled.

### I.5 The fever-sisters — St Sisinnius and the twelve shakers

*Russia, Bulgaria, Serbia, Romania. For fevers and chills.*

Saint Sisinnius (in other versions the Archangel Michael) sees twelve wild-haired women rise out of the sea: the Fevers, daughters of King Herod. He seizes them and beats them until they confess their names and swear that wherever their names are read, they will not come. The Russian list Afanasyev gives (names vary by manuscript): Tryaseya the Shaker, Ogneya the Burner, Ledeya the Freezer, Gneteya the Crusher, Grynusha the Chest-presser, Glukheya the Deafener, Lomeya the Breaker, Pukhneya the Swelling, Zhelteya the Yellowing, Korkusha the Cramper, Glyadeya the Watcher, Neveya the Deadly.

1. Write the legend and all twelve names on paper.
2. Read it over the sick person three times at dawn, or fold it and hang it round the neck.
3. When the fever breaks, burn the paper.

**Age: Medieval, with a late-antique root.** The Slavonic Sisinnius prayer was translated from Greek no later than the 12th century. The 11th-century Glagolitic *Euchologium Sinaiticum* already holds a cycle of prayers against fever, and Slavonic lists of forbidden books condemned such "false prayers". The idea that naming a demon disarms it is older still: the *Testament of Solomon* (early centuries CE) has Solomon interrogate demons for their names.

### I.6 Avestița — the childbed amulet

*Romania, with Greek, Slavic and Jewish twins. To guard a mother and newborn in the forty days after birth.*

Coming down from the Mount of Olives, Saint Sisoe sees the Archangel Michael seize Avestița, "the Wing of Satan", a demon sent to stop the birth of Christ. She harms women in childbed and infants. Michael forces her to reveal her tricks and her names, and sends her back to the depths of hell until the Last Judgment. Wherever her names are written, she cannot enter.

**Her names.** Usually nineteen. The manuscripts include Avezuha, Navadariia, Samca, Solomiia, Viștița, Scormela, Huluba, Grapa, Muha, Puha and Zlaia.

1. Copy the legend and all nineteen names onto paper (a *zapis*). Some copies add a drawing of the demon, which keeps her off too.
2. Fold it and hang it at the mother's bed, or sew it into the baby's swaddling.
3. Keep it in place through the forty days after the birth.

**The words** (composite): *Unde vor fi scrise numele mele, acolo eu nu voi intra.* "Where my names are written, there I shall not enter."

**Age: Ancient root, and the best-documented chain in this book.** In "Two Thousand Years of a Charm against the Child-Stealing Witch" (*Folk-Lore*, 1900), Moses Gaster traced the Romanian text back through Greek charms of St Sisinnios against the demon Gylou, the child-stealing Gello of classical Greece. Jewish amulets against Lilith, naming three guardian angels, are the same story; Ashkenazi families across Eastern Europe hung a *kimpet-tsetl* (childbed note) for the same purpose. A 6th-century painting at the Coptic monastery of Bawit in Egypt shows Sisinnios on horseback spearing a female demon. In Romanian, the Sisoe legend is in the Codex Sturdzanus (1590–1602); the oldest dated Avestița copy is from 1752.

## Book II — Love, fate and the destined one

Most old love magic was worked by young women on a handful of nights, and most of it is divination: finding out who the destined one (*ursitul*) is, not forcing anyone. Binding magic lives in Book V.

### II.1 The Lady of the Forest — gathering the mătrăgună

*Romania. For love, for being noticed at the village dance, for luck.*

The Romanian *mătrăgună* is deadly nightshade (*Atropa belladonna*). Villagers called it *Doamna Codrului* ("Lady of the Forest") or *Împărăteasa Buruienilor* ("Empress of the Herbs") and approached it like a powerful woman: with gifts.

**Safety: deadly poisonous.** Every part of belladonna is toxic, and its sweet black berries can kill a child. Wash your hands after touching it. Never put it in food or drink, and never give it to anyone.

1. Go to the plant at night or before dawn, in secret, hair unbound, naked or in an undershirt. Speak to no one on the way.
2. Lay gifts at its foot: bread, salt, sugar or honey, a coin, a little brandy.
3. Tell it aloud what you want, then dig a piece of root without breaking it.
4. Go home by another road without looking back. The root is carried in the bosom or the belt, or soaked in bathwater before a dance. It is never eaten.

**The words.** The couplet *Mătrăgună, Doamnă bună, / mărită-mă într-o lună* ("Mătrăgună, good Lady, marry me off within a month") is widely repeated. I could not trace it to a dated collection, so treat it as folk-sounding rather than documented.

**Age: Ancient root.** Around 300 BCE, Theophrastus (*Enquiry into Plants* 9.8.8) recorded how Greek root-cutters took the mandrake. They drew three circles round it with a sword, cut it facing west, then danced round it and said "as many things as possible about the mysteries of love" (Hort's 1916 translation). True mandrake does not grow wild in the Carpathians; its cousin belladonna inherited the role. Continuity is plausible, not proven.

### II.2 Three nights to see the destined one

*Romania, with Russian and Serbian twins. St Andrew's Eve (29 November), New Year's Eve, Epiphany Eve (5 January).*

- **St Andrew's Eve:** sleep with a sprig of basil under the pillow, and the face you dream of is your *ursit*. Or eat a very salty cake (*turtă de sare*) and drink nothing: whoever brings you water in the dream is the one. Sow wheat in a pot that night; if it stands thick and green by New Year, so will your luck.
- **New Year's Eve:** in the dark, go to the fence and count nine stakes, then tie a red thread on the ninth. Look at it in daylight. Straight and smooth means a young, handsome husband; crooked means an old one; bark still on means a rich one.
- **Epiphany Eve:** when the priest blesses the house, take a sprig from his bunch of basil and sleep on it.
- **The mirror (Russian *svyatki*, Christmastide):** at midnight, set two mirrors facing each other with a candle between. The destined face appears far down the corridor of reflections. Folk warning: cover the mirror the moment a face appears, because it may not be human.

**Age: Folk-recorded, widespread; Medieval / early modern in Russia.** Simion Florea Marian documented the Romanian forms in *Sărbătorile la români* (1898–1901). In 1551 the Moscow Stoglav council attacked the *rusal'i* revels held on Christmas Eve and Epiphany Eve, as well as at midsummer.

### II.3 Midsummer — wreaths, dew and the fern flower

*Romania (Sânziene), Bulgaria (Enyovden), the East Slavs (Kupala). The night of 23–24 June.*

On the shortest night the fairy Sânziene fly, herbs reach full strength, and treasure shows itself with ghostly fires.

- **Romania:** weave a wreath of yellow bedstraw (*Galium verum*, itself called *sânziene*) and throw it onto the roof. If it stays up, you marry within the year. Hang another on the gate for protection, and wash your face in the dawn dew for beauty.
- **Bulgaria:** before sunrise, gather "77 and a half herbs" for the 77 and a half illnesses. The half is a stalk picked with your eyes closed, for the illness no herb cures. Girls name bunches of flowers for marriage divination (*Enyova bulya*), and fires are lit to strengthen the "heavenly fire".
- **East Slavs:** float wreaths with candles on the river; one that sails far means a wedding, one that sinks means none. Couples jump the bonfire holding hands. At midnight the fern blooms for an instant: its flower shows hidden treasure. Demons guard it, so draw a circle round yourself and do not look back, whatever you hear.

**Age: Medieval / early modern for the Slavic rites.** The Stoglav (1551) condemned the *rusal'i* of St John's Day. The Romanian forms are folk-recorded. Dictionaries derive *Sânziene* from Latin *Sanctus dies Johannis*, St John's Day; the popular derivation from *Sancta Diana* is possible but disputed.

### II.4 Masa Ursitoarelor — laying the table for the Fates

*Romania; Bulgarian orisnitsi, Serbian suđaje, Greek Moires. The third night after a birth.*

On the third night three Fates, the *Ursitoare*, come to the cradle and decree the child's life: its length, its marriage, its death. The family's task is to make them well disposed.

1. Sweep the room, leave the door unlocked and keep the dogs quiet.
2. Set a small table by the cradle: bread or ring-shaped *colaci*, salt, wine, honey or sweets, and a coin. Some add a mirror, a comb and a candle.
3. The household goes to bed early and quietly. The midwife (*moașa*), who laid the table, may stay awake to overhear the decree.
4. In the morning the food goes to the midwife or to the poor.

**Age: Ancient root, with a medieval witness.** The three Fates are classical: the Greek Moirai and the Roman Parcae. Anti-pagan sermons of 11th–13th-century Rus' rebuke Christians for setting meals for "Rod and the Rozhanitsy", most likely birth-and-fate spirits of the same kind.

### II.5 The night-flying lover — Zburătorul and the fiery serpent

*Romania, Russia, Bulgaria. To guard a sleeping girl or young wife.*

The *Zburător* ("Flier") comes down the chimney as a streak of fire and takes the shape of a handsome young man. The women he visits waste away, sleepless and longing. The Russians call him the fiery serpent, *ognennyy zmey*; Bulgarian songs are full of girls loved by a *zmey*.

1. Rub garlic on the window frames and around the chimney mouth at dusk.
2. Smoke the room with incense and put wormwood under the pillow.
3. Lay a knife or sickle across the threshold: iron bars spirits.
4. If he still comes, a charmer banishes him with the usual formula, "where the rooster does not crow".

**Age: Medieval witness.** The 16th-century Russian *Tale of Peter and Fevronia of Murom* opens with a flying serpent visiting a prince's wife in her husband's shape. In Romania the belief was strong enough for Ion Heliade Rădulescu to build his poem *Zburătorul* (1843) on it.

## Book III — House, herd and field

This is communal magic: rites a whole village performed against plague, cattle-death, witches and hail. Several are dated by chronicles and church bans, which makes this the best-attested book of all.

### III.1 The plague shirt — cămașa ciumii

*Romania (Oltenia, Făgăraș), Serbia's Timok valley, Bulgaria. Against plague and cholera; later, to protect soldiers going to war.*

1. Gather a group of old women, often widows: three, seven or nine. Some villages wanted them all to share a name, such as seven Marias near Făgăraș.
2. Between dusk and dawn, they spin, weave, cut and sew one whole shirt. When plague broke out in Serbia in 1837, Prince Miloš Obrenović had nine old women do it naked and in secret, by hearth-light, without a candle.
3. Carry it to the crossroads with food, singing, shouting and banging dishes. Throw the shirt to the wind and leave the food "for the Plague".
4. Alternatively, hold it open on the road and have everyone crawl through it, children first.

**Bulgarian twin:** in eastern Bulgaria, "pure women" wove a cloth (*chumino*) in a single night from the wool of a black ram. At night the household crawled through it naked outside the village, men and women in separate groups. Ethnographer Dimitar Marinov recorded it in the early 20th century.

**Age: Medieval / early modern.** The shirt is first mentioned in the Romanian lands in Anton Maria Del Chiaro's *Istoria delle moderne rivoluzioni della Valachia* (Venice, 1718); Prince Miloš's was made in 1837, and Tudor Pamfile recorded the rite in Oltenia in 1916. It is still alive: men of the Timok valley carried threads from such shirts to the Yugoslav wars in 1991 and to Kosovo in 1999. The Russian twin is the "one-day church" (*obydennaya tserkov'*), raised from fresh logs by a whole town in 24 hours. The Novgorod chronicle records the first in 1390, and Vologda still raised one in October 1654.

### III.2 Ploughing the circle — opakhivanie

*Russia, Ukraine, Belarus; Bulgaria, Serbia. To lock an epidemic out of the village.*

- **The Russian form:** at midnight the village women, often naked or in white shifts with hair unbound, drew a furrow round the whole village. One of them pulled the plough while the rest beat pans and carried icons and brooms. In the accounts, any creature they met was taken for "Cow Death" in disguise.
- **The Bulgarian form:** twin blacksmiths forge the ploughshare in a single night, naked and silent, stopping when the cock crows. Twin brothers yoke twin oxen and plough one or three furrows round the village.

**Age: Medieval, with an 8th-century hint.** Dimitar Marinov recorded the Bulgarian rite in the early 20th century, already rare and only in eastern Bulgaria. A 2012 study cites naked men ploughing round Tarnovo in the Black Death of 1347 and round Lyaskovets in the cholera of 1837. A Frankish list of pagan practices from about 743, the *Indiculus superstitionum*, already condemns "furrows around farmsteads".

### III.3 Living fire — focul viu, zhivoy ogon', nodfyr

*Romania, the Slavic lands, Germanic and Gaelic Europe. Against cattle plague.*

1. Put out every fire in the village.
2. Make new fire by friction only: a dry pole spun against a plank, or a rope sawn round a post. In Serbia a boy and a girl did it, naked, in a dark room. In Bulgaria two naked men rubbed branches together in the forest.
3. Drive the cattle through the smoke or over the embers, and scatter the ashes on the fields.
4. Every household relights its hearth from the new fire.

In the Rhodopes, a tree set alight by lightning counted as living fire: every hearth in the village was put out and relit from it.

**Safety:** treat it like any bushcraft fire: bare ground, water at hand, animals kept at a sane distance.

**Age: Early medieval.** The same *Indiculus* (c. 743) condemns "fire made by rubbing wood, that is, *nodfyr*". The rite ran from Scotland to Russia into the 19th century.

### III.4 St George's Eve — guarding the milk

*Romania (Sângeorz), Bulgaria (Gergyovden), Serbia and North Macedonia (Đurđevdan). The night of 22–23 April.*

**The threat:** before dawn, witches go naked into other people's pastures and drag a towel or rope through the dew. They are gathering the *mană*, the fruitfulness of cows and fields. Next day your cow gives water instead of milk.

1. At dusk on the eve, set green branches (willow, beech, birch) and sods of green turf on the gate, over the doors and on the cowshed.
2. Rub garlic on the cows' horns and udders, and on the doorposts.
3. In Maramureș, sheep going out to pasture are passed over a chain, an axe and a small fire.
4. At dawn, wash your own face in the dew.

**A tale told across the region:** a farmer hiding in his field hears a witch chant "I take all, I take all!" as she drags her towel. He mutters, "And I take half", and next morning his cow fills every bucket in the house. Irish and Scottish May Eve lore tells of witches "skimming the dew" the same way. On this night buried treasure also burns with a bluish flame: throw a garment over the spot and dig there later.

**Age: Folk-recorded, widespread.** The same dew-witch appears from Ireland to Macedonia, on May Eve in the west and St George's Eve in the east. Such a spread suggests medieval roots at least.

### III.5 The house spirit — moving the Domovoi, sparing the house snake

*Russia, Ukraine, Belarus; Romania, Lithuania.*

The *Domovoi* ("house one", or simply "Grandfather") lives behind the stove. Leave him bread and salt, milk or porridge there, and never curse in his hearing.

1. When you move, scoop embers from the old stove into a pot, or invite him into an old bast shoe.
2. Bow at the old threshold and say: *Dedushka domovoy, poydyom s nami v novyy dom*, "Grandfather Domovoi, come with us to the new house."
3. At the new house, let the cat in first, then tip the embers into the new stove.

In Romania the guardian is the house snake (*șarpele casei*), living under the threshold or in the foundation. Never kill it: its death foretells the master's. Leave it a saucer of milk. Lithuanians kept the grass snake (*žaltys*) in the same way.

**Age: Folk-recorded.** Described in detail only from the 18th–19th centuries; most scholars see an ancestor spirit behind him.

### III.6 The shadow in the foundation — the builder's rite

*Romania, Bulgaria, Serbia, Greece, Hungary. To make a building stand.*

In the ballad, Meșterul Manole walls his own wife into the monastery of Curtea de Argeș so that it stops collapsing. Serbia's *Building of Skadar*, Greece's *Bridge of Arta* and Hungary's *Kelemen the Mason* tell the same story, in some 700 local variants.

**The lore:** masons "stole" a passer-by's shadow by measuring it with a reed and walling the reed into the foundation. The victim died within forty days and became the building's unseen guardian, its *stafie*. Passers-by were warned not to let their shadow be taken. Emily Gerard (1888) heard of professional "shadow-traders" who supplied builders with victims.

**The kindly version, still common:** under the cornerstone, put a coin, bread and salt, and a sprig of basil, and pour out a little wine. In many Balkan villages a rooster is killed over the foundation.

**Age: Folk-recorded.** Vasile Alecsandri first printed the Manole ballad in 1852–53. The shadow looks like a softened form of foundation sacrifice, which is found worldwide, but the order of development is guesswork.

### III.7 Driving off the hail — Solomonari, bells and willow

*Romania; the Hungarian garabonciás, the Serbian zduhać. When a hail cloud comes.*

**The lore:** the *Solomonari* are wandering scholars trained at the *Șolomanța*, a school beneath a lake or at the end of the world. They ride dragons through the clouds and bring hail where they are offended. One may come to your door dressed as a ragged beggar: refuse him at your peril.

1. Ring the church bell. Western bells were often cast with the words *fulgura frango*, "I break the lightning".
2. Burn a few of the willow catkins blessed on Florii (Palm Sunday) and kept by the icons, and set the Easter candle in the window.
3. Drive an axe into the yard, blade up, to cut the cloud.
4. Feed the next beggar who knocks.

**Age: Folk-recorded, with a medieval background.** The name links them to Solomon, master of demons in medieval lore, and Moses Gaster wrote the classic study of them (1884). Emily Gerard's 1885 essay "Transylvanian Superstitions" turned their school into the "Scholomance", where the Count of Bram Stoker's *Dracula* (1897) learned his black arts.

## Book IV — Divination

Telling the future was the most everyday magic of all: a woman in Brodina, Suceava, recalled her mother casting beans before deciding whether to set out on a journey. Two of these methods descend from books that the Church banned.

### IV.1 The onion calendar

*Romania, Hungary. New Year's Eve. To forecast the rain of the coming year.*

1. Cut an onion in half and peel off twelve cup-shaped layers.
2. Name each for a month, January to December, and line them up.
3. Put a pinch of salt in each and leave them overnight on the table or windowsill.
4. In the morning, the cups holding water mark the wet months; the dry ones mark drought.

**Age: Folk-recorded.** The idea behind it, that the twelve days of Christmastide foretell the twelve months, is medieval and widespread.

### IV.2 Casting the 41 beans — datul în bobi

*Romania, Moldova, Bosnia (bacanje graha), Russia; the Turkic steppe. For any yes-or-no question: a journey, a match, a lost animal.*

1. Take 41 dried beans or corn kernels. Mix them in a circle on a sieve or a cloth while saying your question.
2. Split them at random into three heaps. From each heap remove four at a time until one to four remain.
3. Lay the remainders in a row of three, then gather the rest and repeat twice more. You now have a three-by-three grid.
4. Read it: Romanian practice reads the number and position of beans on the three lines. Many readers treat the top row as the head (thoughts), the middle as the heart, the bottom as the road.

There was never one fixed key: each bean-reader read in her own way.

**Age: Folk-recorded; origin unknown.** The Kazakh and Kyrgyz *kumalak* uses the same 41 counters, the same removal by fours and the same nine-square grid. That points to an old Eurasian route, probably through Turkic or Persian hands, though no one has traced it.

### IV.3 Sieve and shears — finding a thief

*Pan-European, England to Russia. To name who took something.*

1. Stick the points of an open pair of shears into the rim of a sieve.
2. Two people balance the shears' handles on their fingertips, so the sieve hangs free.
3. Ask: "By Saints Peter and Paul, did (name) take it?" for each suspect.
4. At the guilty name, the sieve turns or falls.

**Age: Ancient root.** Theocritus (3rd century BCE) mentions a country-woman "sieve-diviner" (*koskinomantis*) in his third Idyll, and Artemidorus lists sieve-diviners among the fortune-tellers of the 2nd century CE. Whether they used shears is not recorded.

### IV.4 The thunder book — Gromovnicul

*Romania, Bulgaria, Serbia, Russia. To read the year from its first thunder.*

The *gromovnic* (from Slavonic *gromŭ*, "thunder") is a manuscript book of omens, arranged by the signs of the zodiac. Thunder heard under a given sign, by day or by night, foretells war, famine, disease or the fate of rulers. Many copies add earthquakes, lightning and hail.

1. Note the sign of the zodiac, and whether it is day or night, when you hear thunder.
2. Look up the entry and read the year's fortune.
3. At that first thunder, villagers rubbed their backs against a wall or tree, or touched a stone to the head, so as to be strong all year.

**Age: Medieval, with a classical root.** Romanian gromovnics survive from 1636 (Sărata Oltului). The most widespread version is ascribed to the Byzantine emperor Heraclius ("Iraclie împărat"), probably printed at Govora in 1639. A Romanian index of forbidden books from 1667–1669 lists the genre among heretical books. It continues the Greek *brontologion*. John Lydus (6th century) preserved a Roman thunder calendar by Nigidius Figulus (1st century BCE), who claimed Etruscan sources.

### IV.5 The body's omens — the twitching book

*Romania, Bulgaria, the Slavic lands.*

- Right palm itches: money is coming. Left palm: money is going.
- Right eye twitches: joy, or a dear face. Left eye: tears.
- Ears ringing: someone is speaking of you.
- Hiccups: someone is thinking of you.

**Age: Medieval.** Slavonic *trepetniki* ("twitching books") listed what each tremor of the body foretold, and the Church banned them with the thunder books. Their model is a Greek treatise on twitching ascribed to Melampus, from the Hellenistic period.

### IV.6 Choosing your Babă — the first nine days of March

*Romania, Moldova; Bulgaria's Baba Marta. 1–9 March.*

Baba Dochia drives her sheep up the mountain in March, shedding one of her nine sheepskin coats each warm day, until the frost turns her to stone.

1. Before 1 March, secretly pick one day between the 1st and the 9th: that is "your Babă".
2. Watch its weather. Sunny and mild means a good year for you; rain or sleet means a sad or troubled one.
3. Wear the red-and-white *mărțișor* until you see the first stork or a tree in blossom, then hang it on a branch.

**Age: Medieval root, with a 19th-century myth on top.** The name almost certainly comes from St Eudokia (Evdochia), whose feast falls on 1 March. The romantic story of Dochia as a daughter of the Dacian king Decebal, fleeing the emperor Trajan, was put into verse by Gheorghe Asachi after an 1838 trip to Mount Ceahlău. B. P. Hasdeu already rejected attempts to read "Dochia" as "Dacia". The 1 March customs of Bulgaria, North Macedonia, Moldova and Romania were inscribed together on UNESCO's intangible heritage list in 2017.

### IV.7 Drawing down the moon

*Bulgaria (Strandzha, the Rhodopes), Greece (Epirus). Witch-lore to know rather than a rite to perform.*

On certain nights the *magyosnitsi* (witches), or in some villages "wise men", pull the moon down from the sky. On earth she turns into a cow or a calf. They milk her, and moon-milk cures any illness or makes their own cows yield more. Older Greek versions from Epirus have women in white play music, dance, offer the moon food and invite her down to tell them the future. South Slavic lore even explains the moon's dark spots as stains from her time on earth.

**Age: Ancient root.** Aristophanes' *Clouds* (423 BCE) jokes about hiring a Thessalian witch to pull the moon down, and Plato's *Gorgias* mentions the Thessalian women who draw down the moon. The Frankish *Indiculus* (c. 743) condemns the belief that women "command the moon" and can "take away men's hearts". Few motifs in this book span 2,400 years so clearly, though no one knows how it travelled.

## Book V — Curses, bindings and their undoing

Folk tradition was blunt about curses: they work only when earned. The curse of a wronged mother, orphan or widow "catches"; an undeserved one comes back on the person who cast it. That is why most of this book is about undoing.

### V.1 The art of the blestem — a formulary of curses

*Romania, Bulgaria, Serbia, Russia.*

Romanian curses run on the optative "-ar", "may it…". The heaviest is a wish that the earth refuse the body, so that it never rots and walks as a strigoi. A curse said kneeling, with hair unbound, or before an icon with a candle was thought heavier still. In many villages only the one who laid a curse could lift it, by forgiving aloud.

| Language | Curse | Meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Romanian | *Bate-te-ar Dumnezeu!* | May God strike you |
| Romanian | *Arde-te-ar focul!* | May fire burn you |
| Romanian | *Trăsni-te-ar Sfântul Ilie!* | May St Elijah's thunderbolt strike you |
| Romanian | *Pământul să nu te primească!* | May the earth refuse you (and you walk as a strigoi) |
| Bulgarian | *Gram da te udari!* (Гръм да те удари!) | May thunder strike you |
| Bulgarian | *Da ti izsahne rakata!* (Да ти изсъхне ръката!) | May your hand wither |
| Serbian | *Dabogda ti kuća izgorela!* | God grant your house burns down |
| Russian | *Chtob tebe pusto bylo!* (Чтоб тебе пусто было!) | May everything turn empty for you |

Romanian folk song even has a genre for it, the abandoned girl's curse on the lover who left her.

**Age: Folk-recorded.** The formulas were written down in the 19th century; the habit of formal cursing is as old as writing, as the more than a thousand surviving Greek and Roman curse tablets show.

### V.2 The wedding padlock — legatul

*Romania, the Balkans, the East Slavs; the "ligature" of medieval Western Europe.*

**The binding:** while the couple is led three times round the table in church, an enemy snaps a padlock shut or ties knots in a thread, then throws the key into a well or river. The groom is "bound": impotent, or the marriage barren, until it is undone.

**Guarding a wedding:**

1. Kinswomen watch the guests for anyone fumbling in a pocket during the ceremony.
2. Bride and groom carry garlic or a pin in their clothes.
3. In Russia a trusted wedding-sorcerer or the best man went ahead of the procession to "close" the road against spoiling.

**Undoing (*dezlegare*):** find the lock and open it, or the knots and untie them. Failing that, a charmer ties nine knots in a red thread and unties them one by one, counting down from nine to none, then burns the thread.

**Age: Early medieval.** The Frankish *Indiculus* (c. 743) already condemns "ligatures", though there the word means amulets tied on the body. Hincmar of Reims discussed impotence by sorcery in the 860s divorce case of King Lothar II, and the theme runs through European witchcraft writing for the next eight centuries.

### V.3 Turning back the făcătură

*Romania (făcătură, făcut), Bulgaria (magiya), Russia (porcha). When something has been "done" to you.*

**The signs:** objects where none should be. A knot of hair under the threshold, a bundle of rags, needles, eggshells, grave soil, or (in Russia and Ukraine) a wreath of feathers grown inside a pillow. Then sudden illness, quarrels in the house, dying animals.

1. Never touch the thing with bare hands. Lift it with tongs or two sticks.
2. Burn it in the stove with incense, or give it to running water.
3. Then speak the counter-charm. A Romanian unbinding recorded before 1912 runs: *Cine-a făcut cu unu, / io desfac cu doi; / cine-a făcut cu doi, / io desfac cu trei* ("Who made it with one, I unmake with two; who made it with two, I unmake with three").
4. A common Romanian cleansing follows: at dawn, at a crossroads, wash the afflicted with water from nine wells and nine sprigs of basil. Throw the water backward over the shoulder and go home without looking back.

The Orthodox route is still in use: a priest reads the exorcism prayers of St Basil the Great (*Molitfele Sfântului Vasile*) over the afflicted.

**Age: Ancient root.** Plato (*Laws* 11, 933b) describes Athenians alarmed by wax figures left at their doors, at crossroads or on their parents' graves. The village forms are folk-recorded.

### V.4 Footprints, hair and nails

*Romania, the East Slavs.*

What has touched you stays bound to you. Soil from your footprint (*urma*), dug up and dried in a pot of basil or above the stove, makes you "dry up" with longing and return. The same soil can be used to harm. Russians called it "taking out the track" (*vynut' sled*).

1. Burn or bury your hair combings and nail clippings; never leave them where a bird or a neighbour could take them.
2. Do not leave clothes outside overnight.
3. Keep a lock of a beloved's hair yourself, and you hold the same power.

**Age: Folk-recorded.** James Frazer called this "contagious magic" and found it on every continent; its age is beyond reckoning.

### V.5 The twisted ears — the zalom

*Russia, Ukraine, Belarus, Poland. A curse on a field.*

A sorcerer twists and knots a handful of standing grain, the *zalom* or *zakrutka*, in an enemy's field. Whoever reaps it falls ill, and the harvest spoils.

1. Do not touch it or reap it.
2. A healer or priest pulls it out with an aspen stick (the aspen being deadly to evil) and burns it on the spot with prayers.

**The mirror image:** at the end of harvest, reapers left the last ears standing and tied them into "Volos's beard", an offering. Same gesture, opposite intent.

**Age: Folk-recorded.** The name Volos may preserve the pre-Christian god Volos (Veles), "god of cattle", by whom the Rus' swore in their 10th-century treaties with Byzantium.

## Book VI — Rites of the year

These are the great public rites, performed by groups rather than one charmer. Several are living heritage today, and three are on UNESCO's intangible heritage list.

### VI.1 Paparuda, Peperuda, Dodola — calling the rain

*Romania, Bulgaria, Serbia, Greece. In drought, or on a set day in the third week after Easter.*

1. Dress a young girl in a skirt of green leaves and branches (dwarf elder or walnut), head to foot.
2. With her companions she dances from house to house, singing and clapping.
3. At every gate the housewife throws a bucket of water over her and gives eggs, flour or coins.

**The words.** The Romanian song opens *Paparudă, rudă, / vino de ne udă* ("Paparuda, kinswoman, come and drench us") and asks for rain on gardens, fields and mill-race. Serbian songs to *Dodola* carry the refrain *Oj dodo, oj dodo le*.

**Age: Folk-recorded, widespread.** The same leaf-clad dancer runs from the Danube to the Aegean. Linguists have tied *Dodola* and the Greek *Perperouna* to Perun, the Slavic thunder-god: an attractive theory, still disputed.

### VI.2 Caloianul — burying the rain-doll

*Romania (Muntenia, Dobrogea, Dolj); Bulgaria's German. In drought, or the third week after Easter.*

1. The girls of the village model a small man of clay and dress him in rags, ribbons and flowers, with half a red Easter eggshell for a cap.
2. Lay him in a little coffin and mourn him like a real death, with laments: *Iene, Caloiene…*
3. Bury him in a secret place, at a crossroads or on a riverbank.
4. After three days dig him up, break him and give the pieces to running water. End with a funeral feast, the *pomana caloienilor*.

**Age: Folk-recorded.** Teodor Burada and I. A. Candrea (1928) recorded it in Brăila, Dolj, Ialomița and Buzău; Candrea derived the name from Slavonic *kalĕnŭ*, "of clay". Readings of it as a dying-and-rising vegetation god are Frazerian guesses.

### VI.3 Sorcova and survachka — striking for health at New Year

*Romania (sorcova), Bulgaria (survakane). The morning of 1 January.*

1. Cut a branch from a living tree; the Bulgarians prefer cornel, the first tree to blossom. Romanian children now often use a stick wrapped in paper flowers.
2. Decorate it with coins, popcorn, dried fruit, small ring-breads and coloured thread.
3. Tap each member of the household on the back while chanting the blessing, and receive coins or sweets.

**The words.** Romanian: *Sorcova, vesela, / să trăiți, să înfloriți, / ca un măr, ca un păr, / ca un fir de trandafir* ("Sorcova, merry one: may you live and flower like an apple tree, a pear tree, a stem of rose"). Bulgarian: *Surva, surva godina, vesela godina… zhivo-zdravo do godina, do amina!* ("Surva, surva year, merry year… alive and well till next year, till amen!").

**Age: Ancient root.** Bulgarian ethnologist Angelina Ivanova compared the survachka with the Greek *eiresione*: a decorated olive or laurel bough carried by youths with songs of plenty in classical Athens. The New Year carol itself, *colindă*, takes its name from the Roman *Calendae*, the very feast the Council in Trullo banned in 692.

### VI.4 The Căluș — the healing horse-dancers

*Romania (Olt valley, Oltenia, Muntenia), and the Vlachs of Bulgaria and Serbia. Rusalii week (Pentecost).*

**The belief:** in Rusalii week the *Iele* (also called *Rusaliile*, *Frumoasele*, "the Beautiful Ones", or *Dânsele*, "They") dance in the night air. Whoever sees them, works on their days or sleeps under a tree where they danced is "taken by the Rusalii" (*luat din Rusalii*): crippled, struck dumb or mad.

**The cure:** an odd-numbered band of men, seven, nine or eleven, led by a master (*vataf*) who knows the charms, swears an oath on a flag topped with garlic and wormwood. They dance round the sufferer laid on the ground, with bells on their legs and staves in their hands. A pot is smashed and the sufferer is leapt over until he rises. A masked "mute" (*mutul*) clowns among them.

**For everyone else, during Rusalii week:** carry wormwood (*pelin*) and garlic in your belt or under your pillow. Do not swim, do not work in the fields and do not sleep outdoors.

**Age: Early modern, likely older.** Dimitrie Cantemir described the călușari in his *Descriptio Moldaviae* (written 1716): they slept only under church roofs, believing that anywhere else "the Beautiful Ones" would seize them. The name *Rusalii* comes from the Roman rose-festival of the dead, the *Rosalia*. UNESCO proclaimed the Căluș ritual a masterpiece in 2005 and inscribed it on its Representative List in 2008.

### VI.5 Nestinarstvo — dancing on embers

*Bulgaria (the village of Bulgari, Strandzha); the Greek Anastenaria. Feast of Saints Constantine and Helena: 21 May, or 3–4 June in Bulgari, which keeps the old calendar.*

The icons of the two saints are carried in procession to a holy spring, to the sound of the drum and bagpipe. At dusk the villagers form a silent circle round a bed of glowing embers. The *nestinari*, through whom the saints are believed to speak, enter the circle in trance and dance barefoot on the coals, sometimes prophesying.

**Safety: watch it, do not try it.** The nestinari train for years within the tradition, and fire-walking injures the untrained.

**Age: Folk-recorded.** It was once danced in some thirty Bulgarian and Greek villages and survives in Bulgari, a village of about a hundred people. The Church long disapproved; claims of a Thracian, Dionysian origin are popular and unprovable. UNESCO inscribed it in 2009.

### VI.6 The masked men — kukeri and their kin

*Bulgaria (kukeri, survakari), Romania (the goat and the bear dancers), Serbia, North Macedonia. New Year to the eve of Lent.*

Men in carved wooden masks and sheepskins, hung with huge bronze bells, leap and stamp through the village. The noise and the masks drive out the evil of the old year and wake the fields. In Romania the same work is done by young men dressed as a goat (*capra*) or a bear (*ursul*) who dance from house to house at New Year.

**Age: Ancient root, by way of a ban.** In 692 the Council in Trullo forbade Christians to wear "comic, satyric or tragic masks" at the Kalends. The masks are still here, and UNESCO inscribed the survakari feast of the Pernik region, Surova, in 2015.

### The vampire's paper trail

- **The word:** a Novgorod priest signed himself "Upir Likhyi" in a colophon dated 1047, often called the first written vampire. The colophon survives only in 15th–16th-century copies, and "Upir" may simply be a personal name. Firmer is the anti-pagan *Word of St Gregory* (11th–13th century): it says the Slavs first sacrificed to "upyri and bereginy", later to Rod and the Rozhanitsy, then to Perun.
- **The spade:** at Sozopol, Bulgaria, archaeologists found two men buried about 700 years ago with iron rods driven through the chest (2012). Bulgarian historians count over a hundred such burials and say the practice survived in some villages into the 1900s. At Pień, Poland, a 17th-century young woman was found in 2022 with a sickle across her neck and a padlock on her big toe.
- **The paper:** on 26 January 1732 Austrian military surgeons signed *Visum et Repertum*, their report on the "vampires" of Medveđa in Serbia. It caused a sensation across Europe and made the Serbian word *vampir* international. Emily Gerard's 1885 essay passed the Romanian remedies, stake, garlic and burned heart, to Bram Stoker.
- **The present:** in 2004, in Marotinu de Sus in Dolj County, relatives exhumed a 76-year-old man who had died the previous December. They cut out and burned his heart, and a sick relative drank the ashes in water. Six men were convicted of illegally exhuming a corpse and given six-month sentences that they did not have to serve.

Tudor Pamfile, *Boli și leacuri* (1911) and *Mitologie românească* (1916). Artur Gorovei, *Descântecele românilor* (1931), with more than 2,500 charms. I. A. Candrea, *Iarba fiarelor* (1928) and *Folklorul medical român comparat* (1944). Moses Gaster, *Literatura populară română* (1883) and "Scholomonar" (*Archiv für slavische Philologie*, 1884). Ion Ghinoiu, *Obiceiuri populare de peste an* (1997) and *Panteonul românesc* (2001).
- **Travellers and early witnesses:** Anton Maria Del Chiaro, *Istoria delle moderne rivoluzioni della Valachia* (1718). Dimitrie Cantemir, *Descriptio Moldaviae* (1716). Emily Gerard, "Transylvanian Superstitions" (1885) and *The Land Beyond the Forest* (1888). Agnes Murgoci, "The Vampire in Roumania" (*Folklore*, 1926).
- **Bulgaria and Serbia:** *Sbornik za narodni umotvoreniya* (Sofia, from 1889). Dimitar Marinov, *Narodna vyara i religiozni narodni obichai* (1914). Vuk Karadžić, *Srpski rječnik* (1818, 1852).
- **The East and West Slavs:** L. N. Maikov, *Velikorusskie zaklinaniya* (1869). A. N. Afanasyev, *Poeticheskie vozzreniya slavyan na prirodu* (1865–69). Oskar Kolberg, *Lud* (1857–1890). W. F. Ryan, *The Bathhouse at Midnight: Magic in Russia* (1999), the best single book in English.
- **Comparative:** Éva Pócs, *Fairies and Witches at the Boundary of South-Eastern and Central Europe* (1989) and *Between the Living and the Dead* (1999). Mircea Eliade, *Zalmoxis, the Vanishing God* (1972). Ronald Hutton, *The Triumph of the Moon* (1999) and *The Witch: A History of Fear* (2017). Alexander Carmichael, *Carmina Gadelica* (1900). Jonathan Roper (ed.), *Charms, Charmers and Charming* (2009).
