A warning about modern “ancient” spellbooks

  • Most modern spellcraft is recent. It descends mainly from 19th- and 20th-century revivals, such as the Hermetic Order of the Golden Dawn (founded 1888) and Gerald Gardner’s Wicca (made public in the 1950s). Those drew on Renaissance grimoires, Freemasonry, Kabbalah and the Greek magical papyri, with Celtic festival names added to a largely modern ritual calendar.
  • Genuine Celtic magic barely survives in Celtic words. The main pieces are lead tablets from Roman Gaul, written in Latin letters. The Larzac tablet, found in 1983 and dated about 100 CE, is the longest text in Gaulish: a curse involving a circle of women around one Severa Tertionicna. It still cannot be fully translated.
  • The “Necronomicon” is not Sumerian. The so-called Simon Necronomicon (1977; Avon paperback 1980) presents itself as a Mesopotamian grimoire. It is a modern compilation mixing real Sumerian and Babylonian names with H. P. Lovecraft’s fiction and Aleister Crowley’s magic. It is commonly attributed to Peter Levenda, who denies writing it, and is widely regarded as a hoax. Nothing in it is a translation of a known tablet.
