The Book of Old Magic

Chapter VIII

Curses

Harmful private magic counted as witchcraft, a capital crime under Assyrian law, so the great curses of Mesopotamia that survive are public and legal. Kings, landowners, treaty-makers and librarians called down the gods on whoever broke their word or touched their monuments, and these curses are the most violent texts in the language.

The Curse of Agade

Source
A Sumerian poem known from Ur III and Old Babylonian copies, c. 2000–1700 BCE, about the fall of Agade (Akkad), the capital of Sargon’s dynasty, c. 2200 BCE. Edited by Jerrold Cooper (1983); lines 210–281 in the standard text.
Use
Not a working spell but a lesson: the gods’ curse on a city whose king, Narām-Sîn, attacked Enlil’s temple, the Ekur at Nippur.
Label
Close (the whole curse and its fulfilment, lines 210–281, with 272–280 condensed). Line numbers follow ETCSL.

Note. The poem was written a century or more after the events. Narām-Sîn’s own bricks show him rebuilding the Ekur, not wrecking it; the curse is a theology of disaster, not history.

Hammurabi’s curses

Source
The epilogue of the Laws of Hammurabi, on the basalt stele in the Louvre (Sb 8), c. 1754 BCE. Translated by Robert Harper (1904) and by L. W. King, both now in the public domain.
Use
Against any future ruler who ignores, alters or defaces the laws.
Label
Close (all fourteen curses, in order; a few divine epithets shortened).

Note. In L. W. King’s public-domain translation the last curse reads: “May Bel curse him with the potent curses of his mouth that can not be altered, and may they come upon him forthwith.” Bel, “Lord,” is Enlil.

The boundary-stone curses

Source
Kudurrus, carved stones recording royal land grants in Babylonia, from the Kassite period (14th century BCE) into the 1st millennium BCE, many in the British Museum and the Louvre. L. W. King edited the British Museum stones in 1912 (public domain).
Use
To protect a land grant, and the stone that records it, from anyone who moves, defaces or disputes it.
Label
Composite (typical clauses).

Note. The stones also carry the gods’ symbols in relief (the moon’s crescent, the sun’s disc, Ištar’s eight-pointed star, Marduk’s spade, Nabû’s stylus, a great snake), so the cursing gods stand guard on the stone itself.

Esarhaddon’s treaty curses

Source
The succession oath (adê) that Esarhaddon of Assyria imposed in 672 BCE to secure the throne for his son Ashurbanipal. Copies come from the temple of Nabû at Kalḫu (Nimrud) and from Tell Tayinat in Turkey. Edited by Simo Parpola and Kazuko Watanabe (SAA 2, no. 6, 1988).
Use
Self-curses sworn by vassals and officials, some acted out with objects as they took the oath.
Label
Composite (a selection; in SAA 2, no. 6, the iron earth and bronze sky are lines 528–532 and the wax and clay figures line 608). The complete treaty is in Parpola and Watanabe’s edition and online in ORACC’s State Archives of Assyria Online.

Note. The bronze sky and iron earth reappear in Deuteronomy 28:23: “And thy heaven that is over thy head shall be brass, and the earth that is under thee shall be iron” (King James Version). Many scholars think Deuteronomy borrowed from this treaty; others argue the curse form is older than both.

The librarian’s curse

Source
The standard colophons on tablets of Ashurbanipal’s library at Nineveh, 7th century BCE.
Use
To protect the king’s books from theft and from scribes who would claim them.
Label
Close

A shorter variant: “Whoever erases my name and writes his own, may Nabû, scribe of all things, erase his name.”

Ištar’s threat at the gate

Source
The Descent of Ištar to the Netherworld, Akkadian, known from Middle and Neo-Assyrian copies from Assur and Nineveh. The goddess makes the same threat to Anu in the Epic of Gilgameš, Tablet VI.
Use
Not a spell, but the most frightening words of power in Akkadian: what the goddess says to the gatekeeper of the dead.
