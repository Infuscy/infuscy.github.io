The Book of Old Magic

Chapter IX

Rites of temple and king

The largest rites were performed for gods and kings: one brought a statue to life, the other let a king survive his own death sentence.

In this chapter

  1. Opening the mouth of a god
  2. The substitute king

Opening the mouth of a god

Source
Mīs pî (“washing of the mouth”), ritual tablets from Nineveh (Neo-Assyrian) and Babylon (Late Babylonian) with their Sumerian incantations, 1st millennium BCE; edited by Christopher Walker and Michael Dick (2001). Mouth-washing of statues is already mentioned in the Middle Babylonian period.
Use
To turn a newly made statue into a living god. One text states the problem plainly: without the opening of the mouth, “this statue cannot smell incense, eat food or drink water.”
Label
Summary

Ritual

  1. In the workshop, on a favourable day: set up two holy-water vessels, a red cloth in front of the god and a white cloth to its right, and offerings for Ea and Asalluḫi. Wash the statue’s mouth and recite three times: “From today you go before your father Ea.”
  2. To the river, by torchlight, leading a ram, reciting “As you grew up, as you grew up from the forest.” Seat the god on a reed mat, facing the sunset. Draw water for seven holy-water basins and put in tamarisk, soapwort, date-palm heart, seven palm shoots and reeds.
  3. Under the night sky: offerings for nine great gods (Anu, Enlil, Ea, Sîn, Šamaš, Adad, Marduk, Gula and Ištar) and the stars, toward the north; and, toward the south, for the gods of craftsmen. Recite “Tamarisk, pure wood” and “On the day the god was created,” washing the mouth again and again.
  4. The disowning. The craftsmen’s hands are symbolically cut off with a tamarisk blade, and they swear they did not make the god: the craft gods did.
  5. Into the temple. The god, now able to eat, drink, smell and hear, is led to his throne.

Note. This is the ritual the Hebrew prophets mocked when they described idols as wood and metal “made by human hands” (Isaiah 44, Jeremiah 10). The disowning meets the same worry from the inside: the statue is declared the work of gods, not of human hands.

The substitute king

Source
Letters from the scholars of Esarhaddon of Assyria (680–669 BCE), edited by Simo Parpola, and an earlier Babylonian chronicle.
Use
When an eclipse announced the king’s death, the death was diverted onto someone else.
Label
Summary

Ritual. The scholars worked out which part of the moon or sun was darkened, and so which land and which king the omen struck. If it was their own king, a substitute (šar pūḫi) was dressed in the royal robes, declared king and seated on the throne, sometimes with a substitute queen, for up to 100 days. The real king lived hidden, and the letters call him “the farmer.” Exorcistic rites, including a namburbi, transferred the omen’s evil onto the substitute. At the end the substitute was put to death, given a royal burial, and the real king returned.

Two cases. In Esarhaddon’s reign a young man named Damqi was killed with his queen to protect the crown prince Šamaš-šum-ukīn. Centuries earlier, the chronicle says, King Erra-imittī of Isin set his gardener Enlil-bāni on the throne as substitute; the king then died in his palace sipping hot broth, and the gardener stayed king.
