The Book of Old Magic

Chapter X

Kindred magic of the ancient world

Mesopotamia’s neighbours practised the same logic of likeness and transfer: break the pot, burn the wax, send the animal away. Four of the best-documented rites are given here for comparison.

In this chapter

  1. Smashing the red pots (Egypt)
  2. Overthrowing Apep (Egypt)
  3. The mouse and the ram (the Hittites)
  4. The Aramaic wax curse

Smashing the red pots (Egypt)

Source
Egyptian execration texts, from the 6th Dynasty (24th–22nd century BCE) into the New Kingdom; Middle Kingdom deposits at Saqqara, at Mirgissa in Nubia (more than 175 pots) and at other sites, mostly in cemeteries. The closing list follows J. A. Wilson’s translation in ANET, pp. 328–329; the Berlin bowls were published by Kurt Sethe (1926), the Brussels and Saqqara figurines by Georges Posener (1940).
Use
To destroy enemies of the king and of cosmic order (foreign rulers, Egyptian rebels and evil itself) before they could act.
Label
Summary, with the closing list Close.

Ritual. Write the enemies’ names on red pots or on clay figurines of bound captives. Smash them and bury the pieces. Red stood for danger.

Overthrowing Apep (Egypt)

Source
The Book of Overthrowing Apep in the Papyrus Bremner-Rhind (British Museum EA 10188, columns 22–32), written about 305 BCE for the Theban priest Nesmin and preserving older temple ritual; translated by Raymond Faulkner (1937–1938).
Use
Recited in temples to help the sun god Ra defeat Apep (Apophis), the chaos serpent who attacks his boat every night.
Label
Summary, with key lines Close (abridged).

Ritual. Draw Apep on a new sheet of papyrus in green ink, or model him in wax (some passages ask for red wax), and write his name on him. Spit on him, trample him four times with the left foot, stab him and burn him. A further section lists the names of Apep, so that every one of them can be destroyed.

The mouse and the ram (the Hittites)

Source
Hittite ritual texts from Ḫattuša, 14th–13th century BCE: the ritual of Ambazzi (CTH 391) and the ritual of Uḫḫamuwa of Arzawa against plague (CTH 410).
Use
To load evil onto an animal and send it away, out of a person or out of the country.
Label
Summary

Ritual. In the ritual of Ambazzi, the evil is transferred from the client to a mouse, which is sent off into the wild to carry it away. When plague struck the army, Uḫḫamuwa’s ritual crowned a ram with a wreath of coloured wool and drove it down the road into enemy country, with a prayer to whatever enemy god had sent the plague: accept this ram, make peace, and keep peace with Hatti.

Note. The scapegoat of Leviticus 16, sent into the wilderness bearing the people’s sins, belongs to the same family of rites.

The Aramaic wax curse

Source
The Sefire stele I (KAI 222), in Old Aramaic, mid-8th century BCE, found near Aleppo: a treaty between Bar-Ga’yah of KTK and Mati’el, king of Arpad.
Use
Treaty self-curses, acted out with objects as the oath was sworn.
Label
Close (abridged).

Note. It is the closest western parallel to Esarhaddon’s wax-and-clay curse (Chapter VIII), sworn about eighty years earlier.
