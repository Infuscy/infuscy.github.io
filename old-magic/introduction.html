The Book of Old Magic

Before you open this book

Every spell, ritual and curse in this book comes from a real ancient text, most often a clay tablet, written between about 2600 and 100 BCE. Nothing has been invented. The English is a fresh rendering made for this book, and where the clay is broken the gap is marked, not filled.

Why most of this magic is defensive

Magic in Mesopotamia was a profession. The exorcist (āšipu, also mašmaššu) trained for years and served temples, kings and households, working alongside the physician (asû). His craft was legitimate; witchcraft (kišpū) was a crime.

Hammurabi’s laws (c. 1754 BCE, §2) send a man accused of sorcery into the river, which judges him. The Middle Assyrian Laws (tablet A, §47) order death for anyone caught practising witchcraft.

So almost everything that survives is counter-magic: protection, healing, exorcism and the undoing of curses. The witch appears only as the enemy. The aggressive curses that survive are public ones, on monuments, boundary stones, treaties and the last lines of library tablets.

Where the tablets come from

Period Dates (BCE) What survives from it
Early Dynastic c. 2600–2350 The oldest known incantations, from Fara (Šuruppak) and Ebla: short, archaic, only partly understood
Old Akkadian c. 2350–2150 The Kish love charm, the oldest love spell known
Old Babylonian c. 2000–1600 Short, vivid spells against dogs, scorpions, Lamaštu and crying babies; Hammurabi’s curses
Middle Babylonian and Middle Assyrian c. 1600–1000 Boundary-stone curses; the great incantation series take their standard form
Neo-Assyrian c. 900–612 Most surviving copies of the great series, from Ashurbanipal’s library at Nineveh, the exorcists’ house at Assur and Sultantepe; Esarhaddon’s treaty curses
Neo- and Late Babylonian c. 626 to the 1st century Copies from Babylon, Sippar and Uruk, where the last families of exorcists worked

The exorcist’s own reading list

Most of the genres in this book appear together in one ancient list, the so-called Exorcist’s Manual (KAR 44 = VAT 8275, Berlin). It was copied by Kiṣir-Nabû in the “House of the Exorcist” at Assur in the 7th century BCE, and it presents itself as the opening lines of the compositions of exorcism, “fixed for study and reading.” Among dozens of titles it names Udug-hul and Sag-gig (Chapter I), Maqlû and Šurpu (Chapters II–III), the namburbi rites (Chapter III), the mouth-washing of statues (Chapter IX), and the court rites (egalkurrû) and potency texts (ŠÀ.ZI.GA) of Chapter VI. Markham Geller (2018) reads it as two curricula: a core to be mastered first, then specialist texts.

How to read an entry

  • Source: the series or tablet, its period and language, where it was found.
  • Use: what the ancient client wanted.
  • Ritual: what the tablet tells the exorcist to do, where that survives.
  • Words: the incantation itself.
  • Akkadian or Sumerian: the original, set under the English, where it has been checked against an edition.
  • Note: context, parallels and what is uncertain.

In the words, […] marks clay broken away, (words) are added for sense, (?) marks an uncertain reading and italics give the original Akkadian or Sumerian. “N, son of N” stands where the tablet leaves a blank for the client’s name (annanna mār annanna).

Each entry carries one label:

  • Close follows one well-preserved text line by line. If it is abridged, the label says so.
  • Composite every line is ancient, but the selection and order are this book’s.
  • Summary a long rite retold in prose, not translated line by line.

Using it today

Where a rite needs only harmless things (an onion, clay, wool, flour, water, a lamp), the entry says how it can be done now. Where it called for something no one should use, the entry says so and stops there. Whether you read these as rites, literature or history is up to you; the book records what was done and believed.

On this edition

This edition is unexpurgated. The love and potency texts of Chapter VI are translated as they stand, sexual vocabulary included, and every ritual recipe is given in full wherever the tablet preserves it, including the ghost-raising recipe in Chapter IV. A few entries are still abridged, because the full text is very long or could not be checked line by line against an edition: Maqlû III, the last incantation of Maqlû VIII and the oven passage of Maqlû II, the onion rite, the bad-dream clod, the Cow of Sîn, the second lullaby, the treaty curses of Esarhaddon, the Book of Overthrowing Apep and the Sefire curses. Their labels say so, and Appendix C lists the editions that give them whole.

Line references. “I 1–36” means Tablet I, lines 1–36 of a series; obv. and rev. are the front and back of a tablet; a prime (15′) counts lines from a break, not from the top. In long translations, line numbers in italics open the line they refer to.

Transliteration. Akkadian is normalized, in italics, with long vowels marked (alsīkunūši ilū mušīti, “I call upon you, gods of the night”). Sumerian words are hyphenated sign by sign (nam-búr-bi), with determinatives raised (d before a god’s name, ki after a place); logograms and sign names are in capitals and upright (ŠÀ.ZI.GA, KA.INIM.MA), and so are the marks that open and close a spell (ÉN, TU₆ ÉN). In the originals, square brackets enclose signs restored where the clay is broken, round brackets signs that only some copies write, and ‹ › a word the scribe left out.

The originals. Fourteen entries give the original under the English, and the formulas in “The grammar of a spell” give theirs. The blocks added in this revision name the edition they follow: Tzvi Abusch’s Maqlû and the Descent of Ištar in the electronic Babylonian Library (eBL), the Electronic Text Corpus of Sumerian Literature (ETCSL) for the Curse of Agade, ORACC for Esarhaddon’s treaty and the library colophons, SEAL and SOAS’s BAPLAR archive for the dog and toothache spells, and eBL’s transliterations of single Nineveh tablets for the formulas. The other entries are still translation only: their editions (Geller’s Udug-hul, Farber’s Lamaštu, Reiner’s Šurpu, Biggs’s ŠÀ.ZI.GA and others) have not yet been checked line by line for the originals, and the Egyptian, Hittite and Aramaic texts of Chapter X are given in English only.

Translations. The English is this book’s own unless another translation is named. Longer word-for-word quotations come only from public-domain translations: R. Campbell Thompson (1903–04), L. W. King and the King James Version. Short phrases quoted from modern editions are marked as quotations.
