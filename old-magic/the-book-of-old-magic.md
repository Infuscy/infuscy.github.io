# The Book of Old Magic

*Incantations, rituals and curses from Sumer, Babylon and Assyria, c. 2600–100 BCE*

Nov 21, 2026 · @Florin

## Before you open this book

*"Let the one who knows show it to the one who knows. The one who does not know must not see it. It is forbidden by the great gods."* — the standard warning written at the end of secret Babylonian and Assyrian ritual tablets

Every spell, ritual and curse in this book comes from a real ancient text, most often a clay tablet, written between about 2600 and 100 BCE. Nothing has been invented. The English is a fresh rendering made for this book, and where the clay is broken the gap is marked, not filled.

### Why most of this magic is defensive

Magic in Mesopotamia was a profession. The exorcist (*āšipu*, also *mašmaššu*) trained for years and served temples, kings and households, working alongside the physician (*asû*). His craft was legitimate; witchcraft (*kišpū*) was a crime.

Hammurabi's laws (c. 1754 BCE, §2) send a man accused of sorcery into the river, which judges him. The Middle Assyrian Laws (tablet A, §47) order death for anyone caught practising witchcraft.

So almost everything that survives is counter-magic: protection, healing, exorcism and the undoing of curses. The witch appears only as the enemy. The aggressive curses that survive are public ones, on monuments, boundary stones, treaties and the last lines of library tablets.

### The exorcist's own reading list

Most of the genres in this book appear together in one ancient list, the so-called *Exorcist's Manual* (KAR 44 = VAT 8275, Berlin). It was copied by Kiṣir-Nabû in the "House of the Exorcist" at Assur in the 7th century BCE, and it presents itself as the opening lines of the compositions of exorcism, "fixed for study and reading." Among dozens of titles it names *Udug-hul* and *Sag-gig* (Chapter I), *Maqlû* and *Šurpu* (Chapters II–III), the namburbi rites (Chapter III), the mouth-washing of statues (Chapter IX), and the court rites (*egalkurrû*) and potency texts (ŠÀ.ZI.GA) of Chapter VI. Markham Geller (2018) reads it as two curricula: a core to be mastered first, then specialist texts.

### How to read an entry

- **Source:** the series or tablet, its period and language, where it was found.
- **Use:** what the ancient client wanted.
- **Ritual:** what the tablet tells the exorcist to do, where that survives.
- **Words:** the incantation itself.
- **Note:** context, parallels and what is uncertain.

In the words, \[…\] marks clay broken away, (words) are added for sense, (?) marks an uncertain reading and *italics* give the original Akkadian or Sumerian. "N, son of N" stands where the tablet leaves a blank for the client's name (*annanna mār annanna*).

Each entry carries one label:

- **Close:** follows one well-preserved text line by line. If it is abridged, the label says so.
- **Composite:** every line is ancient, but the selection and order are this book's.
- **Summary:** a long rite retold in prose, not translated line by line.

### Using it today

Where a rite needs only harmless things (an onion, clay, wool, flour, water, a lamp), the entry says how it can be done now. Where it called for something no one should use, the entry says so and stops there. Whether you read these as rites, literature or history is up to you; the book records what was done and believed.

## The grammar of a spell

Nearly every incantation in this book is assembled from the same seven parts. Learn them once and every spell that follows becomes readable, even where the clay is broken.

1. **The opening mark.** Sumerian spells begin with the sign ÉN, often expanded to *én é-nu-ru*; Akkadian ones with *šiptu*, "incantation." It announces that what follows is not ordinary speech.
2. **The credentials.** The exorcist never speaks for himself. "I am the man of Ea, I am the man of Damgalnunna, I am the messenger of Asalluḫi," he says in the great demon series. The words, and therefore their power, belong to the gods.
3. **The naming of the evil.** The demon, disease or witch is described: where it came from, what it looks like, what it did, where it hides. What can be named exactly can be commanded.
4. **The conversation of the gods.** In hundreds of spells the young god Asalluḫi (later identified with Marduk) sees the sufferer, goes to his father Enki (Ea) in Eridu and reports the case. Ea answers: "My son, what do you not know? What could I add for you? What I know, you know." Then he gives the cure, which is exactly the ritual the exorcist is about to perform.
5. **The likeness and the oath.** An action on an object stands for the cure: "As this onion is peeled and burned, so may the curse be peeled away." Then the evil is put under oath: Sumerian *zi an-na hé-pà, zi ki-a hé-pà*, "By heaven be adjured! By earth be adjured!"
6. **The disclaimer.** Many Akkadian spells close: "The incantation is not mine. It is the incantation of Ea and Asalluḫi, of Damu and Gula, of Ningirima, lady of incantations." Sumerian ones end with the mark TU₆.ÉN.
7. **The label and the rite.** Under a ruled line the tablet names the spell (KA.INIM.MA, "wording for …") and then gives its ritual (KÌD.KÌD.BI, "its procedure"): what to make, where, when, and how many times to recite.

### The tools of the craft

| Tool | What it is | What it does |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Holy-water basin (*egubbû*) | Water drawn from river or well, with tamarisk, soapwort and other plants | Sprinkles and purifies people, rooms and objects |
| Censer and torch | A brazier of juniper, and a burning reed torch | Smoke and fire pass around the patient; Girra the fire god consumes the evil |
| Flour circle (*zisurrû*) | A line of flour drawn on the ground | A boundary around a bed, a patient or a doorway that evil may not cross |
| Figurine (*ṣalmu*) | Clay, dough, tallow, wax, bitumen or wood | A body for the witch, demon, ghost or even the patient, which can then be burned, drowned, buried or sent away |
| Cord and knots (*kiṣru*) | Wool or goat hair, often spun by a young girl | Binds the evil; untying undoes a witch's knots |
| Substitute (*pūḫu*) | A kid, piglet or figurine | Takes the patient's place and carries the evil away |
| Amulet | Stones, a leather bag, a pierced clay or bronze plaque | Worn at the neck, wrist or bed head as standing protection |

### Times, places and numbers

- **Times:** the night, under the stars, for appeals to the cosmic judges; dawn, as Šamaš the sun rises, for verdicts; the end of the month, when the moon is hidden and ghosts walk.
- **Places:** the riverbank (the river carries evil down to the Apsû, the deep), the empty steppe, the roof, the crossroads, the threshold.
- **Numbers:** three recitations, seven knots, "seven and seven."
- **Endings:** the client goes home by a different road and does not look back.

## Chapter I · Against demons

Demons in Mesopotamia were not fallen angels. They were agents with names and shapes, often acting with the gods' permission, and they could be ordered away only by someone who knew exactly what they were.

### The Seven

**Source.** *Udug-hul* (Akkadian *Utukkū lemnūtu*, "Evil Demons"), the great bilingual Sumerian–Akkadian demon series. Its spells go back to Old Babylonian Sumerian originals; most surviving copies are Neo-Assyrian, from Nineveh, 7th century BCE.

**Use.** Against the Sebettu, the "Seven": storm-demons, called messengers of Anu, who strike people and cattle and in one myth darkened the moon.

**Label.** Composite (three passages of the series).

**Words.**

Raging storms, evil gods are they,\
merciless demons, created in the vault of heaven.\
Workers of evil are they;\
they lift their heads to evil, every day to evil,\
to work destruction.

Of these seven, the first is the south wind.\
The second is a dragon whose gaping mouth no one can measure.\
The third is a grim leopard that carries off the young.\
The fourth is a terrible šibbu-serpent.\
The fifth is a furious wolf (?) that does not know how to turn back.\
The sixth is a rampant \[…\] that marches against god and king.\
The seventh is a storm, an evil wind that takes revenge.

Seven are they, seven are they!\
In the depths of the sea, seven are they;\
stretched out in heaven, seven are they.\
They are neither male nor female.\
They are the whirling wind.\
They take no wife; they father no son.\
They know no mercy and no pity.\
They do not listen to prayer or plea.\
They are horses bred in the mountains.\
They are the throne-bearers of the gods.\
They stand in the road and foul the highway.\
Evil are they, evil are they!\
Seven are they, seven are they, twice seven are they!\
By heaven be adjured! By earth be adjured!

They are the messengers of King Anu;\
from city to city they bring darkness.\
Seven gods of the wide heavens, seven gods of the broad earth,\
seven robber-gods are they.

**Note.** The same Seven were also worshipped as protective gods and identified with the Pleiades; in the *Erra Epic* they are the war god's weapons. Reginald Campbell Thompson's 1903 translation of the series, now in the public domain, is still readable.

**The myth.** The series also tells how the Seven stormed heaven and darkened the moon god Sîn: an eclipse. Enlil sent his vizier Nusku down to Ea in the Apsû, and Ea sent his son Marduk against them. Inside an incantation the story works as a precedent: the god who once drove the Seven from the sky will drive them from the body.

### Lamaštu, daughter of Anu

**Source.** The *Lamaštu* series: three tablets of incantations and rituals in its 1st-millennium form (edited by Walter Farber, 2014), with Old Babylonian forerunners in Akkadian and Sumerian from about 1800 BCE. More than sixty amulets against her survive.

**Use.** To protect pregnant women, mothers and newborns from the demoness who kills them.

**Label.** Composite (the portrait); Close (the seven names); Summary, with line references to Farber's edition (the rites).

**Words: her portrait.**

She is savage, she is furious, she is divine, she is blinding (?), she is a she-wolf: the daughter of Anu.\
Her hand is a snare; her embrace is death.\
She touches the bellies of women in labour; she pulls the child from the pregnant woman.\
Her head is a lion's head, her body a donkey's.\
She roars like a lion and howls like a demon-dog.

**Words: her seven names.**

"Daughter of Anu" is her first name.\
The second: "Sister of the gods of the streets."\
The third: "Sword that splits the skull."\
The fourth: "She who kindles fire."\
The fifth: "Goddess whose face is fierce."\
The sixth: "Entrusted to the hand, taken in by Irnina."\
The seventh: "By the great gods be you adjured! Fly away with the birds of the sky!"

The seventh name is itself the oath that banishes her: to say her full name is to send her away.

**Ritual.** Lamaštu was treated as a dangerous guest to be fed, bound and sped on her way. The canonical series prescribes a seven-day rite that begins on the evening of the first day (Lam. III 110). Line references are to Farber's edition; the sequence follows Barkowsky's reconstruction.

1. **Days 1–3** (Lam. I 23–31; III 110–118). Make a clay figurine of Lamaštu as a prisoner, holding a black dog. Set it at the head of the baby's bed for three days and recite the incantation over it morning, noon and evening. Put a piglet's heart in her mouth, in place of the baby's heart. Then bury the figurine in a corner of an outside wall.
2. **Day 4** (Lam. I 47–57; III 119–129). Make a second figurine with a clay donkey and provisions for the journey, and take it out to the steppe. Make her swear by heaven, by earth and by the Anunnaki; hold her with a rope inside a circle of flour, and leave her there for Šamaš. Next morning, turn her to face the sun.
3. **Days 5–7** (Lam. I 94–99; III 130–135). Keep a third figurine at the baby's head for three days, then stab it with a dagger and bury it in a corner of the outer wall.

Other rites in the series carry her out of the house in a red leather bag (Lam. I 155–166), put her in a boat with two white and two black dogs and send it out to the open sea (Lam. II 41–48), and set dog figurines at the doors and windows (Lam. II 61–83; III 8–28). A rite connected with the death of a baby (Lam. I 220–232) repeats the pattern: a clay donkey, provisions and a piglet's heart, recitation three times a day for three days, and the figurine left in the steppe facing the rising sun, inside a flour circle and tied to a bush.

The amulets show the same send-off: a lion-headed demoness kneeling on a donkey in a boat, nursing a dog and a pig, with a comb, a spindle and a pin beside her, while the head of Pazuzu looks over the top. The comb, spindle and pin belong to the amulets; the ritual instructions do not mention them.

**Today.** The farewell can be acted out with a small clay figure, a clay donkey and a little food for the road, carried out of the house and left outside.

### Pazuzu's boast

**Source.** The inscription on the back of the wings of a bronze statuette in the Louvre (MNB 467), Assyria, 8th–7th century BCE. The same self-introduction appears in bilingual incantations.

**Use.** Worn as a head pendant or hung over a bed, above all against Lamaštu.

**Label.** Close.

**Words.**

I am Pazuzu, son of Ḫanbu, king of the evil *lilû*, the wind-demons.\
I climbed the mighty mountains, and they shook.\
The winds I went against were heading west.\
One by one I broke their wings.

**Note.** Pazuzu was himself a savage wind-demon, with a snarling face, wings, talons and a scorpion's tail; his savagery is what made him the best guard against the others. This statuette was the model for the demon of the 1973 film *The Exorcist*. In Assyria he protected mothers and newborns.

### The headache cord

**Source.** *Sag-gig* (Akkadian *muruṣ qaqqadi*, "head sickness"), a nine-tablet bilingual series against the demon of head disease, known from Neo-Assyrian copies; the cuneiform is copied in CT 17, pls. 4–8 and 25–26. First translated by R. Campbell Thompson, *The Devils and Evil Spirits of Babylonia*, vol. 2 (1904), pp. 44–97.

**Use.** Against headache and the fevers that came with it.

**Label.** Summary.

**Words (gist).** Headache roams the steppe like the wind and flashes like lightning; it cuts down the man who does not fear his god like a reed. Asalluḫi reports the case to Ea, and Ea gives the cure.

**Ritual.** Take hair from a young she-goat that has never mated and have a girl spin it into a cord. Tie seven knots and seven knots again, reciting the spell of Eridu over each. Bind the cord around the sufferer's head, neck and limbs, and sprinkle him with the water of the incantation. Morris Jastrow (1915) describes these knotted kid-hair threads tied to the head, neck, hands or limbs of the sick.

**A second rite: the kid.** Another incantation of the series is recited over a kid, a substitute (*pūḫu*) that takes the patient's place: the evil passes into the animal.

**Note.** The exorcist's spells were used alongside the physician's drugs. A medical tradition credits the Kassite-period physician Rabâ-ša-Marduk with "eighteen prescriptions for headache."

**Today.** The knotted cord is the easiest rite in this book to make: undyed wool or goat hair, fourteen knots, one recitation per knot.

### The everyday ward

**Source.** The closing wish of countless Akkadian incantations, from *Udug-hul* onward.

**Label.** Close.

**Words.**

May the evil demon stand aside!\
May the good spirit and the good guardian be ever at my side!

## Chapter II · Against witchcraft

The longest anti-witchcraft rite of the ancient world is *Maqlû*, "Burning": eight tablets holding nearly a hundred incantations, plus a ritual tablet, performed in one night and the following morning at the end of the month Abu (July–August), when spirits passed between the netherworld and the living. Its mature form probably dates to the early 1st millennium BCE; it grew from a short morning rite of about ten spells.

### What the witch was thought to do

The anti-witchcraft texts list her methods in the victim's own voice. She gathered the dust of his feet from a threshold, took his spittle, tore hair from his head and body, and cut the fringe from his garment at the fuller's. She made figurines of him and buried them in the "dark house" (a grave), tied knots of evil against him and slipped spells into his food and drink. *Maqlû* answers each act in kind.

### I call upon you, gods of the night

**Source.** *Maqlû* Tablet I, lines 1–36, the opening incantation of the night.

**Use.** To bring the case against the witch before the stars, the night and the gods of the netherworld, as before a court.

**Label.** Close (complete).

**Words.**

I call upon you, gods of the night.\
With you I call upon the Night, the veiled bride.\
I call upon Dusk, Midnight and Dawn.\
For a witch has bewitched me,\
a lying woman has accused me.\
She has turned my god and my goddess away from me,\
and I have become sickening to all who see me.\
I cannot rest by day or by night.\
A gag keeps filling my mouth;\
she has kept bread from my mouth\
and cut short the water I drink.\
My song of joy has become wailing, my gladness mourning.

Stand by me, great gods, hear my case!\
Judge my case, learn my conduct!\
I have made figures of my sorcerer and my sorceress,\
of the one who bewitches me and the one who has me bewitched.\
I have set them at your feet, and I plead my case.\
Because she has done me evil and sought out what is not right,\
may she die, and may I live!\
May her witchcraft, her spittle and her spells be undone!

May the tamarisk with its lush crown purify me.\
May the date palm, which stands against every wind, release me.\
May the soapwort, which fills the earth, cleanse me.\
May the cone, full of seed, release me.\
Before you I have become pure like grass, clean and bright like nard.\
As for the spell of the evil witch: her word is turned back into her mouth, her tongue is cut short.\
Because of her witchcraft, may the gods of the night strike her;\
may the three watches of the night undo her evil magic.

May her mouth be tallow, may her tongue be salt:\
what spoke evil against me, may it melt like tallow;\
what worked witchcraft on me, may it dissolve like salt.\
Her knots are untied, her works destroyed,\
and all her words fill the empty steppe,\
by the command of the gods of the night.

### The witch who walks the streets

**Source.** *Maqlû* Tablet III, opening incantation.

**Use.** To describe the witch so exactly that she can be seized.

**Label.** Close (opening lines only).

**Words.**

The witch who walks the streets,\
who keeps going into houses,\
who prowls the alleys,\
who scours the squares:\

### Fire god, burn her

**Source.** *Maqlû* Tablet VII, lines 183–203, and the oven incantation of the same series.

**Use.** Recited while the witch's figurines are put in the fire: the clay one bursts, the tallow one melts.

**Label.** Close (VII 183–203, some lines in an order that follows the sense); the oven incantation abridged.

**Words.**

Whoever you are, witch, who took clay from the river for an image of me,\
who buried images of me in the dark house,\
who buried my water in a tomb,\
who picked up my scraps from the dust heaps,\
who tore the fringe from my garment at the fuller's,\
who gathered the dirt of my feet from a threshold:\
I sent to the city's canal, and they pinched off clay for your image;\
I sent to the gate of the quay, and they bought me tallow for your image.\
I am sending against you the burning oven, the flaring fire god,\
the ever-burning fire god, the steady light of the gods.\
She trusts in her clever witchcraft;\
I trust in the steady light of the fire god, the judge.\
Fire god, burn her! Fire god, consume her! Fire god, overpower her!

Pure oven, great daughter of Anu,\
in whom the fire is flaring, in whom the valiant fire god has made his home,\
whose flames reach the sky:\
burn, set alight, consume my witch!\
May the life of my sorcerer and my sorceress end quickly, quickly!

### At dawn: your reflection in the water

**Source.** *Maqlû* Tablet VIII, lines 127–137.

**Use.** The last act of the night: the cleansed client looks at his own face in a bowl of pure water.

**Label.** Close (abridged).

**Words.**

You are my reflection …\
You are mine, and I am yours.\
Let no one know you; let no evil come near you.

### The night of burning

**Source.** The *Maqlû* ritual tablet (Tablet IX), as reconstructed by Tzvi Abusch and others.

**Label.** Summary.

**Ritual.**

1. **Evening, on the roof.** The client invokes the stars, the Night and the three watches. The exorcist draws a flour circle around the crucible, and the whole cosmos is asked to stand still and support the case.
2. **The night.** Figurines of the sorcerer and sorceress, made of clay, dough, tallow, wax, bitumen, wood or sesame pulp, are burned in the crucible, spell after spell. Others are bound with their arms behind them, pierced with date-palm thorns, drowned in black liquid or laid face down. In one rite a figurine of the witch's goddess of fate is defiled: a dark liquid is poured over its head.
3. **Toward dawn.** The client washes over the figurines, sending the witchcraft back to its makers. Nusku, the god of the lamp, is greeted, then the rising sun, Šamaš the judge, who confirms the verdict.
4. **Morning.** The client sees his reflection in pure water. The burnt remains go into the river or out to the steppe.

**Today.** A harmless version needs a balcony at night in late July or August, a line of flour around a fireproof bowl, small figures of wax or dough, and a basin of clean water at sunrise. Burning wax indoors is a fire risk; the ancients did it on the roof.

## Chapter III · Lifting curses and turning omens

A Babylonian could be cursed without any enemy at all: a broken oath, a forgotten wrong or an ill-omened sign was enough to bring misfortune. These rites lift such a curse, or catch the harm an omen announces before it arrives.

### Šurpu: the inventory of wrongs

**Source.** *Šurpu* ("Burning"), nine tablets in Reiner's edition, probably compiled in the Middle Babylonian period, late 2nd millennium BCE; about 170 manuscripts survive, mostly 1st millennium. Edited by Heinrich Zimmern (1896–1901) and Erica Reiner (1958).

**Use.** The sufferer is under a *mamītu*, a curse born of a broken oath or a sin, and does not know what he did. So the exorcist recites every wrong he might have done, and asks the gods to release him from whichever it was.

**Label.** Close (all of Tablet II: lines 1–103 and 187–192 line by line, the lists of 104–186 complete but condensed). Line numbers follow Reiner (1958); \[…\] marks words broken on the tablet.

**Words.**

*1–4* Release him, great gods, lords of absolution: N, son of N, whose god is N and whose goddess is N, who is sick, in danger, in distress and sleepless. Whatever he has done:

*5* He ate what was taboo to his god; he ate what was taboo to his goddess.\
*6* He said "no" for "yes" and "yes" for "no."\
*7* He pointed his finger at his fellow behind his back.\
*8* He slandered; he said what should not be said.\
*9* He \[…\] gossip.\
*10* He twisted \[…\].\
*11* He slighted his god; he despised his goddess.\
*12* \[…\] evil speech.\
*13–14* He spoke improperly; he had falsehood spoken.\
*15* He made a judge give an unjust verdict.\
*16* \[…\] always present (?).\
*17* He kept repeating and inflating his claims.\
*18* He oppressed a helpless woman.\
*19* He drove \[…\] from her city.\
*20–28* He estranged son from father and father from son, daughter from mother and mother from daughter, daughter-in-law from mother-in-law and mother-in-law from daughter-in-law, brother from brother, friend from friend.\
*29* He did not free a captive; he did not release a man in chains.\
*30* He kept a prisoner from seeing the daylight.\
*31* He ordered captives held and chains tightened.\
*32–34* He did not know his wrong; he slighted his god, he despised his goddess.\
*35* He held his father in contempt and his elder brother in hatred.\

### The onion rite

**Source.** *Šurpu* Tablets V–VI.

**Use.** To peel away curse, sin or sickness, and burn it.

**Label.** Close (abridged), with the other objects summarized.

**Words.**

As this onion is peeled and thrown into the fire,\
and the fire god consumes it entirely;\
as it will never be planted in a garden bed,\
never take root in furrow or ditch,\
never send up a stalk to see the sun,\
never come to the table of god or king:\
so may the oath, the curse, the pain and the distress,\
the sickness, the sin, the crime and the wrong\
that are in my body, my flesh and my veins\
be peeled away like this onion.\
May the fire god consume them today.\
May the curse go out, and may I see the light!

**Ritual.** The same words are said over each object in turn, each thrown into the fire: a cluster of dates is stripped, a piece of reed matting is unravelled, goat hair is plucked, red wool is burned, flour is scattered. The rite ends: "May the oath be peeled away like this onion, stripped off like these dates, unravelled like this matting. Oath, be adjured by heaven! Be adjured by earth!"

**Today.** This is the most practicable rite in the book: an onion, a few dates, a scrap of rush matting, a tuft of wool or goat hair, a handful of flour and a fire. Name what you want gone as you peel and burn.

### Namburbi: turning back a bad omen

**Source.** The *namburbi* rituals (Sumerian *nam-búr-bi*, "its release"), 1st millennium BCE, from Nineveh, Assur, Nimrud, Sultantepe, Babylon, Sippar, Ur and Uruk. Edited by Richard Caplice (1965–1974) and Stefan Maul (1994).

**Use.** When a sign announces harm (a dog howling in the house, a snake falling on the bed, fungus on the wall, an eclipse), the harm is already inside the sign, waiting. The namburbi lifts it out before it lands.

**Label.** Summary (the common pattern), with the standard formula.

**Ritual.**

1. Go to the riverbank, to "a place difficult of access"; set up small altars and a reed hut.
2. Purify the client and lead him to a spot strewn with garden herbs behind an altar.
3. Make a clay figurine of the portent: the dog, the snake, whatever was seen.
4. Plead before Šamaš the judge, and Ea and Asalluḫi. The client never disputes the verdict; he appeals it, and asks that the evil be kept inside the body of the portent.
5. Throw the figurine into the river, "down to the Apsû."
6. Go home by a different road, wearing an amulet, and do not look back.

**Words (the standard formula).**

May the evil of this dog not come near me, not approach me,\
not press on me, not reach me!

### The bad-dream clod

**Source.** The dream rituals that accompany the Assyrian Dream Book (*Zaqīqu*), 1st millennium BCE, Nineveh (edited by A. Leo Oppenheim, 1956), and the dream-ritual compendium from Assur, KAR 252.

**Use.** To dissolve the evil of a nightmare before it comes true.

**Label.** Close (abridged).

**Ritual.** Take a clod of earth from a threshold place: the foot of a wall, beside a closed door, the edge of waste ground. Hold it and tell it every dream you saw. Then say the words, and throw it into water.

**Words.**

Clod, as I throw you into the water\
you will crumble, dissolve and come apart.\
So may the evil of the dream I saw\
crumble, dissolve and come apart, and be cast away!\
Šamaš, turn the dream I saw to good.

**Today.** A lump of dry earth and a bowl of water or a stream are all it needs.

## Chapter IV · The dead

The Mesopotamian dead (Sumerian *gidim*, Akkadian *eṭemmu*) lived on in the dust and dark of the netherworld and depended on the living for water, food and remembrance. A ghost that was neglected, or that had died badly, came back, and it caused illness.

### The litany of restless ghosts

**Source.** The ghost passages of *Udug-hul* and of the 1st-millennium ghost rituals collected by JoAnn Scurlock (2006); the first five lines follow a standard Akkadian formula.

**Use.** To name every possible kind of ghost, so that whichever one is haunting the client is caught by the spell.

**Label.** Close (first five lines); Composite (the rest).

**Words.**

Whether you are the ghost of one who lies unburied,\
or the ghost who has no one to care for him,\
or the ghost who has no one to make him offerings,\
or the ghost who has no one to pour him water,\
or the ghost who has no one to call his name;\
whether you are the ghost of one who drowned in the river,\
or of one who died in the steppe and was never buried,\
or of a woman who died in childbirth,\
or of a girl who died unmarried, or a young man who never took a wife,\
or of a wet nurse, a soldier or a slave:\
by heaven be adjured! By earth be adjured!

**Note.** The kind of death shaped the illness. A drowned man's ghost, for instance, left its victim gasping "like one who has just come up out of the water."

**Compare** R. Campbell Thompson's 1903 rendering of a parallel passage, now in the public domain (excerpts): "Whether thou art a ghost that hath come from the earth, or a phantom of night that hath no couch … or one that lieth dead in the desert … or a ghost unburied … or a hag-demon, or a ghoul, or a robber-sprite, or a weeping woman that hath died with a babe at the breast … thou shalt have no water to drink … Into the house enter thou not. Through the fence break thou not."

### Sending a ghost away: the ghost who needed a wife

**Source.** A Babylonian exorcist's tablet in the British Museum, about 1500 BCE, published by Irving Finkel in *The First Ghosts* (2021). Half the tablet is lost. Its back carries a faint drawing of a bearded male ghost, his hands bound, being led away by a woman on a rope.

**Use.** Against a ghost that "seizes hold of a person and pursues him and cannot be loosed."

**Label.** Summary.

**Ritual.** Make two figurines, a man and a woman. Dress the woman in four red garments and a purple cloth and give her a gold brooch. Furnish her completely: a bed, a chair, a mat, a towel, a comb and a flask. At sunrise, facing the sun, set out two vessels of beer, a special vessel and a juniper censer, and draw a curtain like a diviner's. Place the figurines with their gear and speak to Šamaš, who passes through the netherworld every night. The last line of the tablet is a command: "Do not look behind you!"

**Note.** Finkel reads the rite as giving the ghost a bride, so that he finally goes where he belongs. Harsher rites existed for stubborn ghosts: tying up the ghost's figurine to make it swear to stay away, or offering it water laced with urine (one ritual calls for three libations of donkey urine).

### Raising a ghost: the necromancer's trade

**Source.** Two tablets preserve the procedure itself, both edited by Irving Finkel, "Necromancy in Ancient Mesopotamia," *Archiv für Orientforschung* 29–30 (1983–84). K 2779 comes from Ashurbanipal's library at Nineveh (7th century BCE) and was probably copied from an older tablet brought from elsewhere; only its upper half survives. BM 36703 is a Late Babylonian tablet from Babylon. The wider evidence is an Old Assyrian letter from Kanesh in Anatolia (c. 1950–1850 BCE), Babylonian word lists, and Tablet XII of the *Epic of Gilgameš*.

**Use.** To make a ghost visible and audible so that it can be questioned, and then to keep the harm it brings away from the house. K 2779 treats the two as one procedure, which is why it has been classed with the namburbi rites (Chapter III).

**Label.** Close (both procedures of K 2779 as preserved, after Finkel's translation). The incantation itself is written in an obscure "magic-speak" Sumerian; its wording is in Finkel's edition.

**Ritual: to see the ghost.**

1. Take mouldy wood and fresh leaves of the Euphrates poplar; crush them and steep them in water, oil, beer and wine.
2. Add snake tallow, lion tallow and crab tallow; white honey; a frog that lives among the pebbles; the hair of a dog, a cat and a fox; bristles of a chameleon and of a red lizard; a frog's "claw" and the end of its gut; the left wing of a grasshopper; and marrow from the long bone of a goose.
3. Dry, crush and sieve it all, and mix it in wine, water and milk with *amḫara*-plant.
4. Recite the incantation over it three times and anoint your eyes with it.

The tablet promises: "You will see the ghost, and he will speak with you. You can look at the ghost, and he will talk with you."

**Ritual: to avert the ghost's evil.**

1. Crush a potsherd from a ruin mound in water and sprinkle the house with it.
2. For three days make offerings to the family ghosts: beer with roasted barley.
3. Set juniper on a censer before Šamaš, pour him fine beer and make him an offering.
4. Recite an incantation to Šamaš, judge of heaven and the netherworld, that the evil in the ghost's cry be kept away.
5. Do this for three days. Then wash the person's hands and anoint them with oil and *sikillu*-plant (Ú.SIKIL). The tablet ends: "It is finished."

**The Babylon tablet.** BM 36703 has the same two halves. Lines 1–9 conjure a ghost so that it can be consulted; the rest averts the evil that a ghost's cry portends. Both tablets prescribe an elaborate concoction, recited over three times and smeared on the face or the eyes, and Finkel notes that its ingredients stand apart from the ordinary pharmacopoeia.

**The wider evidence.** In the Kanesh letter, two sisters seek answers from women dream interpreters, women omen readers and the *eṭemmū*, "the spirits of the dead." It is the oldest known record of consulting the dead (some read the word as "necromancers"). The word lists name the professionals: the "ghost person" (Sumerian *lú-gidim-ma*, Akkadian *ša eṭemmi*) and the man or woman "who brings up a ghost" (*mušēlû eṭemmi*, *mušēlītu*). In *Gilgameš* XII, a hole is opened in the earth and the ghost of Enkidu rises "like a breath of wind" to tell his friend how the dead fare.

**Note.** Several identifications are uncertain (the frog's "claw," the red lizard, *amḫara*), and the lost lower half of K 2779 may have held more. The pairing on both tablets is the lesson: whoever calls a ghost up must also know how to undo the harm.

**Today.** Not as written. A paste of animal fats, frog and insect parts, beer and milk smeared on the eyes invites a serious eye infection, and several of the animals are protected species. The second rite (sprinkling the house, offering beer and incense, washing the hands) is harmless.

### Feeding the dead

**Source.** The *kispu* offering for the dead, attested from Old Babylonian Mari to the Neo-Assyrian period, and the ghost incantations that list its duties.

**Use.** To keep the family dead content, and in the netherworld.

**Label.** Summary.

**Ritual.** The caretaker of the dead (*pāqidu*), usually the eldest son, owed three things: to make the funerary offering of food (*kispa kasāpu*), to pour cool water (*mê naqû*) and to call the dead person's name aloud (*šuma zakāru*). The offering was renewed regularly, above all at the end of the month, when the moon was hidden.

**Today.** At dusk on the last day of a lunar month, pour cool water on the ground or into a bowl and say the names of your dead aloud. Of all the rites in this book, it is the one most people already half-know.

## Chapter V · House, mother and child

Evil comes in at the door. These are the spells of ordinary households: guarding the threshold, bringing a child safely into the world, getting it to sleep, and dealing with the everyday terrors of dog bites and toothache.

### Guardians under the floor

**Source.** *Šēp lemutti ina bīt amēli parāsu*, "to block the foot of evil from a man's house" (KAR 298 and duplicates), 1st millennium BCE, edited by Frans Wiggermann (1992). Archaeology matches the text: brick boxes of clay figurines found under floors, corners and thresholds of Neo-Assyrian and Neo-Babylonian houses and palaces.

**Use.** To seal a house against every evil the text lists at its start: demons, angry gods, ghosts of ancestors, disease, misfortune, Fate and Death.

**Label.** Close (the two invocations); Summary (the rite).

**Words: taking the clay.**

Clay pit, clay pit, you are the clay pit of Anu and Enlil,\
the clay pit of Ea, lord of the deep, the clay pit of the great gods.\
To be placed in the house of N, son of N, to drive out the foot of evil,\
I pinch off their clay before you, in the clay pit.

**Words: installing the sages.**

You are the statues of the sages, the watchers.

**Ritual.** The exorcist models protective figures: the seven sages (*apkallū*) carved in wood, fish-cloaked or bird-headed; monsters defeated in myth and now enlisted as guards; and clay dogs. Groups of seven are buried at the head of the bed, in the foundation, at the threshold of the chapel and in front of the door behind the chair.

**The dogs of Nineveh.** Five painted clay dogs were found buried at a doorway of Ashurbanipal's North Palace at Nineveh (Room S, door d, about 645 BCE), each painted a different colour, each with its name written on its side. They are now in the British Museum (BM 30001–30005). Their names are spells in themselves:

- "Don't think, bite!"
- "Loud is his bark!"
- "Biter of his foe!"
- "Catcher of the enemy!"
- "Expeller of evil!"

**Today.** A small clay dog with a name scratched on its side, buried under the doorstep or kept by the door, is the oldest house guardian in the book.

### The Cow of Sîn

**Source.** A birth incantation known in Middle Assyrian and Neo-Assyrian versions, notably in the Assur birth compendium (BAM 248); edited by Niek Veldhuis (1991).

**Use.** For a woman in hard labour.

**Label.** Close (abridged).

**Words.**

There was a cow of Sîn named Geme-Sîn, "Maid of Sîn."\
She was beautifully adorned, lovely in form.\
Sîn saw her and loved her.\
Out of sight of the herdsmen, a fierce young bull mounted her.\
When her days were full and her months complete,\
the cow knelt and went into labour.\
At her cry, at her screams of labour,\
Sîn, the light of heaven, heard her.\
Two daughters of Anu came down from heaven:\
one carried oil from the jar, the other the water of labour.\
With the oil from the jar she touched her brow;\
with the water of labour she sprinkled her whole body.\
She did it a second time; she did it a third time,\
and the calf dropped to the ground.\
As Geme-Sîn gave birth safely,\
so may this young woman in labour give birth safely!\
Let the midwife not be delayed; let the pregnant woman be delivered!

**Note.** In some versions the helpers who descend are "two protective spirits of heaven" (*lamassū*). The myth shows the cure being done in heaven so that it can be done again on earth.

### Hush, little one

**Source.** An Old Babylonian tablet (BM 122691), 18th century BCE, said to come from near Nippur; edited by Walter Farber (1981). A second version is on an Ashmolean tablet (OECT 11, 2).

**Use.** "Incantation to quiet a little one": a spell, and almost certainly a lullaby.

**Label.** Close (first version); Close, abridged (second).

**Words.**

Little one, who lived in the house of darkness,\
now you have come out, now you have seen the light of the sun.\
Why do you cry? Why do you scream?\
Why did you not cry in there?\
You have woken the god of the house; the bull-man has stirred:\
"Who woke me? Who startled me?"\
"The little one woke you; the little one startled you!"\
As on drinkers of wine, as on the tavern-keeper's child,\
may sleep fall on him!

**Akkadian (first version, normalized as in SEAL).** *ṣeḫrum wāšib bīt ekletim*\
*lū tattaṣâm tātamar nūr Šamaš*\
*ammīn tabakki ammīn tugāg*\
*ullikī'a ammīn lā tabkî*\
*ili bītim tedki kusarikkum iggeltâ*\
*mannum idkianni mannum ugallitanni*\
*ṣeḫrum idkīka ṣeḫrum ugallitka*\
*kīma šātî karānim kīma mār sābītim*\
*limqutaššum šittum*

**Words: the second version.**

Because of your crying the god of the house cannot sleep;\
slumber does not take the goddess of the house.\
Whom shall I send to Enkidu? …\
"He who caught the deer, let him catch this one!\
He who bound the gazelle, let him bind this one!"\
Until his mother wakes him, may he not wake!

### The mad dog

**Source.** Old Babylonian spells known in several copies (VS 17, 8 in Berlin; BM 79125 and others), c. 1900–1600 BCE.

**Use.** "Spell to save one bitten by a dog."

**Label.** Composite.

**Words.**

Long of leg, swift of running,\
gaunt with hunger, short of food.\
The shade of the wall is where he stands;\
the threshold is where he lies.\
In his teeth he carries his seed:\
wherever he bit, he left his offspring.\
The incantation is not mine: it is the incantation of Damu and Gula.\
Gula spoke it; I recited it.

**Akkadian (opening of one copy, normalized).** *urruk birkīšu aruḫ lasāmam*\
*īṣ bubūtam etnuš akālam*\
*ina šinnīšu e'il nīlšu*\
*ašar iššuku mārāšu īzib*

**Ritual.** One copy says: anoint the bite with oil and apply ghee while reciting. Another: take earth from the foundation of a tavern wall, smear it on the bite and bandage it, "and he will get better."

**Note.** The dog's "offspring" left in the wound is rabies. The Laws of Ešnunna (§§56–57) fine the owner of a known vicious dog that bites a man to death 40 shekels of silver.

**Today.** No. A dog bite needs washing and a doctor; rabies is still almost always fatal once symptoms appear.

### The toothache worm

**Source.** A Babylonian tablet in the British Museum (CT 17, 50), 1st millennium BCE, from Babylon. Its colophon says it was copied from an old fired tablet belonging to Marduk-nādin-aḫi, by Nabû-nādin-ipri, son of Kudurrānu.

**Use.** "Incantation against toothache."

**Label.** Close.

**Words.**

After Anu made the heavens,\
the heavens made the earth,\
the earth made the rivers,\
the rivers made the canals,\
the canals made the marsh,\
and the marsh made the worm.\
The worm went weeping before Šamaš;\
before Ea its tears flowed:\
"What will you give me to eat?\
What will you give me to suck?"\
"I will give you the ripe fig and the apricot."\
"What are they to me, the ripe fig and the apricot?\
Lift me up and let me live between the tooth and the gum!\
I will suck the blood of the tooth\
and gnaw away the gum."\
(Drive in the peg and seize its foot.)\
"Because you said this, worm,\
may Ea strike you with the might of his hand!"

**Akkadian (normalized).** *ultu Anum ibnû šamê*\
*šamû ibnû erṣetum*\
*erṣetum ibnû nārāti*\
*nārāti ibnâ atappāti*\
*atappāti ibnâ rušumta*\
*rušumta ibnû tūltu*\
*illik tūltu ana pān Šamaš ibakki*\
*ana pān Ea illakā dimāša*\
*mīna tattanna ana akāliya*\
*mīna tattanna ana munzuqiya*\
(Ea's offer and the worm's reply: two lines)\
*šuknanni-ma ina birīt šinni u lašḫi šūšibanni*\
*ša šinni-ma lunzuqa damīšu*\
*u ša lašḫim luksup kusāpēšu*\
*sikkata rite-ma šēpa ṣabat*\

*limḫaṣki Ea ina dannati rittišu*

**Ritual.** Mix small beer, a lump of malt and oil. Recite the incantation three times over it and put it on the tooth.

**Note.** The line in brackets is an instruction to the healer, probably to grip and pull the tooth. The tooth-worm survived in European folk dentistry until the 18th century.

**Today.** Beer, malt and oil will do no harm. A dentist will do more.

## Chapter VI · Love, desire and favour

Love magic is among the oldest written magic of all: the first love spell predates Hammurabi by five centuries. The later Assyrian material is frank and practical, and it is translated here as it stands. The chapter ends with the rites for winning favour at court, which work on the same principle: they turn another person's heart.

### The Kish love charm

**Source.** MAD 5, 8 (CDLI P285640), an Old Akkadian tablet from Kish, c. 2340–2200 BCE, now in the Ashmolean Museum, Oxford. It is the oldest love incantation known. Edited by Joan and Aage Westenholz, "Help for Rejected Suitors: The Old Akkadian Love Incantation MAD V 8," *Orientalia* 46 (1977), and by Nathan Wasserman, *Akkadian Love Literature of the Third and Second Millennium BCE* (2016).

**Use.** For a man whose love is not returned.

**Label.** Close (complete; obscure words marked (?); line numbers after the CDLI transliteration).

**Words.**

*obv. 1–11* Enki loves the love-charm.\
The love-charm, Ištar's child, sits in her lap,\
moving through the sap of the incense tree.\
You two lovely maidens, you are in bloom!\
Down you come to the garden, down to the garden,\
and you have drunk (?) the sap of the incense tree.\
*12* I have seized your drooling mouth;\
*13–14* I have seized your many-coloured (?) eyes;\
*15–16* I have seized your vulva, (wet) with urine.\
*17–19* I hurried to the garden of Sîn\
and cut the poplar\
*rev. 1* for her day.\
*2–5* Circle me among the box trees,\
as the shepherd circles his flock,\
as the goat its kid, the ewe its lamb, the mare its foal.\
*6–10* His arms are adorned (?); oil and the harp; his lips …;\
a cup of oil is in his hands,\
the scent of cedar at his shoulders.\
*11–12* The love-charms have won her over and driven her to ecstasy.\
*13* I have seized your mouth of love.\
*14–19* I adjure you by Ištar and Išḫara:\
until his neck and your neck are entwined,\
you shall find no peace!

**Akkadian (obv. 12–16; rev. 14–19).** *aḫuz pâki ša rugātim*\
*aḫuz burāmāti īnīki*\
*aḫuz ūrki ša šīnātim* …\
*Ištar u Išḫara utammēki*\
*adi ṣawāršu u ṣawārki lā etemdā*\
*lā tapaššaḫīni*

**Note.** The charm works by compulsion. The love-charm (*ir'emum*) seizes the woman's mouth, eyes and vulva, each named with a bodily fluid or quality, and she is put under oath by Ištar and Išḫara, goddesses of love and of oaths, to have no peace until their necks are entwined. Her consent is the thing the spell sets out to override; in Mesopotamian love magic that is the rule, not the exception. The third seizure is literal in the Akkadian, *ūrki ša šīnātim*, "your vulva of urine." The garden, the sap and the cedar oil are the stock imagery of Mesopotamian love poetry.

### Rising of the heart

**Source.** The ŠÀ.ZI.GA texts (Sumerian *šà-zi-ga*, Akkadian *nīš libbi*, "rising of the heart"), incantations and recipes for male desire and potency. The tradition runs from the Middle Babylonian to the Achaemenid period, with copies from Ḫattuša (Boğazköy; KUB 4, 48 and KUB 37, 80), Ashurbanipal's library at Nineveh (7th century BCE), the exorcists' house at Assur (several in the hand of Kiṣir-Nabû) and Sultantepe. Edited by Robert Biggs, *ŠÀ.ZI.GA: Ancient Mesopotamian Potency Incantations* (TCS 2, 1967), whose text numbers are used below, and re-edited by Gioele Zisa, *The Loss of Male Sexual Desire in Ancient Mesopotamia* (2021). Markham Geller has questioned the old reading of the title as referring only to erection.

**Use.** To restore a man's desire and potency, and in several texts to rouse the woman too; many rites anoint both partners.

**Label.** Close (the words of nos. 14 and 15, and all the rites); Summary (the words of nos. 9, 13, 16 and 23, and the last lines of no. 14). Text numbers are Biggs's.

**No. 14** (KAR 236:1–17; duplicates KAR 70 rev. 10–24 and KAR 243).

Let the wind blow! Let the mountains quake!\
Let the clouds gather! Let the rain fall!\
Let the ass swell and mount the jenny!\
Let the buck be roused!\
At the head of my bed a ram is tied;\
at the foot of my bed a weaned sheep is tied.\
(The closing lines call on the partner, in explicit terms, to make love.)

*Rite.* Powder magnetic iron ore and iron and put them in *pīru*-oil. Recite the incantation over it seven times. The man rubs his penis and the woman her vagina with the oil; then they make love.

**No. 15** (LKA 101 rev. 12–19; LKA 95 rev. 6–11; STT 280 iv 37–41).

Let the wind blow! Let the mountains quake!\
Let the clouds gather! Let the rain fall!\
Let my potency flow like river water;\
let my penis be a taut harp string!

*Rite.* Take a harp string, tie three knots in it and recite the incantation over it seven times. Tie it to his right hand and to his left.

**No. 16** (KAR 236). Tie a ram at the head of the bed and a weaned sheep at its foot, and tie their wool around the waist. The incantation asks for lovemaking like that of the ram, the weaned sheep, the partridge, the pig, the wild bull and the stag, each with its number of times, rising from eleven to fifty.

**No. 13** (KAR 70 rev. 45–49). A buck is tied at the head of the bed and a ram at its foot; the incantation commands "Rise! Mount!" and calls up a prancing wild bull.

**No. 9** (LKA 103:1–13). The speaker asks for lovemaking like a mountain goat's, a stag's and a partridge's, "as in my youth." *Rite:* put the head of a male partridge, a silver bead, a gold bead and a stag's dewclaw in a leather bag and recite over it seven times.

**No. 23** (LKA 102 rev. 6–16). An incantation to Adad, the storm god. *Rite:* magnetite and iron in *pīru*-oil, seven recitations, and both partners are anointed.

**The common recipe.** Many rites use the same mixture: crushed magnetic iron ore, sometimes with iron, in oil, recited over three or seven times and rubbed on both partners. One version anoints the man's navel with magnetite in oil and the woman's with iron in oil, so that the two are drawn together. Biggs suggests the powders were meant to add friction, not only to lubricate. A catalogue of the corpus lists incantations by their first lines, among them "Let the wind blow! Let the mountains quake!" and "Let the wind blow! Let the grove sway!"

**Today.** Not on the skin. Powdered iron ore is abrasive and will irritate or tear the delicate skin it was meant for.

### Charms to make a woman come (KAR 61 and KAR 69)

**Source.** Two tablets from Assur, KAR 61 and KAR 69, given in transliteration and translation in the appendix to Biggs 1967.

**Use.** For a man who wants a particular woman to come to him and lie with him.

**Label.** Close (the rites, as preserved); Summary (the broken incantations).

**Ritual.**

1. **An apple or a pomegranate** (KAR 61:1–10). The incantation "The beautiful woman" is addressed to Inana (Ištar). Recite it three times over an apple or a pomegranate and give the fruit to the woman. When she has sucked its juice, she will come to you, and you can make love to her.
2. **If she does not come** (KAR 61:11–21). Throw *tappinnu*-flour into the river as an offering to Ea. Take clay from both banks and make a figurine of her; write her name on its left hip. Facing Šamaš, recite "The beautiful woman" over it, then bury it at the outer gate of the West Gate. When she walks over it, recite the incantation three times: she will come.
3. **At the head of the bed** (KAR 61:22–25). Wrap *mēsu*-wood, boxwood, a stone and other things in sheep's wool and put them at the head of the bed. "She cannot stop talking," and you can make love to her.
4. **Oil at the neck** (KAR 61 rev. 15–29). A broken incantation names clay carried down by the Tigris and the Euphrates, tortoises and fish. Two incantations for love-play are recited over oil, and something, lost in the break, is placed around the neck.
5. **Gaga** (KAR 69:1–5). An incantation of Gaga is recited seven times over a pomegranate or an apple, which the woman sucks.
6. **The stars** (KAR 69:6–19). The incantation invokes the fig tree, the Pleiades and Mercury: Anu and Enlil created the stars and Nudimmud (Ea) endowed them, and the speaker sends the stars to the woman he names. Make a figurine of her from the fat of a white sheep's left kidney and write her name on its right hip. Set up a censer of juniper before Ištar, pour a libation of beer and recite seven times. She will speak to you; then she will come.
7. **The Pleiades** (KAR 69 rev. 2–9). Break juniper; tie a linen cloth at the waist; recite seven times. Put it at the head of the bed, a censer at the foot, and pour beer: she will come.
8. **"I have seized you"** (KAR 69 rev. 10 ff.). The incantation binds her as bitumen clings to a boat. Recite it seven times over beer and give it to her to drink. The rite is done on the 21st day.
9. **"Ištar has looked upon you"** (KAR 69 rev. 20–27). Make a figurine of bitumen mixed with clay, write her name on its left hip and bury it where she will walk.

**Note.** Like the Kish charm, these are compulsion charms: nothing in them asks what the woman wants. A named figurine buried in the victim's path is exactly what *Maqlû* accuses witches of doing.

### Lying with Nanāya: a fragment from Ḫattuša

**Source.** A fragment in Middle Babylonian Akkadian found at Ḫattuša, the Hittite capital (excavation number 61/r), published by Gernot Wilhelm (1991, no. 27) and edited by Nathan Wasserman, *Akkadian Love Literature* (2016), pp. 239–240.

**Use.** A love incantation invoking Nanāya, a goddess of love, and Kilili, a figure associated with Ištar. The fragment does not show whether the speaker is a man or a woman.

**Label.** Close (fragment; primes count lines from the break).

**Words.**

*15′* I am lying with you, Nanāya!\
*16′* \[…\] like a ewe with a ram \[…\]\
*17′* \[…\] an ululation like a woman near childbirth, a war-cry \[…\]\
*18′–19′* In place of my wailing woman \[…\] your two openings \[…\] your bed \[…\]\
*20′* I will make love to you, Kilili; I will lie with you, Kilili!

**Instruction** (*22′–23′*). "Approach her right side! Come on! Approach her lofty bedchamber!"

### Entering the palace: charms for favour at court

**Source.** *Egalkurrû* (Sumerian *é-gal-ku₄-ra*, "entering the palace") is the exorcists' name for charms that make a king, an official or a judge well disposed. Two tablets from Assur, edited by Wiebke Meinhold, KAL 7 (2017), nos. 24 and 25:

- **No. 24** (VAT 7820), copied by Kiṣir-Aššur as a "fourth extract"; duplicates LKA 106, LKA 107a and BM 103385.
- **No. 25** (VAT 8230 + VAT 13033a), with a colophon naming Kakkullānu the apprentice. An older copy is KAR 237, edited by Erich Ebeling (MAOG 5/3) and discussed by H. Stadhouders (CRRAI 56). Further duplicates: KAR 238, LKA 105, LKA 107, LKA 107a, BM 103385, SpTU I 18 and SpTU II 24.

**Use.** To be admitted to an audience and received kindly, or to win a lawsuit.

**Label.** Close (no. 24); the seven incantations of no. 25 condensed.

**No. 24: against strife.**

Strife comes from the roof, enmity from the earth.\
Among good brothers swords are raised; among friends there is enmity.\
May good befall me; may my words be pleasing!

*Rite.* Recite it over a lump of salt and throw the salt down before the prince. Or recite it seven times over an *asḫur*-stone, anoint your face, hang the stone around your neck and go into the palace.

**No. 25: seven incantations.**

1. "I anoint myself with pure oil and climb onto the roof: the sky shakes, the mountains break, oil bursts from a strong reed, the staffs of the great officials break in the palace gate." Three times over pure oil; anoint your eyes and hands, and your lawsuit will succeed.
2. The oils of life, of the stone vessels, of Anu and Antu. Seven times; anoint your eyes and hands, and the prince will rejoice over you.
3. Anointed with the oil of life, the speaker raises his head before king and nobles, and seven maidens appear. Three times over good oil.
4. Ea's oil: "May the prince rejoice over me as Sîn, Šamaš and Ištar do." Seven times; anoint your face and hands, and all who see you will rejoice.
5. "Incantation for a cheerful face": three times over *sassatu*-grass, which is tucked behind the ears.
6. For the shoes: three times over your shoes, so that wherever you go people rejoice over you.
7. For the belt: a belt of life and a belt of red wool. "May the lord look at me, rise, kiss me and turn his heart to me." Three times over the belt.

**Today.** Harmless: a little scented oil on the face and hands before a hard meeting is the whole rite.

## Chapter VII · Night and divination

The Babylonians did not only fight fate; they asked it questions. The diviner (*bārû*) read the will of the gods in a lamb's liver, in oil on water and in the stars, and he worked at night, when the gods of the day had gone to bed.

### Prayer to the Gods of the Night

**Source.** A late Old Babylonian diviner's prayer, c. 1800–1600 BCE, known from two tablets, one in the Louvre (AO 6769) and one in the Hermitage, St Petersburg. It calls itself "24 lines, a prayer of the night."

**Use.** Said by the diviner before extispicy, the reading of a sacrificed lamb's entrails, asking the stars to put the truth into the lamb.

**Label.** Close.

**Words.**

The princes are shut in,\
the bolts are lowered, the rings are in place.\
The noisy crowds have fallen silent;\
the doors that stood open are locked.\
The gods of the land and the goddesses of the land,\
Šamaš, Sîn, Adad and Ištar,\
have gone into the lap of heaven.\
They give no judgment; they decide no case.\
Night is veiled.\
The palace, its chapel and its sanctuary are dark.\
The traveller calls out to his god; the plaintiff sleeps on.\
The judge of truth, the father of the fatherless,\
Šamaš, has gone into his chamber.\
May the great gods of the night,\
shining Girra, warrior Erra,\
the Bow, the Yoke,\
Orion, the Dragon,\
the Wagon, the Goat,\
the Bison, the Horned Serpent,\
stand by me!\
In the extispicy I am performing,\
in the lamb I am offering,\
put the truth for me!

**Akkadian (selected lines, normalized after SEAL).** *pullulū rubû*\
*wašrū sikkūrū šīrētum šaknā*\
*ḫabrātum nišū šaqummā*\
*petûtum uddulū bābū* …\
*pussumat mušītim*\
*ekallum šaḫurša kummu adrū* …\
*ina têrti eppušu*\
*ina puḫād akarrabu*\
*kittam šuknām*

**Note.** The Wagon is the Big Dipper and the Goat is Lyra; the other constellations are identified less securely. When the sun god, the judge, is away, the stars sit in court in his place.

### Oil on water

**Source.** Old Babylonian oil-omen tablets (about eight are edited, among them BM 22446 and BM 22447 in the British Museum and YOS 10, 57, 58 and 62 at Yale), early 2nd millennium BCE; edited by Giovanni Pettinato (1966). The collection was later copied as far away as Ḫattuša, the Hittite capital.

**Use.** To put a question to the gods and read the answer in the shapes oil makes on water (lecanomancy).

**Label.** Summary, with one omen given Close.

**Ritual.** The diviner pours oil into a bowl of water and reads how it moves: whether it spreads, divides, forms rings or sinks. A second compendium covers the reverse operation, pouring water onto oil.

**Words (one omen).**

If you throw water onto the oil and it spreads evenly: the man's household will be scattered.

**Today.** A bowl of water and a spoonful of olive oil. Write down what you see before deciding what it means: the omen tablets are lists of observations first.

## Chapter VIII · Curses

Harmful private magic counted as witchcraft, a capital crime under Assyrian law, so the great curses of Mesopotamia that survive are public and legal. Kings, landowners, treaty-makers and librarians called down the gods on whoever broke their word or touched their monuments, and these curses are the most violent texts in the language.

### The Curse of Agade

**Source.** A Sumerian poem known from Ur III and Old Babylonian copies, c. 2000–1700 BCE, about the fall of Agade (Akkad), the capital of Sargon's dynasty, c. 2200 BCE. Edited by Jerrold Cooper (1983); lines 210–281 in the standard text.

**Use.** Not a working spell but a lesson: the gods' curse on a city whose king, Narām-Sîn, attacked Enlil's temple, the Ekur at Nippur.

**Label.** Close (the whole curse and its fulfilment, lines 210–281, with 272–280 condensed). Line numbers follow ETCSL.

**Words.**

*210–213* Sîn, Enki, Inana, Ninurta, Iškur, Utu, Nuska and Nisaba, the great gods,\
cooled Enlil's heart with water and cursed Agade:\
"City, you who dared to attack the Ekur, who defied Enlil,\
Agade, may what you did to Nippur be done to you!\
*214–221* May your wells be filled with severed heads.\
May friend not know friend, nor brother brother.\
May your young women be cruelly struck down;\
may your old men weep for their dead wives.\
May your doves moan on their ledges,\
your small birds be struck down in their nests.\
May you live in fear, like a frightened dove!\
*222–244* Because you attacked the Ekur as though you were attacking Enlil,\
may your walls ring with lament;\
may your giguna become a heap of dust;\
may the lahama figures of your pilasters topple like drunken young men.\
May your clay return to the Abzu, clay cursed by Enki;\
may your grain return to the furrow, grain cursed by Ezina;\
may your timber return to the forest, timber cursed by Ninilduma, god of carpenters.\
May the butcher of oxen slaughter his wife,\
the butcher of sheep slaughter his child.\
May the water sweep away your beggar;\
may your prostitute hang herself at the door of her brothel;\
may your pregnant temple women lose their children.\
May your gold be sold at the price of silver,\
your silver at the price of pyrite,\
your copper at the price of lead.\
*245–255* May your strong man lose his strength:\
may he be unable to lift his sack of rations\
or take pleasure in his donkeys, and lie idle.\
May hunger kill your city.\
May those who ate fine food lie starving in the grass;\
may a man gnaw the coating of his roof\
and chew the leather hinges of his father's door.\
May gloom fall on your palace, built for joy;\
may the evils of the steppe howl there without end.\
*256–271* May foxes sweep your purification pens with their tails;\
may the *ukuku*, the bird of mourning, nest in your gates;\
may Nanna's bulls bellow there like wanderers lost in the steppe.\
May tall grass grow on your canal towpaths,\
the grass of mourning on your wagon roads;\
may wild rams and venomous snakes block your towpaths;\
may the reeds of lament grow on your plains;\
may brackish water flow where your sweet water flowed;\
may no one who settles in you find rest!"\
*272–280* And under Utu, the sun, it came to pass: everything happened as the gods had said.\
*281* Agade is destroyed. Praise Inana!

**Note.** The poem was written a century or more after the events. Narām-Sîn's own bricks show him rebuilding the Ekur, not wrecking it; the curse is a theology of disaster, not history.

### Hammurabi's curses

**Source.** The epilogue of the Laws of Hammurabi, on the basalt stele in the Louvre (Sb 8), c. 1754 BCE. Translated by Robert Harper (1904) and by L. W. King, both now in the public domain.

**Use.** Against any future ruler who ignores, alters or defaces the laws.

**Label.** Close (all fourteen curses, in order; a few divine epithets shortened).

**Words.**

If that man does not heed the words I wrote on my stele, scorns my curses and does not fear the curses of the gods; if he overturns the law I gave, changes my words, erases my name and writes his own:

May Anu the great, father of the gods, who proclaimed my reign, strip him of royal splendour, break his sceptre and curse his fate.\
May Enlil, the lord who fixes destinies, whose command cannot be changed, who made my kingship great, raise against him a revolt his hand cannot put down and let the wind that overthrows his house blow; may he decree his reign as years of groaning, of scarcity and of famine, darkness without light and death before his eyes; and may he order with his weighty word the destruction of his city, the scattering of his people, the end of his rule and the removal of his name and memory from the land.\
May Ninlil, the great mother, whose word carries weight in the Ekur, speak evil of him before Enlil in the place of judgment, and put into Enlil's mouth the ruin of his land, the destruction of his people and the pouring out of his life like water.\
May Ea, the great prince, take away his understanding and wisdom and lead him into forgetfulness; may he dam his rivers at their source and let no grain, the life of the people, grow in his land.\
May Šamaš, the great judge of heaven and earth, overturn his rule and annul his laws; may he confuse his way and wreck the march of his army; may he show him in visions the uprooting of his throne and the ruin of his land; may the sentence of Šamaš overtake him swiftly; and may he deny him water above, among the living, and below, in the earth, to his ghost.\
May Sîn, lord of heaven, take from him the crown and throne of kingship; may he lay on him a heavy penalty, a great guilt that never leaves his body; may he fill the days, months and years of his reign with sighing and tears, and decree for him a life that is like death.\
May Adad, lord of abundance, withhold from him the rain of heaven and the flood of the springs; may he destroy his land by want and famine, thunder fiercely over his city and turn his land into ruin mounds.\
May Zababa, the great warrior, shatter his weapons on the battlefield, turn day into night for him and set his enemy over him.\
May Ištar, lady of battle and war, curse his kingship in her anger and turn his good fortune to evil; may she shatter his weapons, stir up revolt against him, strike down his warriors so that the earth drinks their blood, heap their corpses on the plain, and hand him over to his enemies to be led away in bonds.\
May Nergal, the mighty one among the gods, burn his people in his fire like a reed thicket, cut off his limbs with his mighty weapon and shatter him like a clay figure.\
May Nintu, the exalted lady of the lands, deny him an heir; may she leave him no name and no offspring among his people.\
May Ninkarrak, daughter of Anu, put into his limbs a high fever, a grievous sickness, a wound that will not heal, whose nature no physician knows, which no bandage soothes, which clings like the bite of death; and may he lament his lost vigour until his life is gone.\
May the great gods of heaven and earth, all the Anunnaki, lay a curse on the temple precinct, on the walls of this Ebabbar, on his dominion, his land, his warriors, his subjects and his troops.\
May Enlil curse him with the mighty curses of his mouth, which cannot be changed, and may they overtake him at once.

**Akkadian (the opening conditions).** *šumma awīlum šū ana awâtiya ša ina narîya ašṭuru lā iqūlma*\
*errētiya imēšma errēt ilī lā īdurma* …

**Note.** In L. W. King's public-domain translation the last curse reads: "May Bel curse him with the potent curses of his mouth that can not be altered, and may they come upon him forthwith." Bel, "Lord," is Enlil.

### The boundary-stone curses

**Source.** *Kudurrus*, carved stones recording royal land grants in Babylonia, from the Kassite period (14th century BCE) into the 1st millennium BCE, many in the British Museum and the Louvre. L. W. King edited the British Museum stones in 1912 (public domain).

**Use.** To protect a land grant, and the stone that records it, from anyone who moves, defaces or disputes it.

**Label.** Composite (typical clauses).

**Words.**

Whoever in days to come removes this stone, smashes it,\
throws it into water or fire, buries it in the earth or hides it where it cannot be seen,\
or has a fool, a deaf man or a stranger do it for him;\
whoever moves this boundary or says "this field was never given":\
may Anu, Enlil and Ea, the great gods, look on him in anger,\
tear out his foundation and destroy his seed.\
May Sîn, who lives in the bright sky, clothe his body in leprosy like a garment,\
so that he roams like a wild ass outside his city wall.\
May Adad overwhelm his field with flood.\
May Gula, the great physician, put into his body a sore that never heals,\
so that he bathes in blood and pus as in water.

**Note.** The stones also carry the gods' symbols in relief (the moon's crescent, the sun's disc, Ištar's eight-pointed star, Marduk's spade, Nabû's stylus, a great snake), so the cursing gods stand guard on the stone itself.

### Esarhaddon's treaty curses

**Source.** The succession oath (*adê*) that Esarhaddon of Assyria imposed in 672 BCE to secure the throne for his son Ashurbanipal. Copies come from the temple of Nabû at Kalḫu (Nimrud) and from Tell Tayinat in Turkey. Edited by Simo Parpola and Kazuko Watanabe (SAA 2, no. 6, 1988).

**Use.** Self-curses sworn by vassals and officials, some acted out with objects as they took the oath.

**Label.** Composite (a selection; in SAA 2, no. 6, the iron earth and bronze sky are lines 528–532 and the wax and clay figures line 608). The complete treaty is in Parpola and Watanabe's edition and online in ORACC's State Archives of Assyria Online.

**Words.**

May Ninurta, first among the gods, strike you down with his fierce arrow,\
fill the plain with your corpses and give your flesh to eagles and vultures.\
Just as this ewe has been cut open and the flesh of her young put in her mouth,\
so may they make you eat, in your hunger, the flesh of your brothers, your sons and your daughters.\
Just as a honeycomb is pierced through and through with holes,\
so may your flesh be pierced with holes while you are still alive.\
Just as this bedbug stinks, so may your breath stink before god, king and people.\
Just as an image of wax is burned in fire and an image of clay dissolved in water,\
so may your figure be burned in fire and sunk in water.\
May they make your ground like iron.\
Just as rain does not fall from a sky of bronze,\
so may no rain and no dew come on your fields and meadows.

**Note.** The bronze sky and iron earth reappear in Deuteronomy 28:23: "And thy heaven that is over thy head shall be brass, and the earth that is under thee shall be iron" (King James Version). Many scholars think Deuteronomy borrowed from this treaty; others argue the curse form is older than both.

### The librarian's curse

**Source.** The standard colophons on tablets of Ashurbanipal's library at Nineveh, 7th century BCE.

**Use.** To protect the king's books from theft and from scribes who would claim them.

**Label.** Close.

**Words.**

Palace of Ashurbanipal, king of the world, king of Assyria, who trusts in Aššur and Ninlil.\
Whoever takes this tablet away, or writes his own name in place of mine,\
may Aššur and Ninlil cast him down in fury and wrath,\
and wipe out his name and his seed from the land.

A shorter variant: "Whoever erases my name and writes his own, may Nabû, scribe of all things, erase his name."

### Ištar's threat at the gate

**Source.** *The Descent of Ištar to the Netherworld*, Akkadian, known from Middle and Neo-Assyrian copies from Assur and Nineveh. The goddess makes the same threat to Anu in the *Epic of Gilgameš*, Tablet VI.

**Use.** Not a spell, but the most frightening words of power in Akkadian: what the goddess says to the gatekeeper of the dead.

**Label.** Close.

**Words.**

Gatekeeper, open your gate for me!\
Open your gate, so that I may come in!\
If you do not open the gate for me to come in,\
I will smash the door, I will shatter the bolt,\
I will smash the doorpost, I will tear down the doors.\
I will raise up the dead to eat the living:\
the dead will outnumber the living!

## Chapter IX · Rites of temple and king

The largest rites were performed for gods and kings: one brought a statue to life, the other let a king survive his own death sentence.

### Opening the mouth of a god

**Source.** *Mīs pî* ("washing of the mouth"), ritual tablets from Nineveh (Neo-Assyrian) and Babylon (Late Babylonian) with their Sumerian incantations, 1st millennium BCE; edited by Christopher Walker and Michael Dick (2001). Mouth-washing of statues is already mentioned in the Middle Babylonian period.

**Use.** To turn a newly made statue into a living god. One text states the problem plainly: without the opening of the mouth, "this statue cannot smell incense, eat food or drink water."

**Label.** Summary.

**Ritual.**

1. **In the workshop,** on a favourable day: set up two holy-water vessels, a red cloth in front of the god and a white cloth to its right, and offerings for Ea and Asalluḫi. Wash the statue's mouth and recite three times: "From today you go before your father Ea."
2. **To the river,** by torchlight, leading a ram, reciting "As you grew up, as you grew up from the forest." Seat the god on a reed mat, facing the sunset. Draw water for seven holy-water basins and put in tamarisk, soapwort, date-palm heart, seven palm shoots and reeds.
3. **Under the night sky:** offerings for nine great gods (Anu, Enlil, Ea, Sîn, Šamaš, Adad, Marduk, Gula and Ištar) and the stars, toward the north; and, toward the south, for the gods of craftsmen. Recite "Tamarisk, pure wood" and "On the day the god was created," washing the mouth again and again.
4. **The disowning.** The craftsmen's hands are symbolically cut off with a tamarisk blade, and they swear they did not make the god: the craft gods did.
5. **Into the temple.** The god, now able to eat, drink, smell and hear, is led to his throne.

**Note.** This is the ritual the Hebrew prophets mocked when they described idols as wood and metal "made by human hands" (Isaiah 44, Jeremiah 10). The disowning meets the same worry from the inside: the statue is declared the work of gods, not of human hands.

### The substitute king

**Source.** Letters from the scholars of Esarhaddon of Assyria (680–669 BCE), edited by Simo Parpola, and an earlier Babylonian chronicle.

**Use.** When an eclipse announced the king's death, the death was diverted onto someone else.

**Label.** Summary.

**Ritual.** The scholars worked out which part of the moon or sun was darkened, and so which land and which king the omen struck. If it was their own king, a substitute (*šar pūḫi*) was dressed in the royal robes, declared king and seated on the throne, sometimes with a substitute queen, for up to 100 days. The real king lived hidden, and the letters call him "the farmer." Exorcistic rites, including a namburbi, transferred the omen's evil onto the substitute. At the end the substitute was put to death, given a royal burial, and the real king returned.

**Two cases.** In Esarhaddon's reign a young man named Damqi was killed with his queen to protect the crown prince Šamaš-šum-ukīn. Centuries earlier, the chronicle says, King Erra-imittī of Isin set his gardener Enlil-bāni on the throne as substitute; the king then died in his palace sipping hot broth, and the gardener stayed king.

## Chapter X · Kindred magic of the ancient world

Mesopotamia's neighbours practised the same logic of likeness and transfer: break the pot, burn the wax, send the animal away. Four of the best-documented rites are given here for comparison.

### Smashing the red pots (Egypt)

**Source.** Egyptian execration texts, from the 6th Dynasty (24th–22nd century BCE) into the New Kingdom; Middle Kingdom deposits at Saqqara, at Mirgissa in Nubia (more than 175 pots) and at other sites, mostly in cemeteries. The closing list follows J. A. Wilson's translation in ANET, pp. 328–329; the Berlin bowls were published by Kurt Sethe (1926), the Brussels and Saqqara figurines by Georges Posener (1940).

**Use.** To destroy enemies of the king and of cosmic order (foreign rulers, Egyptian rebels and evil itself) before they could act.

**Label.** Summary, with the closing list Close.

**Ritual.** Write the enemies' names on red pots or on clay figurines of bound captives. Smash them and bury the pieces. Red stood for danger.

**Words (the closing list of the Middle Kingdom texts).**

… every evil word, every evil speech, every evil slander,\
every evil thought, every evil plot, every evil fight,\
every evil quarrel, every evil plan, every evil thing,\
every evil dream, every evil sleep.

### Overthrowing Apep (Egypt)

**Source.** The *Book of Overthrowing Apep* in the Papyrus Bremner-Rhind (British Museum EA 10188, columns 22–32), written about 305 BCE for the Theban priest Nesmin and preserving older temple ritual; translated by Raymond Faulkner (1937–1938).

**Use.** Recited in temples to help the sun god Ra defeat Apep (Apophis), the chaos serpent who attacks his boat every night.

**Label.** Summary, with key lines Close (abridged).

**Ritual.** Draw Apep on a new sheet of papyrus in green ink, or model him in wax (some passages ask for red wax), and write his name on him. Spit on him, trample him four times with the left foot, stab him and burn him. A further section lists the names of Apep, so that every one of them can be destroyed.

**Words.**

Rise, Ra, and crush your enemies!\
Shine, Ra, for your enemies have fallen!\
Ra triumphs over you, Apep! (four times)\
Be destroyed, Apep!

### The mouse and the ram (the Hittites)

**Source.** Hittite ritual texts from Ḫattuša, 14th–13th century BCE: the ritual of Ambazzi (CTH 391) and the ritual of Uḫḫamuwa of Arzawa against plague (CTH 410).

**Use.** To load evil onto an animal and send it away, out of a person or out of the country.

**Label.** Summary.

**Ritual.** In the ritual of Ambazzi, the evil is transferred from the client to a mouse, which is sent off into the wild to carry it away. When plague struck the army, Uḫḫamuwa's ritual crowned a ram with a wreath of coloured wool and drove it down the road into enemy country, with a prayer to whatever enemy god had sent the plague: accept this ram, make peace, and keep peace with Hatti.

**Note.** The scapegoat of Leviticus 16, sent into the wilderness bearing the people's sins, belongs to the same family of rites.

### The Aramaic wax curse

**Source.** The Sefire stele I (KAI 222), in Old Aramaic, mid-8th century BCE, found near Aleppo: a treaty between Bar-Ga'yah of KTK and Mati'el, king of Arpad.

**Use.** Treaty self-curses, acted out with objects as the oath was sworn.

**Label.** Close (abridged).

**Words.**

As this wax is burned by fire, so may Mati'el be burned by fire.\
As this bow and these arrows are broken, so may Inurta and Hadad break the bow of Mati'el and the bows of his nobles.\
As the man of wax is blinded, so may Mati'el be blinded.\
As this calf is cut up, so may Mati'el and his nobles be cut up.

**Note.** It is the closest western parallel to Esarhaddon's wax-and-clay curse (Chapter VIII), sworn about eighty years earlier.

## Appendix A · Bestiary of demons and guardians

Most Mesopotamian demons were classes, not individuals, and few were purely evil: the same monster could attack a household or, conscripted and buried under its floor, defend it.

### The attackers

| Name | What it is | What it does |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Lamaštu | Daughter of Anu; lion-headed, donkey-eared, taloned | Kills unborn and newborn children and their mothers |
| The Sebettu, "the Seven" | A band of storm-demons | Strike people and cattle; once darkened the moon. Also worshipped as protective gods, the Pleiades |
| *Utukku* (Sumerian *udug*) | The generic demon of *Udug-hul* | Attacks the body; a "good *utukku*" also exists |
| *Gallû* | Netherworld constables | Seize the living and drag them below |
| *Rābiṣu*, "the Lurker" | A demon of doorways and dark corners | Lies in wait for its victim |
| *Lilû*, *lilītu*, *ardat lilî* | Wind-spirits of young men and women who died unmarried | Roam in search of the spouses and children they never had |
| *Eṭemmu* | A ghost | Returns when neglected or badly dead and causes illness (Chapter IV) |
| Asakku | A disease demon, defeated by Ninurta in myth | Wasting fever |
| Namtar, "Fate" | Vizier of the netherworld | Plague and the hour of death |
| *Mimma lemnu*, "Any Evil" | A catch-all name for every unnamed evil | One text imagines it bull-headed; Kummâ saw it with two heads |
| Pazuzu | King of the *lilû* wind-demons | Savage, and therefore the best guard against Lamaštu |

### What an Assyrian prince saw in the netherworld

**Source.** *The Underworld Vision of an Assyrian Prince*, a single tablet from Assur (VAT 10057), 7th century BCE; edited by Alasdair Livingstone (SAA 3, no. 32, 1989). The prince Kummâ dreams that he goes down to the netherworld and sees its gods one by one. It is the most detailed description of Mesopotamian demons that survives.

**Label.** Summary (the order and descriptions follow the tablet; several are damaged).

| Being | How the prince saw it |
| --- | --- |
| Namtar, vizier of the netherworld | Holding a man by the hair in his left hand, a dagger in his right |
| Namtartu, his consort | Head of a protective genius, human hands and feet |
| Death | Head of a *mušḫuššu* dragon, human hands; feet lost |
| The evil *šēdu* | Human head and hands, a crown, eagle's feet; treading on a crocodile with his left foot |
| Alluḫappu | Lion's head, human hands and feet |
| The Upholder of Evil | Bird's head, wings spread, flying back and forth; human hands and feet |
| Ḫumuṭ-tabal, the ferryman of the dead | Head of the Anzû bird; hands and feet lost |
| The ghost | Head of an ox; hands and feet of Anzû |
| Šulak | An ordinary lion standing on its hind legs |
| The Oath (*mamītu*) | Head of a goat |
| Nedu, gatekeeper of the netherworld | Lion's head, human hands, bird's feet |
| Any Evil | Two heads, one a lion's; the other lost |
| Muḫra | Three feet, the two front ones a bird's, the back one a bull's; blazing with terrifying radiance |
| Two unnamed gods | One with the head, hands and feet of Anzû; one with a man's head and a crown |

Then a sixteenth figure: a man with a pitch-black body and a face like Anzû, dressed in red, a bow in his left hand and a dagger in his right, treading on a snake. Last of all Nergal on his throne, with lightning flashing and the gods of the netherworld kneeling. Nergal seizes the prince by the hair; his vizier Išum pleads for him, and he is sent back. The prince wakes, runs into the street and praises Nergal and Ereškigal.

## Appendix B · Powers invoked and materia magica

An exorcist needed two things: the right gods to appeal to, and the right materials to act out the cure. Both lists below are drawn from the entries in this book.

### The powers

| Power (Sumerian / Akkadian) | Domain | Role in the spells |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Enki / Ea | The Apsû, the sweet waters under the earth, at Eridu; wisdom | Owner of every incantation; gives the cure in the conversation of the gods |
| Asalluḫi / Marduk | Ea's son | The working exorcist-god who carries the case to his father |
| Ningirima | Lady of incantations | Named in closing formulas; purifier of water |
| Utu / Šamaš | The sun; judge of heaven and earth | Hears cases at dawn; presides over namburbi rites and the dispatch of ghosts |
| Gibil / Girra | Fire | Burns the witches' figurines in *Maqlû* and the curses in *Šurpu* |
| Nusku | The lamp, the fire that watches at night | Greeted at dawn in *Maqlû* |
| Nanna / Sîn | The moon | Punishes oath-breakers with skin disease; father of the calf in the birth spell |
| Gula / Ninkarrak | Healing, with her dog | Patron of physicians; dog-bite spells; disease curses |
| Damu | Gula's son, a healer | Named in closing formulas |
| Inana / Ištar; Išḫara | Love, war and oaths | Love charms; the threat at the gate |
| Nergal and Ereškigal | King and queen of the netherworld | Receive the dead; judge the prince in Kummâ's vision |
| Kusu | Purification | The holy-water basins of *Mīs pî* |
| The gods of the night | The stars, the Night and its three watches | Witnesses in *Maqlû*; give the truth in divination |

### Materia magica

| Material | What it does in the rites |
| --- | --- |
| Tamarisk | The purest wood: holy water, mouth-washing, the purifying "lush crown" of *Maqlû* |
| Soapwort (*maštakal*) | A cleansing plant steeped in holy water |
| Date palm (heart, shoots, thorns) | Release and purity; its thorns pierce witches' figurines |
| Cedar, cypress, juniper | Incense and fumigation |
| Flour | The protective circle (*zisurrû*); scattered offerings |
| Clay from the clay pit or riverbank | Figurines of demons, guardians, witches and patients |
| Tallow, wax, bitumen, dough, sesame pulp | Figurines meant to melt, burst or burn |
| Goat hair and wool (red, white) | Cords and knots; burned in *Šurpu* |
| Onion, dates, reed matting | Peeled, stripped and unravelled in *Šurpu* |
| Salt | Dissolves: "what worked witchcraft on me, may it dissolve like salt" |
| Oil, ghee, honey, beer, malt | Anointing, mouth-washing, the toothache cure |
| Magnetic iron ore | Powdered into oil for the potency rites |
| River water | Carries evil away, "down to the Apsû" |
| Animal fats and parts (snake, lion and crab tallow; a frog; dog, cat and fox hair; a grasshopper's wing; goose marrow) | The eye paste for seeing a ghost (Chapter IV) |
| Potsherd from a ruin mound | Crushed in water and sprinkled to keep a ghost's evil from the house (Chapter IV) |

Some ingredients were chosen for what they meant, not for what they were: earth from a tavern wall (for a dog bite) or donkey urine poured for an unwelcome ghost.

## Appendix C · Sources, editions and a warning

Every entry rests on a published scholarly edition; the renderings in this book are new, but the texts are not. Use the editions below to check any line.

### A warning about modern "ancient" spellbooks

- **Most modern spellcraft is recent.** It descends mainly from 19th- and 20th-century revivals, such as the Hermetic Order of the Golden Dawn (founded 1888) and Gerald Gardner's Wicca (made public in the 1950s). Those drew on Renaissance grimoires, Freemasonry, Kabbalah and the Greek magical papyri, with Celtic festival names added to a largely modern ritual calendar.
- **Genuine Celtic magic barely survives in Celtic words.** The main pieces are lead tablets from Roman Gaul, written in Latin letters. The [Larzac tablet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Larzac_tablet), found in 1983 and dated about 100 CE, is the longest text in Gaulish: a curse involving a circle of women around one Severa Tertionicna. It still cannot be fully translated.
- **The "Necronomicon" is not Sumerian.** The so-called [Simon Necronomicon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simon_Necronomicon) (1977; Avon paperback 1980) presents itself as a Mesopotamian grimoire. It is a modern compilation mixing real Sumerian and Babylonian names with H. P. Lovecraft's fiction and Aleister Crowley's magic. It is commonly attributed to Peter Levenda, who denies writing it, and is widely regarded as a [hoax](https://isaw.nyu.edu/library/blog/necronomicon). Nothing in it is a translation of a known tablet.
