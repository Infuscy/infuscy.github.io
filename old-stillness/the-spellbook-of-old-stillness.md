# The Spellbook of Old Stillness

Nov 24, 2026 · @Florin

Thirty-four meditation techniques, each traced to a named text or teacher from before 1800. They come from seven traditions: China, Japan, Tibet, India, Greece and Rome, the Christian desert and cloister, and the Sufis.

## How to read this book

A spell gets into this book only if its method is written down in a source older than 1800. Where the date or the author is uncertain, its seal says so.

**The rule of inclusion**

- The method appears in a text written before 1800, or in the recorded words of a teacher who lived before then. Two centuries back from today is 1826; 1800 leaves a margin.
- The steps come from the source. Where later teachers added steps, images or claims, the entry flags them.
- Practices that their own traditions reserve for initiates are named but not taught (the Locked Spells, end of Chapter III).
- Methods are paraphrased from the sources, not quoted at length.

**Anatomy of a spell**

- **Source:** the text, its author and its date.
- **Seal:** how firm that provenance is (table below).
- **Casting:** the method, as the source gives it.
- **Promised effect:** what the tradition says the practice does. Reported here, not endorsed or tested.
- **Caution:** where the source itself, or plain sense, calls for one.

| Seal | What it means |
| --- | --- |
| Gold | Known author, secure date, and the method is spelled out in their own text |
| Silver | The method is clearly in the text, but the date spans centuries or the author is traditional rather than proven |
| Bronze | The source names or sketches the practice; the detailed steps come from later (still pre-1800) commentary or a living lineage |

Dates: "c." means circa. A range such as "4th–3rd c. BCE" means scholars disagree within that window. For teachings first passed on by memory, such as the Pali Canon, both the teaching's traditional date and the writing-down date are given.

## Before you cast

The old manuals were the first to warn that meditation can make people ill. Modern trials support modest benefits, and almost none have tested the specific methods in this book.

**What the traditions themselves recorded**

- Zhiyi's *Concise Śamatha and Vipaśyanā* (6th c.) gives a full chapter to illness, including illness caused by meditating unskilfully, and treats it with breath and attention methods ([Salguero's translation](https://buddhistuniversity.net/content/articles/treating-illness-translation-of-chapter_salguero-p)).
- Hakuin wrote *Yasenkanna* (1757) about his own "Zen sickness": a burning head and lungs, legs as cold as ice. His cure is Spells 9 and 10 ([Waddell's translation](https://otani.repo.nii.ac.jp/record/8339/files/EBNVol.34No.1_OCR_05.pdf)).
- Tibetan teachers call unusual meditation experiences *nyams*; Zen calls perceptual disturbances *makyō*. Both treat them as things to pass through, not prizes.

**What modern research shows**

- A 2014 review of randomized trials in *JAMA Internal Medicine* found moderate evidence that mindfulness programs bring small improvements in anxiety, depression and pain. Evidence for stress, mood, attention and sleep was low or insufficient, and there was no evidence that any program beat active treatments such as drugs or exercise ([Goyal et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24395196/)).
- Those trials tested modern programs, mostly mindfulness courses; mantra programs showed no clear effect. For most spells here no controlled study exists, so their effects are the tradition's claims, not findings.
- A 2017 study interviewed nearly 100 Western Buddhist meditators and teachers and catalogued 59 kinds of meditation-related experience. Many were challenging, such as hypersensitivity to light or sound, and some persisted after retreats and got in the way of work ([Lindahl et al.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5443484)). It did not measure how common they are.

**Plain rules for casting**

- Start short, ten to twenty minutes, and lengthen slowly.
- Never force or hold the breath. The old manuals themselves ask for breath that is quiet and unforced.
- With a history of psychosis, dissociation, trauma or severe depression, talk to a clinician before long or intensive practice.
- If anxiety, insomnia, a sense of unreality or strange perceptions persist after sitting, stop and get help. Meditators in the 2017 study said that simply talking to someone who knew these experiences helped.

## The Foundation: the seat

Three of the oldest manuals agree on almost everything: a quiet place, a firm seat, a straight spine, nose over navel, and breath that makes no sound. They disagree about the eyes.

|  | Zhiyi, *Concise Śamatha-Vipaśyanā* (6th c., China) | Kamalaśīla, *Stages of Meditation* (late 8th c., India/Tibet) | Dōgen, *Universal Recommendation for Zazen* (1227, Japan) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Before sitting | Moderate food and sleep; loosen your clothes | A very soft, comfortable seat | A quiet room; moderate food and drink; set all affairs aside |
| Legs | Half or full cross-legged | Full or half lotus | Full or half lotus, on a round cushion over a thick mat |
| Hands | Left palm over the right, resting close to the body | Not among his eight points | Right hand on the left foot, left palm on the right, thumb tips touching |
| Spine and head | Straight, neither hunched nor stiff; nose over navel | Straight, neither leaning back nor bent; shoulders level; nose over navel | Upright, leaning to no side; ears over shoulders, nose over navel |
| Eyes | Closed, just enough to shut out the light | Neither open nor shut, resting toward the tip of the nose | Always open |
| Mouth | Lips and teeth lightly together, tongue to the palate | Teeth and lips natural, tongue behind the upper teeth | Lips and teeth closed, tongue against the palate |
| Breath | Silent, unbroken and fine: not audible ("wind"), stuttering ("panting") or coarse ("qi") | Not noisy, forced or agitated; soft and effortless | Gentle, through the nose |

Zhiyi and Dōgen also give an entry and an exit. Before settling, sway the body left and right a few times; Zhiyi adds breathing stale air out through the mouth, up to three times. Afterwards, rise slowly. Zhiyi's exit is the most careful: move the shoulders, hands and neck, then the feet, rub the palms warm and cover the eyes with them, and get up only once the body has cooled.

Where the manuals disagree, on the eyes, choose one way and keep it. A low stool is also old: the Hesychast Gregory of Sinai (d. 1346) had beginners sit on one about a span (some 20 cm) high (Spell 28). The spells that follow assume this seat unless they say otherwise.

Sources: [Kamalaśīla's eight points](https://www.tibetanbuddhistencyclopedia.com/en/wiki/The_sources), [Zhiyi's four kinds of breath](https://ianburgess.me.uk/?p=7370), [Gregory of Sinai's span-high seat](https://ejournals.epublishing.ekt.gr/index.php/bz/article/download/36325/30184/144404).

## Chapter I — China

China's oldest meditation text is about 2,350 years old. Its first instruction, align the body and let the breath settle, runs through every Chinese method after it.

### 1. Inward Training (*Neiye* 內業)

**Source:** *Neiye*, anonymous rhymed verses preserved inside the *Guanzi* compilation; dated to c. 350 BCE by [Harold Roth](https://mitpressbookstore.mit.edu/book/9780231115650). · **Seal:** Silver

**Casting**

1. Align the body: sit square and upright, limbs settled. The text warns that inner power will not come to an unaligned body.
2. Let the breath become calm and regular. Then relax it and let it expand.
3. Let the mind grow tranquil, then enlarge it and let it go.
4. Keep the body calm and unmoving, hold to the One, and let the myriad disturbances drop away.

**Promised effect:** the vital essence (*jing*) collects in a tranquil mind, bringing clear perception, a sound body and long life.

### 2. Fasting of the Heart-Mind (*xinzhai* 心齋)

**Source:** *Zhuangzi*, chapter 4, one of the "inner chapters" (4th–3rd c. BCE), where Confucius instructs his disciple Yan Hui. · **Seal:** Silver

**Casting**

1. Unify your intent.
2. Stop listening with the ears; listen with the heart-mind.
3. Stop listening with the heart-mind; listen with the *qi*, the breath-energy. The ears stop at sound and the mind stops at matching things to ideas, but *qi* is empty and waits on everything.
4. Rest in that emptiness. The text's own definition: the Way gathers only in emptiness, and emptiness is the fasting of the heart-mind.

**Promised effect:** Yan Hui can then move among the powerful, even a tyrant, without harming or being harmed.

**Note:** It is a teaching story, not a session manual. "Listening with the *qi*" is often taught today as listening to the breath; *qi* can mean breath or vital energy.

### 3. Sitting and Forgetting (*zuowang* 坐忘)

**Source:** *Zhuangzi*, chapter 6 (4th–3rd c. BCE), turned into a seven-step course by the Daoist patriarch Sima Chengzhen (647–735) in his *Zuowang lun* (Essay on Sitting in Oblivion). · **Seal:** Gold (Sima's text)

In the *Zhuangzi*, Yan Hui lets limbs and body fall away, dismisses perception and knowledge, and merges with "the Great Thoroughfare". Sima's seven steps run: respect and faith; cutting off entanglements; taming the mind; detachment from affairs; true observation; deep stability; attaining the Dao ([Kohn's study](https://asianethnology.org/article/1191/download)).

**Casting** (the heart of "taming the mind")

1. Sit quietly. Gather the mind away from outer objects, and attach to nothing.
2. When a thought arises, let it go at once. When the mind runs off, bring it back gently.
3. Don't crush every stirring blindly: Sima warns that this produces a dull, sightless stillness. Extinguish the agitated mind, never the mind that sees.
4. Carry the same stillness into daily affairs.

**Promised effect:** union with the Dao, which Sima describes as transforming the body as well as the mind.

### 4. The Six Healing Breaths (*liuzijue* 六字訣)

**Source:** Tao Hongjing (456–536), Daoist physician of the Shangqing school, in *Yangxing yanming lu* (On Nourishing Life and Extending the Years); the Buddhist master Zhiyi used the same six breaths a generation later. · **Seal:** Silver

**Casting**

1. Breathe in quietly through the nose. Tao's rule: one way to breathe in, six ways to breathe out.
2. Breathe out slowly through the mouth, shaping one of six sounds: *chui*, *hu*, *xi*, *he*, *xu* or *si*.
3. In Zhiyi's version, imagine the sound circling inside the mouth and lips, and make it softly. Use *chui* when you feel cold and *hu* when you feel hot.
4. Do a few rounds, then return to quiet breathing.

**Promised effect:** each sound clears a particular disorder. In Zhiyi, *chui* removes cold, *hu* heat, and *xi* pain and "wind" ([Zhiyi, via Salguero](https://www.buddhistdoor.net/?p=2804)).

**Note:** The arm and body movements of today's qigong version came later; they were added only in the Ming dynasty, after 1368 ([summary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liu_Zi_Jue)). The early texts give breath and sound alone.

### 5. The Six Wondrous Gates (*liu miao famen* 六妙法門)

**Source:** Zhiyi (538–597), founder of the Tiantai school, in *The Six Wondrous Dharma Gates* and his *Concise Śamatha-Vipaśyanā*. · **Seal:** Gold

**Casting**

1. **Counting.** Settle the breath, neither rough nor slippery, and count breaths slowly from one to ten. If you lose count, start again at one.
2. **Following.** When counting is steady, drop the numbers and simply accompany each breath in and out.
3. **Stilling.** Let even the following stop; the mind rests, quiet and unmoving.
4. **Observing.** Look closely at breath, body and mind, and watch them arise and pass.
5. **Returning.** Turn the looking back on the looker, and find no fixed self doing it.
6. **Purifying.** Rest without grasping anything, not even stillness or insight.

**Promised effect:** the first three gates bring concentration and the last three insight; together, Zhiyi says, they lead to awakening.

**Note:** Counting breaths is not in the Buddha's own breath sermon (Spell 15). It enters through later manuals like this one and the *Visuddhimagga*.

### 6. Silent Illumination (*mozhao* 默照)

**Source:** Hongzhi Zhengjue (1091–1157), Caodong Chan master, in his *Inscription on Silent Illumination* and practice instructions. · **Seal:** Gold

**Casting**

1. Sit still with nothing to attain and nothing to fix on.
2. Let thoughts and objects come and go without grasping them or pushing them away. That letting-be is the "silence".
3. Keep awareness bright and open within the silence. That clarity is the "illumination".
4. Keep the two together. Hongzhi's own warning: illumination that loses silence turns aggressive, and silence that loses illumination turns murky ([Leighton's translation](https://upaya.org/2014/10/guidepost-silent-illumination-hongzhi-zhengjue)).

**Promised effect:** the mind's own clarity shows itself without being produced. Hongzhi's image for it is a field, empty and boundless.

**Note:** His contemporary Dahui attacked "silent illumination" as dead sitting, and the quarrel shaped Zen for centuries ([overview](https://encyclopedia.com/religion/encyclopedias-almanacs-transcripts-and-maps/mozhao-chan-silent-illumination-chan)).

### 7. The Critical Phrase (*kanhua* 看話)

**Source:** Dahui Zonggao (1089–1163), Linji Chan master, in his letters to lay students, many of them government officials. · **Seal:** Gold

**Casting**

1. Take the case: a monk asked Zhaozhou whether a dog has Buddha-nature, and Zhaozhou said "*Wu*!" ("No!"). Keep just the word *Wu*.
2. Hold it with doubt: don't think about its meaning, but press on the question itself.
3. Raise it at all times, walking, sitting, at work, in anger or in joy, without a gap.
4. Avoid the errors Dahui lists: reading it as yes or no, reasoning it out, taking it as mere nothing, settling into calm, waiting for enlightenment ([Dahui's letters](https://philpapers.org/rec/ZONTLO)).

**Promised effect:** when the doubt finally breaks, awakening comes all at once.

**Caution:** Hakuin's "Zen sickness" (Spells 9 and 10) followed years of this kind of pressure. Balance it with rest.

## Chapter II — Japan

Japan's spells run from Kakuban's 12th-century manual of the letter A to Hakuin's soft butter, published in 1757 and the youngest spell in the book.

### 8. Just Sitting (*shikantaza* 只管打坐)

**Source:** Dōgen (1200–1253), founder of Japanese Sōtō Zen, *Fukanzazengi* (Universal Recommendation for Zazen), first written in 1227 and revised later. · **Seal:** Gold

**Casting**

1. Find a quiet room. Eat and drink in moderation. Put all affairs aside; don't judge thoughts as good or bad, and don't try to become a Buddha.
2. Take the seat from the Foundation, eyes open.
3. Take one deep breath, sway left and right, then settle into steady stillness.
4. "Think of not-thinking. How? Non-thinking." When a thought arises, notice it; once noticed, it goes. Keep sitting.
5. To end, rise slowly and calmly, never abruptly.

**Promised effect:** none, by design. Dōgen says zazen is not a technique for learning meditation but the dharma gate of ease and joy in itself.

**Note:** The "not-thinking, non-thinking" exchange comes from the Chinese master Yaoshan (8th–9th c.), whom Dōgen quotes.

### 9. The Soft-Butter Method (*nanso no hō* 軟酥の法)

**Source:** Hakuin Ekaku (1686–1769), reviver of Rinzai Zen, *Yasenkanna* (Idle Talk on a Night Boat), published 1757. Hakuin presents it as a hermit's teaching that cured his own Zen sickness ([Waddell's translation](https://otani.repo.nii.ac.jp/record/8339/files/EBNVol.34No.1_OCR_05.pdf)). · **Seal:** Gold for Hakuin's text; the hermit story may be literary

**Casting**

1. Sit or lie still and quiet the breath.
2. Imagine a lump of soft butter, pure in colour and fragrance, the size and shape of a duck's egg, set on the crown of your head.
3. Feel it begin to melt, soaking your head inside and out with a delicious, soothing feeling.
4. Let it flow down over the shoulders, arms and chest, through the lungs, liver, stomach and intestines, and down the spine into the hips.
5. As it flows, feel every knot of tension and pain dissolve and drain downward, like water.
6. Let it pool warmly in the lower body and soak the legs down to the soles of the feet.
7. Repeat the whole descent.

**Promised effect:** the "heart-fire" that rose to the head sinks, the legs warm, and body and mind settle. His monks, Hakuin says, copied the method eagerly and recovered.

**Note:** It works much like a modern "body scan", and is about two centuries older.

### 10. Introspection in the Lower Belly (*naikan* 内観)

**Source:** Hakuin, *Yasenkanna* (1757), the method he says he learned alongside the butter. · **Seal:** Gold

**Casting**

1. At bedtime, lie on your back. Stretch both legs out long and push firmly through the feet.
2. Let breath and attention sink below the navel: into the "field of elixir" (*tanden*), the hips, the legs and the soles of the feet.
3. Filling that region with *ki*, repeat four contemplations to yourself:
   - This ocean of *ki* below my navel, down to my soles, is my original face. What nostrils could it have?
   - It is my true home. What news could come from home?
   - It is the Pure Land of my own mind. What splendour could it need?
   - It is the Amida Buddha of my own body. What sermon could he preach?
4. Keep repeating until the lower body feels full, firm and warm.

**Promised effect:** heat drains from the head and the feet grow warm. Hakuin guarantees the method will cure any kind of Zen sickness, given steady practice.

### 11. Contemplation of the Letter A (*ajikan* 阿字観)

**Source:** Shingon Buddhism. Tradition credits Kūkai (774–835), who brought Shingon from China in the early 9th century; the oldest detailed manual is the *Ajikan* of Kakuban (1095–1143) ([Green's study](https://www.themathesontrust.org/papers/buddhism/Shingon_Ajikan_Meditation.pdf)). · **Seal:** Silver

**Casting**

1. Hang the scroll at eye level: the Sanskrit letter A, in Siddham script, inside a white full-moon disc resting on a lotus.
2. Take the seat and calm the breath. Shingon practice often begins by counting breaths.
3. Gaze at the A in its moon. Chant "A" slowly, aloud or silently, on the out-breath.
4. Close the eyes and bring the moon disc, with its A, into your chest.
5. Expand it slowly until it fills the room, the land and the whole universe, and rest there.
6. Draw it back slowly to its first size in your chest, then open your eyes.

**Promised effect:** in Shingon teaching, A is the unborn source of everything. Kūkai held that through this practice one can become a Buddha in this very body.

**Note:** For centuries the method was passed on inside Shingon temples by priests. Some temples now teach it to visitors ([a temple's guide](https://hirosaki-kanko.or.jp/individual/en_kudoji/ajikan_what.pdf)).

## Chapter III — Tibet

Tibet's openly taught methods come from an 8th-century Indian master, a 12th-century set of slogans and a 16th-century manual. Its most famous practices are kept locked by its own lineages.

### 12. Calm Abiding on an Image (*śamatha*, Tibetan *zhiné*)

**Source:** Kamalaśīla (c. 740–795), *Stages of Meditation* (*Bhāvanākrama*), written in Tibet after the Samye debate of c. 792–794; the nine stages come from earlier Indian works by Asaṅga and the Maitreya texts (4th–5th c.). · **Seal:** Gold

**Casting**

1. Take the eight-point seat (Foundation).
2. Choose an object. Kamalaśīla suggests an image of the Buddha: look at a statue or painting, then hold its form in mind.
3. Place the mind on it. Each time it wanders, notice and bring it back.
4. Watch for two faults. In laxity the image dims and the mind sinks; in excitement the mind jumps to something pleasant. Lift a sinking mind by recalling something inspiring, and calm an excited one by recalling impermanence.
5. When neither fault is present, ease off the effort and let the mind rest evenly.

The nine stages, as the tradition counts them: placing the mind; placing it continuously; re-placing it; placing it closely; taming; pacifying; fully pacifying; one-pointedness; resting in balance.

**Promised effect:** "pliancy", a lightness that makes body and mind workable, and the base for insight meditation.

### 13. Giving and Taking (*tonglen*)

**Source:** *Seven Points of Mind Training*, slogans written down by Chekawa Yeshe Dorje (1101–1175) from the teaching that Atiśa (982–1054) brought to Tibet; his disciple Sechilbuwa (1121–1189) arranged them into seven points ([overview](https://encyclopediaofbuddhism.org/wiki/Seven-Point_Mind_Training)). · **Seal:** Gold

The root verses are terse: train in giving and taking alternately, mount the two on the breath, and begin the taking with yourself ([Lotsawa House translation](https://www.lotsawahouse.org/tibetan-masters/geshe-chekhawa-yeshe-dorje/seven-points-mind-training)).

**Casting**

1. Begin with yourself. On the in-breath, take on your own coming suffering; on the out-breath, send yourself ease.
2. Move to someone you love. Breathe in their pain; breathe out your happiness and good fortune to them.
3. Then a stranger, then someone you dislike, then all beings.
4. Keep the exchange riding the breath: taking on the in-breath, giving on the out-breath.
5. Between sessions, use the slogan "three objects, three poisons". When craving, anger or indifference arises toward a friend, an enemy or a stranger, take that poison on yourself for everyone who suffers from it.

**Promised effect:** self-cherishing weakens, compassion grows, and adversity becomes part of the path.

**Note:** Popular instructions picture suffering breathed in as dark smoke and happiness breathed out as light. The root verses give only the alternation and the breath.

### 14. Searching for the Mind (*Mahāmudrā*)

**Source:** Wangchuk Dorje, 9th Karmapa (1556–1603), *Mahāmudrā: Eliminating the Darkness of Ignorance* and *Ocean of Definitive Meaning*; the method goes back through the 3rd Karmapa (1284–1339) to Gampopa (1079–1153). · **Seal:** Gold

**Casting** (after some calm abiding)

1. Turn attention on the mind itself. Does it have a shape? A colour? Where is it: inside the body, outside it, or somewhere between?
2. Let a thought arise. Ask where it came from, where it stays while present, and where it goes when it ends.
3. Look at the one who is looking.
4. When you find no shape, colour or place, don't conclude "nothing". Rest in the not-finding: vivid, open and aware, without making anything up.

**Promised effect:** recognition of the mind's nature. The 9th Karmapa says thoughts are then seen to arise and dissolve at once, like a picture drawn on water.

**Caution:** Every Mahāmudrā lineage treats a qualified teacher's "pointing out" as essential. This is the preliminary search, not the whole path.

### The Locked Spells

The Tibetan traditions restrict these practices to students with an empowerment and a teacher. They are named here so you can recognise them in books, and are not taught.

| Practice | Source | Why it stays locked |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Inner heat (*tummo*) | Six Yogas of Nāropa, 11th c. | Requires empowerment; relies on breath retention that lineages teach only in person |
| Severance (*chöd*) | Machig Labdrön, 11th–12th c. | A ritual practice requiring empowerment, done deliberately in frightening places |
| Transference of consciousness (*phowa*) | Six Yogas of Nāropa, 11th c. | A practice for the moment of death, given only under transmission |
| Deity yoga | The Buddhist tantras | The tradition's own vows forbid it without empowerment |
| Direct introduction (Dzogchen *trekchö*) | Nyingma school; systematised by Longchenpa (1308–1364) | Depends on a teacher introducing the student directly to the nature of mind |

A book or app that teaches these step by step, without a teacher, is breaking the rules of the tradition it claims.

## Chapter IV — India

India's oldest instructions, the Buddha's breath and death meditations, were memorised for centuries and written down in Sri Lanka in the 1st century BCE.

### 15. Mindfulness of Breathing (*ānāpānasati*)

**Source:** *Ānāpānasati Sutta* (Middle Length Discourses 118), Pali Canon; a teaching attributed to the Buddha (5th c. BCE), written down c. 1st c. BCE. · **Seal:** Silver

**Casting**

1. Go to a forest, the foot of a tree or an empty room. Sit cross-legged, body upright, and set mindfulness to the fore.
2. Breathing in long, know that you breathe in long; breathing out long, know it. Breathing short, know that too.
3. Train yourself: "I will breathe in and out experiencing the whole body."
4. Train yourself: "I will breathe in and out calming the bodily process", which is the breath itself.

These are the first four of sixteen steps. The other twelve run in three sets of four: experiencing rapture, pleasure and the mental process, then calming it; experiencing the mind, gladdening, steadying and freeing it; contemplating impermanence, fading, cessation and letting go.

**Promised effect:** developed fully, the sutta says, this one practice completes the four foundations of mindfulness, the seven factors of awakening, and liberation.

### 16. Recollection of Death (*maraṇasati*)

**Source:** *Maraṇassati Sutta* (Numbered Discourses 6.19 and 6.20), Pali Canon; expanded by Buddhaghosa in the *Visuddhimagga*, chapter 8 (5th c. CE). · **Seal:** Silver

**Casting**

1. Sit and settle the breath.
2. Ask how long you can count on living. In the sutta, monks answer: a day and a night; a day; a meal; four or five mouthfuls; one mouthful; one breath. The Buddha praises only the last two ([AN 6.19](https://www.dhammatalks.org/suttas/AN/AN6_19.html)).
3. So keep death as near as the next mouthful or the next breath, and ask what is worth doing with it.
4. In the evening (AN 6.20), consider the many ways death could come tonight: a snakebite, a fall, food gone bad, a sudden illness. Ask whether any harmful habit is still unabandoned. If so, work at it as urgently as someone whose turban is on fire.
5. Buddhaghosa adds eight reflections: death as an executioner with a raised sword; as the ruin of all success; by comparison with the great, who also died; as sharing the body with many creatures; by the frailty of life; by its having no fixed time; by the limit of the life span; and by the shortness of each moment.

**Promised effect:** in the sutta's words, it plunges into the Deathless. In practice: urgency, diligence and less clinging.

**Caution:** Skip this one during grief or depression, and choose loving-kindness instead.

### 17. Loving-Kindness (*mettā*)

**Source:** *Karaṇīya Mettā Sutta* (Sutta Nipāta 1.8) for the attitude; the person-by-person sequence is Buddhaghosa's, *Visuddhimagga* chapter 9, 5th c. CE ([chapter text](https://www.wisdomlib.org/buddhism/book/visuddhimagga-the-pah-of-purification/d/doc1085079.html)). · **Seal:** Gold

**Casting**

1. Begin with yourself, repeating: may I be happy and free from suffering; may I be free of hostility, trouble and anxiety, and live at ease.
2. Then a person you respect and feel warmly toward, such as a teacher.
3. Then a very dear friend, then a neutral person, then a hostile one.
4. Buddhaghosa's warnings: don't start with a hostile, very dear or neutral person. Don't begin by aiming it at someone of the opposite sex, since, he says, it may turn into desire. Don't aim it at the dead, which he says goes nowhere.
5. If anger rises at the hostile person, go back to an easier one, then try again.
6. "Break the barriers": continue until your kindness is equal for all four, yourself included. His test: if bandits demanded one of the four for a sacrifice, you could not pick anyone.
7. Then extend it in every direction, to all beings.

**Promised effect:** the Pali Canon lists eleven benefits, among them easy sleep, no bad dreams, a serene face and an unconfused death.

### 18. The Earth Disc (*pathavī kasiṇa*)

**Source:** Buddhaghosa, *Visuddhimagga* chapter 4 (5th c. CE); the kasiṇas themselves are listed in the Pali Canon ([chapter text](https://www.wisdomlib.org/buddhism/book/visuddhimagga-the-pah-of-purification/d/doc1085031.html)). · **Seal:** Gold

**Casting**

1. Make the disc. On a frame, spread clean clay the colour of dawn, a light reddish brown, in a circle a span and four fingers across (about 30 cm). Blue, yellow, red or white clay are faults: those colours belong to other kasiṇas.
2. Sit on a seat a span and four fingers high, two and a half cubits (a little over a metre) from the disc.
3. Look with the eyes moderately open, the way you look at your face in a mirror. Don't dwell on the colour or the texture; take it as "earth".
4. Repeat mentally "earth, earth", alternating eyes open and eyes closed.
5. When the disc appears as clearly with eyes closed as open, you have the "learning sign". Go back to your own seat and keep developing it there.
6. In time a "counterpart sign" arises, brighter and smoother, like a mirror drawn from its case or the moon coming out of cloud. Guard it, then extend it step by step: a finger's width, a span, the room, the village, the world.

**Promised effect:** the meditative absorptions (*jhāna*). Buddhaghosa also ties mastery of the kasiṇas to supernormal powers.

### 19. The Sacred Syllable (*Om*)

**Source:** Patañjali, *Yoga Sūtra* 1.27–29, part of the *Pātañjalayogaśāstra*, dated c. 325–425 CE by Philipp Maas ([review](https://law-journals-books.vlex.com/vid/differentiating-yoga-tantra-sanskrit-228312558)); and the *Māṇḍūkya Upaniṣad* (early centuries CE, dating debated). · **Seal:** Silver

**Casting**

1. Take the seat.
2. Repeat Om slowly: aloud at first, then in a whisper, then only in the mind. The Yoga Sūtra's instruction has two parts: repetition (*japa*) and contemplation of its meaning (*bhāvanā*).
3. With each repetition, hold what it stands for. For Patañjali it names Īśvara, the Lord untouched by affliction; for the Māṇḍūkya, A is waking, U is dreaming and M is deep sleep.
4. Rest in the silence after the M. The Māṇḍūkya calls it "the fourth" (*turīya*), beyond the other three.

**Promised effect:** in Yoga Sūtra 1.29, the mind turns inward and the obstacles to practice, from illness and doubt to laziness, fall away.

### 20. Doorways in the Gaps (*Vijñāna Bhairava* dhāraṇās)

**Source:** *Vijñāna Bhairava Tantra*, Kashmir, c. 7th–9th c. CE: 112 short methods in 163 Sanskrit verses, framed as the god Bhairava's answer to the Goddess. · **Seal:** Silver

**Casting** (choose one per sitting)

1. **The turn of the breath** (verses 24–25). The out-breath rises and the in-breath descends. Rest attention at the two points where each one turns, in the pause ([commentary](https://hareesh.org/blog/2022/11/30/vijana-bhairava-tantra-verses-22-27-the-foundational-practice)).
2. **Sound fading.** Listen to a stringed instrument and follow each note as it dies away into silence.
3. **The long syllable.** Intone Om slowly; as the sound fades, dissolve into the soundlessness.
4. **Between two thoughts.** Rest attention in the gap after one thought ends and before the next begins.
5. **The edge of sleep.** As you fall asleep, notice the moment when outer awareness has gone but sleep has not yet come.
6. **Sudden moments.** At the start of a sneeze, in a jolt of fear or a burst of curiosity, the mind stops for an instant. Notice that stop.

**Promised effect:** each gap opens onto unbounded consciousness, which the text calls Bhairava.

**Note:** Many "112 meditations" books are loose poetic rewrites, starting with Paul Reps's 1957 version. For the text itself, use Jaideva Singh's translation (1979) or Christopher Wallis's.

### 21. Steady Gazing (*trāṭaka*)

**Source:** Svātmārāma, *Haṭha Yoga Pradīpikā* 2.31–32 (15th c.), where it is one of six cleansing actions. · **Seal:** Silver (the author is known by name only)

**Casting**

1. Choose a small mark. The text says only "a small target"; a dot on the wall will do.
2. Gaze at it with a still mind and without blinking.
3. Continue until tears come, then close the eyes and rest.

**Promised effect:** the text says it removes eye diseases and sloth, and should be guarded as carefully as a casket of gold.

**Note:** The candle flame, now the standard object, is a later choice; the *Pradīpikā* names only a small mark ([summary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tr%C4%81%E1%B9%ADaka)).

**Caution:** Never strain; stop if your eyes hurt. With an eye condition, ask an eye doctor first.

### 22. Listening to the Inner Sound (*nādānusandhāna*)

**Source:** *Haṭha Yoga Pradīpikā* chapter 4 (15th c.), drawing on older yoga texts such as the *Nādabindu Upaniṣad* (c. 100 BCE–300 CE) ([dating and summary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nadabindu_Upanishad)). · **Seal:** Silver

**Casting**

1. Sit, and close the ears with the hands.
2. Listen inward, to the sound in the right ear, with a concentrated mind.
3. The texts describe a sequence: first like the ocean, thunder, a kettledrum or a waterfall; later like drums and bells; finally like tinkling bells, a flute, a lute and a bee.
4. Move attention to the subtlest sound you can hear, and let the mind dissolve into it.

**Promised effect:** the mind dissolves into the sound (*laya*), leading to absorption (*samādhi*).

**Caution:** Constant ringing in the ears can also be tinnitus. If inner sounds grow loud or distressing, stop and see a doctor.

## Chapter V — Greece and Rome

The Greek and Roman schools trained the mind with daily exercises, which the historian Pierre Hadot called "spiritual exercises". Four of them can be cast straight from the texts.

### 23. The Evening Review

**Source:** The Pythagorean *Golden Verses*, lines 40–44; their date is unknown, but Epictetus quotes them around 108 CE ([text](https://satyori.com/source-texts/golden-verses-of-pythagoras/12/)). Seneca, *On Anger* 3.36 (c. 40s–50s CE), describes the same practice in the philosopher Quintus Sextius and in himself. · **Seal:** Silver for the *Golden Verses*, Gold for Seneca

**Casting**

1. Lie down for the night. Seneca does it once the lamp is out and his wife, who knows the habit, has fallen silent.
2. Before sleep, go back over each of the day's deeds three times (*Golden Verses*), hiding nothing from yourself (Seneca).
3. Ask the Pythagorean questions: Where did I go wrong? What did I do? What duty did I leave undone?
4. Or ask Sextius's: Which bad habit did I cure today? Which fault did I resist? In what am I better?
5. Reprove yourself for the bad and take joy in the good.

**Promised effect:** Seneca says anger cools once it knows it must face this court every night, and the sleep that follows is calm and deep.

### 24. The Morning Rehearsal

**Source:** Marcus Aurelius, *Meditations* 2.1, private notes written c. 170–180 CE ([George Long's translation](https://standardebooks.org/ebooks/marcus-aurelius/meditations/george-long/text/book-2)). · **Seal:** Gold

**Casting**

1. On waking, tell yourself whom you will meet today: the meddling, the ungrateful, the arrogant, the deceitful, the envious, the unsociable.
2. Remind yourself why they act so: they cannot tell good from evil.
3. Remind yourself of what you know. None of them can stain your character but you; they are your kin, sharing the same reason; and people are made to work together, like feet, hands, eyelids or the two rows of teeth.
4. Decide in advance not to be angry with them or turn away from them.

**Promised effect:** you meet other people's faults without being thrown or turning hostile.

**Note:** The related "pre-rehearsal of evils" (*praemeditatio malorum*) runs through Seneca's letters: picture losses in advance, so that none strikes you unprepared.

### 25. The View from Above

**Source:** Cicero, *The Dream of Scipio*, the close of *On the Republic* (c. 51 BCE); Marcus Aurelius, *Meditations* 7.48, 9.30 and 12.24 (c. 170–180 CE). · **Seal:** Gold

**Casting**

1. Close your eyes and imagine rising above where you sit: the room, the street, the city.
2. Keep rising until whole countries spread below: crowds, armies, farms, weddings and funerals, markets, births and deaths, all at once. Marcus asks the reader to look down on the countless herds of humanity in just this way.
3. Rise until the Earth is a speck. Cicero's Scipio, looking down from among the stars, finds Rome's empire so small that he is ashamed of it.
4. Widen time as well: the generations before you and after, and how quickly any fame is forgotten.
5. Come back down slowly to your own body and your own day, keeping that scale.

**Promised effect:** small things shrink, anxieties and ambitions lose their grip, and the soul feels its kinship with the whole.

**Note:** The name "the view from above" is Pierre Hadot's, from the 20th century; the exercise itself is ancient.

### 26. The Luminous Sphere

**Source:** Plotinus (c. 204–270), *Enneads* V.8.9, "On the Intelligible Beauty", written in Rome in the 260s CE ([MacKenna's translation](https://www.ellopos.net/elpenor/greek-texts/ancient-Greece/plotinus/enneads-5.asp?pg=98)). · **Seal:** Gold

**Casting**

1. Picture the whole visible cosmos as a single sphere. Each thing stays distinct in its place, yet all are seen together: sun, stars, earth, sea and living things showing through one another, as on a transparent globe.
2. Hold this gleaming image steadily before the mind.
3. Now imagine a second sphere drawn from it, stripped of size and of all spatial distance. Don't just shrink it: remove the sense of matter altogether.
4. Call on the god who made the sphere you are holding, and ask him to come, bringing his own world with him.

**Promised effect:** the divine arrives as one and many at once, filling the sphere, and the soul passes from the image of the cosmos to the intelligible world it reflects.

**Note:** Plotinus wrote no manual, and this is one of his few step-by-step exercises; scholars read it as a guided practice ([Stróżyński, 2024](https://cambridge.org/core/books/plotinus-on-the-contemplation-of-the-intelligible-world/luminous-sphere/AF6F74833910D2A556FF8B2B4D4F7DFC)).

## Chapter VI — The Desert and the Cloister

Christian contemplatives built their methods on one short prayer repeated without end, a practice first written down by John Cassian in the 420s.

### 27. The Formula of Abba Isaac

**Source:** John Cassian, *Conferences* 10.10 (c. 420s CE), reporting the teaching of the Egyptian desert monk Abba Isaac ([text](https://www.ccel.org/ccel/cassian/conferences.ii.xi.x.html)). · **Seal:** Gold

**Casting**

1. Take one verse: "O God, come to my assistance; O Lord, make haste to help me" (Psalm 70:1, or 69:2 in the old Latin numbering; Douay-Rheims translation).
2. Repeat it unceasingly and inwardly, in every state: temptation, boredom, illness, sleeplessness, distraction, and joy too.
3. Whenever other thoughts arrive, return to the verse, until it holds the whole mind.

**Promised effect:** continual remembrance of God, a heart guarded against every passion, and the "poverty of spirit" of the Beatitudes.

**Note:** St Benedict made this verse the opening of the monastic Office, where it is still sung every day ([WCCM](https://wccm.org/weekly-teachings/cassian)).

### 28. The Prayer of the Heart (Hesychast method)

**Source:** *The Method of Holy Prayer and Attention*, long credited to Symeon the New Theologian (d. 1022) but now usually dated later; Nicephorus the Monk (13th c.); Gregory of Sinai (c. 1255–1346). The Greek *Philokalia* (Venice, 1782) gathered them. · **Seal:** Silver

**Casting**

1. Sit alone in a quiet cell on a low stool about a span high (Gregory of Sinai).
2. Bow the head, chin toward the chest, and fix the eyes on the middle of the body, at the place of the heart.
3. Slow the breath so that it does not come and go loudly or freely. The aim is a gentle, restrained breath, not holding it.
4. Let the mind go down with the breath, searching inwardly for "the place of the heart".
5. Repeat the Jesus Prayer: "Lord Jesus Christ, Son of God, have mercy on me." It is often divided, the first half on the in-breath and the second on the out-breath ([Kallistos Ware](https://www.orthodoxprayer.org/Articles_files/Ware-7%20Breathing%20Exercises.html)).
6. When thoughts come, don't argue with them. Return to the prayer.

**Promised effect:** the mind unites with the heart. The writers speak of warmth and, for the advanced, a vision of uncreated light.

**Caution:** The tradition itself ranks the bodily technique as secondary. Gregory Palamas (1296–1359) treated it as mainly for beginners, and Orthodox teachers warn against breathing methods without a spiritual guide.

### 29. Divine Reading (*lectio divina*)

**Source:** Guigo II (d. 1188), prior of the Grande Chartreuse, *The Ladder of Monks* (*Scala Claustralium*), c. 1150–1180; it is considered the first description of methodical prayer in the Western tradition ([summary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guigo_II)). · **Seal:** Gold

**Casting**

1. **Reading** (*lectio*): read a short passage slowly and attentively. Guigo's own example is "Blessed are the clean of heart: for they shall see God" (Matthew 5:8, Douay-Rheims).
2. **Meditation** (*meditatio*): turn the words over and seek what they mean for you.
3. **Prayer** (*oratio*): answer, asking for what the passage has shown you to want.
4. **Contemplation** (*contemplatio*): rest in it, beyond words.

Guigo's image for the four: reading puts food whole into the mouth, meditation chews it, prayer draws out its flavour, and contemplation is the sweetness itself.

**Promised effect:** a ladder from earth to heaven, with contemplation as a foretaste of eternal joy.

### 30. The One Word

**Source:** *The Cloud of Unknowing*, an anonymous English guide from the late 14th century, chapter 7 ([text](https://ccel.org/ccel/anonymous2/cloud/cloud.xiii.html)). · **Seal:** Silver

**Casting**

1. Lift your heart to God with a simple stirring of love, wanting God himself, not ideas about God.
2. Gather that intention into one short word. The author prefers one syllable to two, and suggests "God" or "love", or any other you like.
3. Fasten the word to your heart so that it never leaves.
4. Use it as shield and spear. With it, beat on the "cloud of unknowing" above you, and push every other thought down beneath a "cloud of forgetting".
5. If a thought asks what you want, answer only with the word.

**Promised effect:** a "naked intent" toward God that pierces the darkness: what thought cannot reach, love can.

**Note:** Centering Prayer, taught since the 1970s, is a modern method adapted from this book (Appendix A).

### 31. The Daily Examen

**Source:** Ignatius of Loyola (1491–1556), *Spiritual Exercises* §43, begun at Manresa in 1522 and approved by the pope in 1548 ([text](https://ccel.org/ccel/ignatius/exercises/exercises.xii.iii.html)). · **Seal:** Gold

**Casting**

1. Give thanks to God for the benefits received.
2. Ask for grace to know your faults and to cast them out.
3. Review the day hour by hour, from rising until now: thoughts, words and deeds.
4. Ask pardon for your faults.
5. Resolve to amend, with grace. End with the Our Father.

**Promised effect:** in Ignatius's framing, a purified conscience and a better confession; in practice, daily attentiveness.

**Note:** The popular "consciousness examen" of the 1970s shifts the focus from faults to noticing God's presence. Ignatius's own five points are about faults and amendment.

## Chapter VII — The Sufi Path

Sufi practice centres on *dhikr*, the remembrance of God, which al-Ghazālī described step by step around 1100.

### 32. Remembrance (*dhikr*)

**Source:** The Qur'an, 13:28 ("Verily in the remembrance of Allah do hearts find rest", Pickthall's translation); the method as described by al-Ghazālī (1058–1111) in *The Revival of the Religious Sciences* (*Iḥyāʾ ʿulūm al-dīn*). · **Seal:** Gold

**Casting**

1. Sit alone, in seclusion, with the heart emptied of other concerns.
2. Repeat "Allāh, Allāh" with the tongue and the heart together, without letting attention lapse.
3. Continue until the tongue falls silent and the word goes on in the heart by itself.
4. Continue until the heart's speaking stops too, and only the meaning of the word remains, without letters or sound.
5. Then wait. Al-Ghazālī says that what follows is not in your power: the seed has been planted.

**Promised effect:** a heart cleared of the world and open to what God unveils. Al-Ghazālī warns that false images may appear first.

**Note:** Orders differ: some practise *dhikr* aloud and in groups, others silently. A common formula is *lā ilāha illā-llāh*, "there is no god but God".

### 33. The Naqshbandi Watchwords

**Source:** ʿAbd al-Khāliq Ghijduvānī (d. 1179), near Bukhara, coined eight Persian watchwords; Bahāʾ al-Dīn Naqshband (1318–1389) added three ([overview](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eleven_Naqshbandi_principles)). They come down mainly through later Naqshbandi works such as *Rashaḥāt ʿayn al-ḥayāt* (early 16th c.) ([study](https://magiran.com/paper/2357835/the-process-of-the-formation-of-the-eleven-principles-of-the-naqshbandiyya-order-in-the-remaining-texts-from-the-beginning-to-rashahat-ayn-al-hayat?lang=en)). · **Seal:** Bronze

**Casting** (carry them through the day)

1. *Hūsh dar dam*, awareness in the breath: let no breath go in or out in forgetfulness of God.
2. *Naẓar bar qadam*, eyes on your steps: when walking, look at your feet so the gaze doesn't scatter.
3. *Safar dar waṭan*, journey in the homeland: travel inward, from blameworthy traits to praiseworthy ones.
4. *Khalwat dar anjuman*, solitude in the crowd: outwardly among people, inwardly with God.
5. *Yād kard*, remembering: practise the *dhikr* your teacher gives you.
6. *Bāz gasht*, returning: after each round of *dhikr*, turn back to God with intention.
7. *Nigāh dāsht*, watchfulness: guard the heart against stray thoughts.
8. *Yād dāsht*, recollection: hold the awareness of God steadily.

Naqshband's three additions: awareness of time (*wuqūf-i zamānī*), checking your state hour by hour; awareness of number (*wuqūf-i ʿadadī*), counting the repetitions of silent *dhikr*; and awareness of the heart (*wuqūf-i qalbī*), keeping attention on the heart during *dhikr*.

**Promised effect:** unbroken presence with God in the middle of ordinary life.

### 34. Watchfulness and Self-Accounting (*murāqaba* and *muḥāsaba*)

**Source:** al-Ghazālī, *The Revival of the Religious Sciences*, book 38, *On Vigilance and Self-Examination*, building on al-Muḥāsibī (d. 857), whose name comes from this very practice ([book summary](https://fonsvitae.com/product/al-ghazali-vigilance-self-examination)). · **Seal:** Gold

**Casting** (six stations across one day)

1. **Setting terms** (*mushāraṭa*), in the morning. Treat the soul as a business partner and today's hours as capital, and agree the day's conditions with it.
2. **Watchfulness** (*murāqaba*), all day. Keep in mind that God sees every act and thought.
3. **Self-accounting** (*muḥāsaba*), in the evening. Review the day as a merchant reviews the books: where did you profit, and where did you lose?
4. **Penalty** (*muʿāqaba*). Answer a lapse with a measured penalty, such as an extra good deed.
5. **Striving** (*mujāhada*). Push harder where the soul fell short.
6. **Reproach** (*muʿātaba*). Rebuke the soul when it resists.

**Promised effect:** a soul kept honest and ready for the final reckoning. The practice's motto is a saying attributed to the caliph ʿUmar: take account of yourselves before you are taken to account.

**Note:** Compare Spells 23 and 31: Pythagoreans, Stoics, Sufis and Jesuits all arrived at a nightly audit of the day.
