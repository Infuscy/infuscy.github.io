Chapter 39: The Dream of Gryphons

That afternoon, by the time the sun was directly overhead, Jace and Grede had already set out — they hadn't even stopped somewhere for lunch.

Carriages to and from Goldshire ran all hours of the day, so the two of them hitched a ride and were on their way.

On one side, dappled light filtering through the Elwynn Forest canopy; little deer peeking out from the thicket; birds of every color flitting back and forth among the branches; the rhythmic clatter of hooves and wheels.

For the moment he'd forgotten all about orcs, werewolves, Death Knights, and the Shadow Council's orders, watching the gates of Stormwind grow farther and farther away until they vanished among the treetops and shafts of light, and let his mind go blank.

Lying back in the jostling rear seat, drinking someone else's goat's milk that had a bit of a gamey smell to it, Jace honestly felt like it'd be fine if the rest of his life went on just like this.

"The other day I took a look at the Mage Quarter's horses — every last one of them a beauty," he asked the Grede beside him, one leg hitched up. "How much would it cost to keep a horse here?"

Grede opened his eyes and looked at the sky. "I don't know horses, never rode one — you're asking the wrong guy. But I know if you want to keep a horse, you've got to distinguish. A regular pack horse and a warhorse aren't even in the same class, let alone those famous steeds only rich folk can ride."

Jace said, "Someone told me the real warhorses that Stormwind's knights ride cost something like several dozen silver a month to keep. You think that number's credible?"

Grede rolled onto his side. "I'd say it's probably about right, but that's factoring in combat wear and tear — like paying someone to source feed, the freight costs for hauling feed, training expenses, and so on. My guess is, if you stabled a warhorse and kept it as a pet, it wouldn't cost nearly that much."

"Keep a warhorse as a pet — wouldn't that just make it an ordinary horse?" Jace asked.

"Ha, exactly. You get what you pay for, always." Grede said, "Some gryphons, cooped up in the nest and fed, can't even fly the first time they try after going a long time without flying — stupid as a pig with a bird's head. But flying them regularly for training costs too much coin and too much effort. Just dealing with gryphons exhausts me. I only want to ride them charging into battle — I've zero interest in managing those monsters' eating, drinking, and shitting. They're loud, they're vicious, and they've got opinions about everything."

Hearing Grede talk about gryphons, Jace's curiosity perked up. "Then have you ever thought about how many trainers suffer behind the scenes so you can ride a gryphon in style?"

"Hah, I'm not heartless," Grede said. "Did I ever tell you about Gotsrum? He's a gryphon keeper. My knowledge of gryphons is limited to how to communicate with them in battle, getting them to obey in a fight… but Gotsrum is a true master trainer. I believe he could glance at a gryphon flying overhead and tell you exactly how heavy the balls hanging between its legs are."

"Gryphons have balls?" Jace asked.

Grede fired back, "If birds and cats both have them, why wouldn't gryphons?"

Well, that actually made sense. Jace asked again, "So what does it feel like to ride a gryphon?"

The dwarf narrowed his eyes and thought for a long while before answering. "It's like riding a woman who can't get enough — she's thrashing wildly, wanting more, but you feel like you're already exhausted, so you can only let her toss you about as she pleases, grabbing your arms, your shoulders, shaking you like crazy. But the moment you whistle, she understands what you mean, and surges forward just the way you want for a spell — then slowly you realize you're losing control again. Come to think of it, even the smell's about the same."

Jace couldn't tell whether Grede was serious, or whether he was simultaneously boasting about having both slept with an insatiable woman and ridden a crazed gryphon — after all, he had no frame of reference for either.

"Wonder if I'll ever get the chance to ride a gryphon," he muttered, as if to himself.

"You?" Grede turned his head to look at Jace.

He didn't answer right away. Instead he gave Jace a thorough once-over, then seemed to think about something else for a while before saying, "I'm thinking of heading back to Aerie Peak before Winter Veil. Coming with me?"

"Why? What for?"

"Maybe you humans don't feel this way, but Winter Veil's the most important holiday for dwarves. I want to go back, see my old friends — at the very least let everyone know I'm still alive."

Jace asked hesitantly, "Does Aerie Peak welcome humans?"

"No," Grede snorted out a laugh. "But they won't string you up and feed you to the gryphons either. I think after the Orc Wars, my clansmen's attitude toward you lot has improved a bit — after all, countless dwarven warriors witnessed human bravery in battle and let go of a lot of old prejudices. At the very least, most of the kin I know think that compared to elves, humans are unquestionably the more reliable allies. Take you — a seventeen-year-old lad, usually timid as a rabbit, but when you ran into an undead monster you were brave as a lion. Wouldn't have guessed it."

Jace gave an awkward laugh — this guy really knew how to make a compliment feel uncomfortable.

The dwarf let out a long breath. "Mainly I wanted to say — you might actually get the chance to ride a gryphon when the time comes."

"For real?" Jace sat up straight. "I can ride a gryphon too?"

"Why couldn't you?" Grede said. "Whether you can ride isn't for us to decide — it's for the gryphon to decide."

"What do you mean?"

"Gryphons are incredibly smart. You won't grasp how smart until you've actually dealt with one. Sometimes it's hard to see them as beasts at all. You could say the relationship between gryphons and our clan isn't one of taming and being tamed — it's more like a naturally formed alliance. Look — even wild gryphons roaming the mountains won't attack dwarves on sight, and our clan treats those gryphons as friends, stepping in to help when they're in danger, and vice versa. It's just that gryphons are more protective of their own safety. If a dwarf runs into a bear, a gryphon might swoop down to help. But if it's a troll, a wild gryphon will tend to look out for itself. Of course, the gryphons who live alongside us fight to the death — unless they decide a suicide run serves no purpose."

"But doesn't that affect your operations?" Jace said. "Say you're determined to carry out a mission that's extremely risky but equally rewarding, and the gryphon refuses — what then? Doesn't that drastically undermine the gryphon riders' combat efficiency? After all, a lot of the time sacrifices have to be made."

The dwarf leaned toward Jace and replied, "And that's the heart of it, lad. Gryphons are generally fearless when it comes to sacrifices that are truly worth making, but they'll flat-out refuse decisions that are just plain stupid. Most of the time they're right, and they actively influence the gryphon riders' tactics. That's why I say gryphons are absurdly smart — like a flock of chattering sages that command your respect."

Chattering sages… from Jace's perspective, he had a hard time wrapping his head around this kind of relationship.

But the Wildhammer dwarves had been living this way for two hundred years. The Hinterlands trolls they fought only grew weaker with time, while the Wildhammer dwarves from afar had built the great Aerie Peak stronghold together with the gryphons — what standing did he have to criticize an alliance forged in iron like that?

"Of course," Grede said, leaning back and resting on his arms, "those gryphons aren't obedient all the time either. The moment they hit the ground, you've got to find a way to cover their eyes — like how you humans put blinders on warhorses, blocking their side vision so they charge straight ahead without fear. Unlike horses, which are too timid, the gryphons' problem is that they're too bold. These little bastards love bullying the weak, and they're always eating. Cooped up in the sky too long, and the moment they land those urges become completely uncontrollable — they run wild no matter how hard you pull, impossible to restrain."

Jace asked, "If I showed up in front of them, would they eat me?"

Grede sighed. "Was everything I just said for nothing? What I mean is, gryphons are willing to cooperate with brave, good people — willing to fight alongside those who treasure the bond between them — and they absolutely have the ability to tell the difference. There was this one time we fought alongside the Hinterlands elves against the trolls, and the elves could even ride gryphons whose dwarven riders had fallen, fighting just as bravely alongside those gryphons as they did with dwarves. If those scheming, shameless elves could manage it, why can't you? Unless you're hiding some dark secret you don't want anyone to know about."

Jace licked his lips and thought to himself — well, actually, yes.

By now he'd already come into contact with one unambiguously dark piece of magic — Fear. So it was possible his body and mind had already been affected by shadow energy in some way, even if he didn't feel it at all.

If those gryphons could sense the darkness in his heart the way the Holy Light could, and rushed over to carry him off in one snap of their beaks — then what?

But the temptation to ride a gryphon was just too great. If he got the chance to ride one even once, what did it matter if he risked getting carried off in a beak? That was flying!

Jace asked again, "So why don't the elves train gryphons for battle? Since they can fight together, why haven't they formed the same kind of alliance as the Wildhammer dwarves and gryphons? The elves have lived in the Hinterlands far longer than the dwarves, haven't they?"

"You're right about that," Grede said. "Before we came to Aerie Peak, the elves had already lived in the Hinterlands for nearly a thousand years."

He pulled a piece of grass he'd somehow plucked from the roadside at some point, rolled it between his fingers, and stuck it in his mouth. "As for why they're unwilling to train gryphons, I think it's actually quite simple — elves don't like cooperating with others, and they don't like things they can't control. They like to enslave every living thing they come across. If they can't enslave it, they kill it. If they can't beat it, or the cost is too high, they wall it outside their homeland and avoid contact as much as possible — unless they run into some enormous trouble they can't handle alone, only then will they lower their heads and ask for help."

Grede went on, "They stubbornly believe that they — the Quel'dorei — are different from us mortals. You know what Quel'dorei means in the elven tongue, don't you? 'The noble born.' They fancy themselves highborn beings born of the Well of Eternity, a sacred race blessed by this world. Everything not ruled by them, everything that doesn't bow to their rules, is a barbarian with no reason to exist. Hard to wrap your head around, hmm?"

Jace gave a dry laugh along with him.

"Still, the red dragons' flames gave those self-righteous sorts a proper lesson," Grede said. "Maybe they've finally understood it's time to step out of their 'invincible' runestone arrays and come take a proper look at the outside world."

"You hate elves this much, so why are you always so keen on getting a look at the Windrunners?" Jace teased.

The dwarf said, "Female elves and male elves aren't the same species! And a female elf in bed and a female elf out of bed are two different species! You're in the Mage Quarter — at least learn some species classification from those mages, lad. Live long enough and you'll understand. Don't be so rigid!"
