## Chapter 40: A Night Walk in the Woods

The warrant said the farm lay a few miles east of Goldshire. By the time they arrived, it was already evening.

The farm owner's name was Stormill. Jace didn't remember a farm by that name on any map, but since "Stormill" meant "vast lake" in Common, most people called this place Lakeview Farm.

Even though there wasn't anything remotely lake-like anywhere nearby — even the closest river was seven or eight miles south.

To deal with the wolves, Jace had brought that orcish rod along, because when facing beasts an inch longer meant an inch stronger. These wolves weren't wargs — probably couldn't take more than a few hits — and using an axe would've been way too unwieldy.

Still, to keep anyone knowledgeable from recognizing it, he'd wrapped it tightly in cloth so the color and material underneath didn't show.

The dwarf was carrying a sword in one hand and had a bow slung across his back — looking far more like a professional fighter than Jace's street-thug getup. The two of them strolled straight toward the farm as bold as you please.

Lakeview Farm wasn't much of a farm, despite the grand-sounding name. By the look of it, it was less impressive than the farm the two of them had snuck into back in Duskwood. Even the mill and the grain storehouse had a bit of a humble, shoestring feel.

Jace sized up the farm's scale and got the illusion that he could've single-handedly put up every building and fence on the property except the house.

"Hey — anyone here?" the dwarf bellowed at the farm.

A man peeked out from behind a standing bundle of straw, then suddenly took off running.

"Why's he running?" Grede asked, puzzled.

Jace glanced at him, then at himself, and immediately shouted, "We're not bandits! We're here to help — Mr. Stormill!"

The man in the middle of his sprint looked back, then kept running without stopping, all the way into the grain storehouse.

The two of them chased after him to the storehouse. The man suddenly charged out clutching a pitchfork. Grede immediately caught the pitchfork between the blades of his sword and swept it aside, pinning it into the dirt.

"We're here to help you — what do you think you're doing?"

"Get out!" the man said through gritted teeth. "What good is coming now?!"

Jace asked, "What happened?"

"My child… my child was only four months old!" he said, glaring at Jace. "She was taken by those wolves — I couldn't even catch up!"

"When?" Jace asked.

"The day before yesterday," the man said. "I don't know how many times I've reported it to the Goldshire guards. If you'd come sooner and found those wolves, it never would've come to this!"

"Why didn't you go look for them yourself?" the dwarf asked.

"I can't leave the farm." The man was so agitated he could barely get the words out. "My wife was bitten — I couldn't kill a single wolf… I can't do anything right. I'm useless, a complete waste!"

With that, he suddenly wrenched at the pitchfork — couldn't pull it free — and turned to snatch the dwarf's sword. Jace rushed forward and wrestled him to the ground, but the man, half-mad as he was, threw off Jace's grip with shocking strength and crashed into the unsuspecting Grede full-on.

The two of them rolled around in the pile of chopped straw, stalks and chaff flying everywhere. The man scrambled up and went for the sword again — but the sword was already at his throat.

"Try grabbing it again and I'll send you to your ancestors before the wolves even open their mouths," the dwarf said coldly.

"I'm going to find those wolves — kill them!"

The man shouted once, then slumped despairingly against the pile of grain sacks, hammering a fist against the storehouse's wooden pillar hard enough to make the whole building shudder.

The dwarf pressed the tip of his sword down. He was getting the sense this man's mind had already come apart from rage.

Jace looked at the man, then toward the cottage he lived in. An old woman stood in the doorway, watching. She could barely stay on her feet.

She didn't say anything — just stared blankly. Jace had the feeling she might be too far gone in years to have a clear mind, and might not even know what was happening over here.

That was probably his mother, or his wife's mother.

This man was supporting two women and a child on this farm. Running into a wolf raid in the middle of the night was genuinely helpless. If his child had been carried off, of course he didn't dare chase too far.

If he went too far and the wolves circled back, his whole family could really be wiped out.

In a situation like that, how could you expect a confused old woman and a young woman who'd just given birth to stand against a pack of wolves?

"My name isn't Stormill," the man said quietly. "This is my wife's family farm. She's a Stormill. My name is Matt — Matt Fullere."

He picked the pitchfork up off the ground again. "I'll take this pitchfork and go with you. We'll kill every last one of those wolves. Kill them all — revenge for my child."

"We've already been paid, Mr. Fullere," Jace said, clapping him on the shoulder. "If you come along, you don't expect us to split the bounty with you too, do you? That's not happening. You stay home, keep your family safe. If we find the wolves, we'll deal with them for you."

This man's mind was muddled with rage by now. Bringing him along would probably only cause more trouble — they'd have to figure out how to keep him from getting hurt on top of everything else.

Fullere stood there stunned for a moment, not knowing what to say.

In the end, he said quietly, "If you find my child… or… remains, or any belongings — please bring them back for me. I'll pay you more. I don't have much on me right now — maybe two silver. I know that's nothing to mercenaries like you, but I sincerely hope — anything at all, even just his clothes… at least give my wife some kind of closure."

Jace said, "Two silver's not nothing. Whatever the outcome, you pull yourself together."

The man nodded. "My wife says the same thing."

Jace said, "Your wife's right. Go back, take good care of her, keep the farm safe, and don't let those wolves in again tonight."

"I understand." The man had just turned to leave when he seemed to remember something. "Those wolves… I've heard people say there's a pack holed up in the forest to the south."

"How many in the pack?" Grede asked.

The man rubbed his eyes. "A hunter said he often sees them prowling the forest in groups of four or five. I don't dare go looking alone, so all I could do was ask Goldshire for help."

After the man left, Grede watched his broken, hollowed-out figure retreat into the distance. "Move now, or wait for daylight?"

Jace slung the rod over his shoulder. "Let's start looking. If there's a chance the child's still alive, we're more likely to run into the wolves at night anyway."

"But what are the odds that child's still alive?" Grede asked.

Jace said, "No matter how slim the odds, we have to get moving as soon as possible, Grede."

The dwarf said, "Don't get your hopes up — know your limits. Wouldn't want the child somehow still alive and the two of us dead."

Jace let out a long breath. "I have a feeling this is going to be a lot more complicated than the bounty made it sound."

"Same here," Grede said.

Searching for something in Elwynn Forest was harder than doing a quest in Elwynn Forest in the game by an order of magnitude.

Especially at night. Jace felt like every direction looked the same — pitch-black patches of woodland, the occasional cry of some animal. Once they'd gone far from the farm, there wasn't even a single landmark left to get their bearings by.

The center of Duskwood had a highland — a primeval forest of enormous trees clustered on raised ground — so you could use the elevation and the forest's shadow as a reference for direction.

But here? Truly blind as a bat. Couldn't make out a thing.

"Ever hunted wolves before?" Jace asked.

"Not at night," Grede said, stepping over a stretch of tree roots. "But I'm certain there are wolves around here — not just because that fellow said so, but because…"

He grabbed a bush. "Look at this."

In the pitch-black night, Jace leaned in close to Grede's hand and barely made out a few silvery threads by the sliver of moonlight.

"Wolf fur?"

Grede nodded. "I've got good news and bad news."

"Give me the good news first," Jace said reflexively.

"Does the order matter?" Grede said. "Good news: there's quite a lot of wolf fur around here. I've only been walking a short while and I've already seen plenty of it. That means we'll probably have an easier time finding the den."

Jace picked up where he left off: "Bad news is, there's too much wolf fur."

Grede spread his hands. "Exactly. Let's hope all this fur is from cubs following the mother wolf. If it's a whole big pack, we might be in serious trouble."

"Should we head back for now?" Jace asked.

"A bit late for that." Grede looked up at the moon's shadow filtering through the canopy. "Better make a fire here — it'll keep the beasts away. I need to get some sleep first and rest up for the fight ahead. Keep watch for a bit, will you? Don't worry, just a quick nap."

Jace knew Grede had always been the type to sleep whenever he was tired, so he nodded. The two of them walked a ways toward a thinner stretch of woods. Grede found a clear spot, sat down, and patted himself down. "Damn it — I didn't bring my tinder. Must've dropped it on the way."

"I've got some." Jace took a box of tinder from the warlock's pouch and handed it over. Grede glanced at his bag. "That's a good-looking pouch, I'll say that much."

This pouch didn't look cheap at all, and it didn't seem like orcish make either — more like something a noble or a high mage would carry around. Strutting through the Mage Quarter with this thing had a certain flair.

Jace gathered dry branches and sticks for fuel while Grede cleared the ground, sat down, and started striking his flint with his dagger — a couple of strikes, a spark, a breath on the tinder.

"Hey, Jace, why don't you take over for a bit? This thing's not cooperating."

"Is the air too damp?"

Jace had just turned around when he saw, ten to twenty meters behind Grede, in the underbrush — pairs of gleaming eyes staring right at them.
