Chapter 46: An Orc's Command

That evening, Jace spent the whole time copying down the orcish script from the scroll.

Fifty-one orcish symbols in total. He had to make sure his transcript was precise and error-free — every curve of every angled line, the placement of every dot, the thickness of every stroke, all of it had to be mimicked exactly. It ate up no small amount of ink and paper, but fortunately those could be charged to Eden Marin's account.

The reason he had to be this meticulous was that he didn't know whether variations in stroke thickness might also change the meaning or pronunciation.

It took him four or five hours. Only once he was certain the copies were virtually identical did he go to bed.

The scroll itself absolutely could not be brought into the Wizard Sanctum. He had no idea what kind of magical power that scroll carried — if there was something he couldn't detect that got picked up by the mages inside the Sanctum, it would spell unnecessary trouble.

The next day, once work was over, Jace immediately took his transcribed pages and rushed to the library. Following the directions Marin had given him, he found the orcish-language research section.

The books here were clearly all very thin new volumes, many of them freshly copied by magic.

With no one else reading nearby, he grabbed several at once and spread them out on the floor: A Primer on Orcish Runes, Basic Patterns of Orcish Rune Combinations, Orcish Runes: A Common-Tongue Abridged Reference, Introduction to Learning Orcish, Orcish for Dummies, and so on.

Then he started looking up the runes from the scroll — and hit a wall on the very first symbol.

Logically, the beginning of a normal text should be some common connecting word or other. How could it possibly be this hard to find?

And yet he spent a full hour flipping through various comparison charts and got nowhere.

Then a possibility occurred to him: it might be a person's name.

If this scroll was a letter, then the opening was probably a form of address, or a name. Generally speaking, even if a name had a concrete meaning, you'd have to trace it back to some ancient linguistic root — so trying to find the meaning of a name in a modern, commonly-used language was bound to be tough.

So he decided to switch tactics. He'd fix a set amount of time for each word; if he couldn't find it, he'd skip to the next one. First pass: skim through everything roughly, guess at the overall meaning, then come back later and do a careful translation with the context to guide him.

Using this method, he quickly found the meaning of the third word: "servant," or "follower." That wasn't hard to understand — the writer of the letter was probably the recipient's superior and chief. In other words, this was a command letter.

The tricky part was the fourth word: "staff."

What was that about?

If this letter had something to do with Gul'dan, then "staff" very likely referred to the legendary Scepter of Sargeras.

The moment he made that connection, his whole body shuddered. That beat-up thing in his room couldn't possibly be the Scepter of Sargeras — of course not.

Even if he, as a transmigrator, had the protagonist halo and a cheat fully activated (he didn't), and somehow had the luck to come into possession of this staff, then how had that orc managed to carry the Scepter of Sargeras through Duskwood?

And on top of that, he'd died.

The person who'd handed him the staff — had they lost their mind? A relic like this, and the Horde's Warchief Ner'zhul would've wanted an entire legion to escort it.

Ner'zhul knew full well that the Horde could no longer take Azeroth, and that Draenor had already been reduced to a vast wasteland by all manner of dark energies and demonic power. So when he learned that the Alliance army was preparing to invade Draenor, Ner'zhul decided to lead what remained of the Horde in Draenor to other worlds, to find a new home.

To open a portal large enough for the surviving Horde to pass through, the Scepter of Sargeras was one of the essential relics.

It had been created for the express purpose of the dark titan Sargeras using it to project his avatar into Azeroth — only that projection hadn't gone very smoothly, and through centuries of coincidence the scepter had eventually ended up in Ner'zhul's hands.

He kept translating, all the way to the last phrase — "for the Horde" — and by the time he'd worked that out, Jace was certain this was a letter.

The general meaning of the letter was: the master tells the servant about three important things… the direction of the Dark Portal — someone has died, someone is the chieftain, from one world to another, for the Horde.

Jace was very familiar with this period of history, so even with a good deal of key information missing, he could already piece together the basic picture of the entire letter.

The three important things represented the Scepter of Sargeras, the Eye of Dalaran, and the Book of Medivh — all relics that Ner'zhul needed in order to find a new world.

Before the Dark Portal was completely destroyed, Ner'zhul had tasked Gul'dan's most trusted Shadow Council member — and the world's first death knight — Teron Gorefiend, to cross the Dark Portal to Azeroth and recover these three relics, to save the Horde.

Centuries ago, after Sargeras' avatar was defeated, Medivh's mother — who by then was already the Guardian of Tirisfal, Aegwynn — had buried his remains and the fallen Scepter of Sargeras in the Tomb of Sargeras.

Gul'dan had wanted to claim this relic along with the power of Sargeras' avatar, but was instead consumed by the demonic forces within and died a miserable death.

The Eye of Dalaran was kept in Dalaran's vault, and the Book of Medivh was under the control of the king of Alterac, the Perenolde family. Because the Kingdom of Alterac had betrayed the Alliance during the Orc Wars, the king was imprisoned and the Alliance took over governance of the kingdom, so Gorefiend simply led black dragons and orcs in an assault on Alterac and seized the Book of Medivh.

In the end, with the help of the Black Dragonflight, Gorefiend successfully took all three relics, returned to Draenor, and handed them over to Ner'zhul, who used them to open a portal — that was the "from one world to another."

One person dies, one person becomes chieftain — that very likely referred to Gul'dan, who had become the Horde's de facto ruler during the Orc Wars, dying in the Tomb of Sargeras, while Ner'zhul would become the chieftain.

Since the Horde on the Azeroth side hadn't yet received this news, that meant this letter was someone who knew the truth informing their counterparts here about the situation on the other side of the Dark Portal, and ordering them to return to the Dark Portal to join the Horde in departing for a new world.

By the time Jace reached this point in his understanding, the sky outside the window had already darkened, but he was still quite pleased with his results. He hadn't expected the letter to be decoded so smoothly.

There was just one problem remaining: what exactly did the first line mean, and what did that "staff" refer to?

After a deeper dive into orcish, he learned that when describing the three relics, the writer had used an honorific symbol to express just how important and sacred these three relics were.

But on the rune for that staff, he didn't see this modifier symbol. Which meant the staff this rune pointed to really wasn't the Scepter of Sargeras.

Did that necessarily mean it was the stick in Jace's possession? Not necessarily. That stick was very likely the messenger's own staff, and the symbol pointed to something else entirely.

This time, Jace found a book on the shelf called An Exploration of Orcish Rune Pronunciation Rules and another called Orcish Pronunciation Instruction, and prepared to try reading the letter aloud.

His thinking was: if he could read it aloud, he might be able to connect this name to some famous figure in his memory.

Orcish pronunciation wasn't very regular — perhaps because orcish vocabulary was full of loanwords from ogre and eredar that had corrupted the original pronunciation rules — but Jace still managed, relying on fuzzy pronunciations, to roughly identify the names Gul'dan and Ner'zhul in the letter.

Gul'dan was dead. Ner'zhul had become chieftain.

Ah… the thrill of decoding a letter was just like solving a math problem.

Even though it was already dark, Jace wasn't the least bit sleepy, because he still hadn't connected the first word to any famous figure.

"Ang'gor."

He sounded out the pronunciation, carefully searching his memory, but couldn't match it to any name he knew.

Wait… if Gul'dan was dead and Ner'zhul had become chieftain, then who had written the letter?

In truth, the answer was already on the tip of his tongue. The writer was probably Teron Gorefiend himself — only he would know the latest progress on collecting the three relics — and the recipient was probably a servant he'd left behind in Azeroth: another servant of the Shadow Council, or more precisely, a death knight.

The sky outside was already showing signs of dawn, but Jace felt even more alert than the night before.

He glanced behind the bookshelves. Plenty of hardworking apprentices and apprentice mages were still persevering with their reading, and the arcane lamps inside the Wizard Sanctum still burned for them. He wasn't the most conspicuous one there.

Jace decided to try translating the few phrases after "Ang'gor" and after "staff" — that is, the first paragraph of the letter.

Drawing on the orcish linguistic rules he'd already grasped, and some of the grammar he'd picked up while translating the letter, he went back through the orcish references he'd been less familiar with at the start, and finally found a phrase that slotted in perfectly after the word "staff" and looked very much like that string of runes on the letter.

It came from a letter written by the Horde's Warchief Blackhand to his own son. Blackhand used the battle standard of the Blackrock clan to represent his own authority — expressing that to see the banner was to see Blackhand himself.

This "see the banner as seeing the person" expression closely matched the string of runes after "staff." It was a postpositive construction, which meant the phrase's meaning was: "see the staff as seeing the person."

If this was Teron Gorefiend's handwritten letter, then "see the staff as seeing the person" — didn't that mean this staff was Gorefiend's own staff of authority?

But Jace remembered clearly that in the game, Teron Gorefiend's weapon looked like a hollow multi-bladed maul, with a dark magic stone set between the blades to empower his weapon.

The stick he had hidden at home was long and thin — it didn't look anything like the battle staff of an immensely strong warrior. It'd make a decent carrying pole, though.

Wait.

Teron Gorefiend had originally been an orc warlock. After he died, Gul'dan had guided his soul into the corpse of a human knight, and only then had he taken on the appearance of a death knight.

Could that stick have been the staff he'd used as an orc warlock, back when he'd lived on Draenor under the name Teron — and after he became a death knight, he'd left it behind as a token of authority, something like that?

Once his wild theories reached this point, Jace felt he'd long since run out of solid evidence and was purely guessing.

And it was the middle of the night. Though he was still full of drive, he could clearly feel his mind starting to slow down — after all, his injuries still ached.

It was still early, well before Marin's usual time for going to his study. He took the books he'd pulled from the shelves and carefully sorted and reshelved them by their original categories, then gathered up his transcription of the letter and left the library.

On the way to Marin's study, he spotted a staff shop by the roadside.
