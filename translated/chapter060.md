## Chapter 60: The Fisher and the Fish

Early the next morning, Jace and Grede set out early into the mountains.

Jace remembered that this road leading straight into the mountains — in twenty-plus years, the Blackrock orcs would gradually take over this area, setting up ambushes along the road to lie in wait for passing travelers. But for now it was still garrisoned by the Lakeshire militia.

The further they got from Lakeshire, the deeper they climbed into the dense forest on the hillsides.

But no matter where you stood, if you looked out and down you could see the surface of Lake Everstill in the distance, and Stonewatch at the edge of your vision. So unlike in Elwynn Forest, you couldn't get lost too quickly, and the two of them weren't particularly on edge.

"Give me the potion first," Grede said, holding out his hand. "If we run into him suddenly, there won't be time to hand them out."

Jace passed him the bottle. Grede held it up and shook it back and forth. "This potion you brewed… feels like it's got an average skill level, honestly."

"Brilliant, Master Alchemist," Jace said, grabbing for the bottle. "Then drink your own potion."

Grede snatched the bottle away and laughed. "Whoa, whoa, why so touchy?"

Just as the two of them were bickering, a dull roll of thunder echoed from the other side of the mountain.

"It's going to rain," Grede said. "See? I told you — those folks in town are reliable. Let's get down to the lake as quick as we can."

Jace kept pace with him. "You remember what those fishermen were saying yesterday? About the murlocs and all that."

Grede said, "We just stay away from where the murlocs live. Those little straw huts they build on the shore are visible from hundreds of yards off."

The two of them ran toward the shore, and just as they were about to emerge from the treeline Jace called out to Grede, "Don't go any closer to the shore. If we do, we won't have a shred of cover — won't we get spotted the moment we get close?"

Grede grabbed a tree trunk to keep from sliding further down the slope. The terrain was already very steep here, only leveling out again once it reached the lakeshore.

"Good point." He looked up and gave Jace, not far above, a thumbs-up. "From here on out, we need to be careful."

The two of them waited by the edge of the woods. By now raindrops had started to fall, scattered and sparse.

Jace made his way from tree to tree until he was beside Grede, steadied himself, and said, "Those militia — are they sure this orc comes out every time it rains?"

"I didn't say definitely," Grede said. "It's just that every time they've spotted him, it was raining. There's still some distance to cover. The militia guess his hideout's probably in that hollow behind the big rock over there. When it rains he runs down to the lakeshore to catch fish to fill his stomach. Let's move closer."

The two of them went a bit further, stopping in a stretch of level woodland. Grede looked out at the lake — nothing unusual at all.

The rain was coming down hard now. Jace was more or less soaked through. He sat on a rock staring at the lake when a flash of lightning suddenly lit up the world, then slowly dimmed again.

He seemed to remember something — something about how that orc caught fish.

"Have you ever heard of people called shamans?" he asked Grede, who was leaning against a tree.

"Heard of them? I know one personally," Grede said, popping a berry he'd pulled from a bush into his mouth. "Humans call them shamans. We call them sages. They're dwarves who can communicate with the earth and the sky. In the ancient days they were the chieftains and counselors of the Wildhammer clan. They'd use the power of the storm to bless our war hammers, making the hammers carry lightning — easy to shatter the wings of those blood-drinking bats. But a lot of people say that even before I was born, the sages were already not as powerful as they were in the time of our ancestors. So by their account, even though they were that incredible, they've only gotten weaker since."

"Probably has something to do with the trolls," Jace said.

Grede spat the berry out downhill. "The sages have said similar things. The trolls' sacrificial activities have affected their connection to the elements, the ancestors, even the spirits. But I don't understand how, exactly."

Jace said, "My guess is this — in the past, the elements in Khaz Modan knew the dwarves. But the Hinterlands have been troll territory for thousands of years. The elemental spirits there have lived alongside the trolls for God knows how long — maybe they've been in contact with troll witch doctors for tens of thousands of years. In other words, the elements there don't trust outsider dwarves."

Grede asked, "How do you know all this?"

"The library," Jace said. "That's not important. What's important is — could that orc be a shaman?"

"What?" Grede said, incredulous. "That orc is a sage of the clan? But according to you, outsiders don't earn the trust of the local elements. Even if… even if he knew the elements back on Draenor, isn't he still an outsider here? Wouldn't he be rejected?"

"Humans here abandoned elemental worship who knows how many years ago," Jace said. "These days, mages just use the arcane to compel the elements to serve them. If you ask me, the elements probably resent humans. They're even willing to work with gnolls! So if a shaman shows up who's skilled at communicating with elements — at least more skilled than a gnoll, say — it wouldn't be strange for the elements to cooperate with him."

But even as he said it, Jace felt something was off. The orc tribes that had invaded Azeroth had all drunk demon blood. They'd already abandoned the Way of the Elements. Logically, anyone tainted with demonic power — even if they'd once been a shaman — would be shunned by the elements as much as possible.

Was he overthinking it?

Grede asked, "So what, the local elements have betrayed us? No, no, wait — why are you suddenly so sure that orc is some kind of shaman?"

Jace explained. "You said the local militia told you that when it rained, that orc stood on the shore chanting for a bit, and the dead fish in the lake floated right up to the surface. Have you considered whether any mage or warlock could actually do that? What magic could pull that off?"

"Let me think…" Grede put on a thoughtful face.

Jace cut him off. "I'll tell you directly — none can. The only thing that can do it is electricity. Electrofishing!"

"Electrofishing?"

"Right. That shaman summons the storm in the rain and channels lightning into the lake. The fish nearby get electrocuted or stunned and float right up to the surface. For a competent shaman, that's no trouble at all — as long as the elements are willing to help him!"

The dwarf didn't know what to say to that. He looked out toward the lake. In the curtain of rain the water had turned an impenetrable gray. Both Stonewatch and Lakeshire on the far shore had faded from view.

"So how do we deal with him?"

"Deal with him? Please." Jace said, "I don't think we can handle an orc shaman. Never mind his power over wind and lightning — shamans are high-ranking, powerful figures among the Horde. Orcs worship strength. Even in close combat… you confident you could beat him? I don't want to take that risk."

"Well… then let's put it on hold. Why didn't you say so earlier?!" Grede hitched up his pack and sword and turned to head back.

"How is this my fault?" Jace complained.

"Ahem."

"Let's go, quick!"

But when Jace turned around, he saw — up in the woods above, towering and indistinct — a tall black silhouette standing there, looking straight at them.
