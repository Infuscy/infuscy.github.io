Chapter 87: The Coastal Fishing Village

Jace left the farm in the afternoon and continued north. After climbing over that high slope, it wasn't long before he caught sight of the sea far below the cliffs.

He walked for two or three hours, the sun already sinking toward the water, before he finally spotted the fishing village Faolble had mentioned.

It was even more desolate than Faolble had guessed. From here it looked completely empty — totally abandoned.

Only the fish-drying racks and such hadn't been taken down. They just sat there, undisturbed.

This so-called fishing village had only four or five houses, all sturdy wooden structures. There was even a small two-story building in the middle, though after being left to rot for a few years it looked both worn and damp. Jace wandered around the village for a long while and didn't find a single soul.

Looks like that old soldier's commission would have to wait. Maybe when he had a chance he could go back and ask for a more specific name or something.

Faolble had said everyone here had moved to Stormwind — maybe if he went back to Stormwind and asked around, he could pick up a lead?

Thinking it over, Jace was already feeling less like sticking his nose into other people's business. That soldier had seemed shady to begin with, and Jace didn't particularly want to get dragged into something weird.

He stood in the empty space between a few houses — apparently where fish had been dried in the old days. Even now it reeked of salt and rot. The cold wind off the ocean cut right through him, damp and bone-chilling. The thought that these houses would have to endure this same wind and rain for heaven knew how many more years made him feel like they wouldn't hold out much longer.

Well, if nobody was here, the place was his to do with as he pleased.

He walked into the largest house. Some of the bigger pieces of furniture were still inside — there was even a bed, but the bedding on it was soaking wet.

The floor was coated in slippery algae and soggy rot. The walls were crawling with some kind of green vines he couldn't name. Even though the sun hadn't fully set yet, the inside of the house was dark as could be. Still, it was a place that could keep out the wind and rain and offer a bit of warmth, and that was something.

Jace opened his warlock's robe and said, "Come on out, Sazeno."

The imp leapt out and materialized with a shout. "I thought you'd forgotten about me!"

The moment this little fire-spewing demon appeared, it lit the whole house in a faint cinnamon-green glow. Jace said with some concern, "Can you dial back the flames a bit?"

"This is the outward expression of my surging inner fire, Master!" Sazeno said. "You want me to suppress my very nature?"

"Suppress as much as you can," Jace said. "What if you set the place on fire and burned me to death?"

"If you burn to death, I'm free!" Jace and the imp said the exact same thing at the exact same time. Jace finished, glared at him, and said, "Say that again and I'll stomp you to death."

"Hah! I'll remember that! The master's orders must not be disobeyed!" Jace went upstairs and found a spot that was still reasonably dry, shook the dust off something the size of a tablecloth, wiped down a table and chair, sat down, and pulled out a book he'd brought — a guide to Orcish pronunciation — and started memorizing.

Since Faolble had said it'd be safer to look around at night, he'd take his advice this time. The thought of suddenly being spotted by murlocs in broad daylight and having to run a half-hour marathon was enough to make him uneasy.

"Does Master plan to sit here reading until nightfall?"

"Shut up."

By evening the sky was getting darker and darker. Jace nibbled on the biscuits he'd brought while reading, but he could barely make out the words anymore, so he said to Sazeno, "Jump up on the table."

"Yes!" Sazeno, who'd been standing to the side in silence for two hours, hopped up. "What's the task?"

"Keep your emotions steady," Jace said. "No fluctuations. Let the fire from your body seep out slowly… bit by bit."

"I don't know why… but the master's orders must be obeyed!"

The imp tried his best to stay still, settling onto the table right in front of Jace's eyes. Sure enough, the flames coming off him weren't jumping around as much anymore.

"Good. Very good." Jace moved the book a little closer to the imp and kept on memorizing.

After his exchanges with the shaman at Lakeshire, Jace had come to realize that pitch and stress were absolutely critical in Orcish. Of course, that was important in many of the languages he'd studied before, but it seemed like the students learning Orcish in Stormwind hadn't paid it much mind.

Maybe it was because they'd never had the chance to listen to an orc speak directly the way Jace had, and had only learned from their teachers — perhaps they truly hadn't grasped how important it was.

In any case, he'd started paying attention to how pitch was expressed in Orcish runes — which components of a rune carried the tone — and slowly, he'd begun to notice some patterns.

An orcish rune was made up of different small symbols. Some represented pronunciation, some indicated tense, some conveyed meaning, and others set the context. They looked crude and clumsy, but they were actually quite complex.

Bit by bit, Jace found himself starting to enjoy it. Pictures were always more interesting than plain letters, and sometimes you could catch glimpses of orcish culture in the shapes of the runes — things unique to Draenor.

Take the rune for "sheep," for example. The human-authored books simply translated it as "sheep," but as a transmigrator who knew what Draenor looked like, Jace knew the drawing was actually a talbuk — the long-legged, high-horned specialty of Draenor.

The sky kept getting darker. Jace thought he could faintly hear something.

Singing.

"Quiet," he ordered the imp.

He looked out the window. There seemed to be something on the shore. He rubbed his eyes, and it vanished.

The flames coming off the little imp, steady as they were, still cast a bright, dancing reflection on the window in the pitch-black night. Jace said, "Move aside for a moment."

The imp obeyed and hopped off the table. Jace pressed his face against the relatively intact glass and looked out, fixing his gaze on the beach. He made out… something, crouched right where the tide could just reach it.

Not a murloc, was it?

Jace watched for a long while. The shadow sat there without moving a muscle. Judging by its silhouette, it didn't look like a murloc — it looked like a real person, hunched over, squatting or sitting there, staring out at the ocean without moving.

Didn't Faolble say nobody lived here anymore? When had that person shown up there?

"Can you see that person?" Jace asked Sazeno.

"I can see them, I can see them!" the imp said excitedly, pressing against the glass. "Why does Master ask?"

Could it be a fisherman who'd come back from the sea?

Jace was a little skeptical. All the houses here looked like they'd been abandoned for ages. If someone was still living here, their living conditions must be miserable.

Then again, when you considered that it was at least a house — tidy it up a bit and it'd be a damn sight better than the refugee camp outside Stormwind where the rain barely stayed out — it wasn't hard to understand.

Jace decided to go take a look. Maybe that person would know where the soldier's wife had gone.

"Hide in the bag first. Don't show yourself. I'll go down and see what's going on," he told Sazeno.

The ocean at night was pitch black. He couldn't make out a thing. Aside from the constant roar of waves breaking on the beach, everything seemed swallowed by the darkness.

Jace stood on the sand and looked both ways, making sure there were no murlocs — or any other monsters — around, then shifted his gaze back to that blurry figure in the distance.

The sky was even darker now than it had been inside the house. He could only make out an indistinct outline in the moonlight.

The figure was extremely thin. The long hair and the shape of the chest and waist suggested a young woman.

That was when he made out the singing clearly.

> The beauty of night, > the twin moons can bear witness. > The beauty of the sea, > whether calm or surging, > you and I can bear witness. > For I have already heard > the tidings your whispers brought. May it carry this longing far away, that you may know.
