## Chapter 448: Return Home

Three days later, the walls of the Lordaeron capital finally came into view up ahead.

The road grew more crowded — people heading to the port, to the various farms, to Brill, to Stratholme in the Eastern Plaguelands. Crossing streams of travelers converged on the high gates of the capital.

But as he drew near, Jace spotted white-robed mages stationed one after another at the city gates.

The Soul Link let Jace feel Morophil's sudden tension. He knew these two demons would never get past the mages' inspection.

Orcish refugees were scattered throughout the Alterac Mountains — from Tirisfal Glades to Silverpine Forest to Hillsbraddock — and among them were no shortage of dark warlocks capable of summoning demons.

Though there'd been no news of Orgrim Doomhammer for a long time, the capital was surely worried that demons might infiltrate the city under the direction of warlocks and cause chaos.

Their concern was entirely justified — after all, Jace was about to bring two demons into the city himself. That was an instability factor in its own right.

Which meant he'd have to detour to Brill first and leave the two demons there.

As he walked along the woodland path heading north, Jace thought: if only he could summon these two demons to his side at any time, and when he didn't need them, send them back to the Twisting Nether — or wherever — to answer his call at a moment's notice.

But of course it wasn't as convenient as the game. Neither Galders nor Jagin had ever heard of any spell that could summon a demon on demand. And Morophil had mentioned that if a banishment spell sent her back to the Twisting Nether, the next time the same spell was used to summon a demon, it might not be her.

This probably represented two distinct schools of thought. One was Galders's approach — that a demon should be on familiar terms with the warlock, like a partner, a friend, a teammate, cooperating and helping each other, until eventually they even needed each other.

With this approach, both sides understood each other's abilities intimately and worked together more seamlessly. But there was no doubt that for warlocks, a group who always lived in the shadows, gaining a friend also meant gaining an attachment — and sometimes, a weakness.

Take Sazeno, for example. When Jace had first summoned this imp, he hadn't cared much about sending him on suicide missions — like charging into a gnoll cave just to see what was inside.

But if he were asked to do that now, he'd probably have a hard time making the call.

This imp was more or less a friend to him now. If he couldn't guarantee his safety, he'd likely find it difficult to send him on missions like that again.

Come to think of it, he really should learn a spell like the Eye of Kilrogg.

Jace remembered Jagin mentioning this spell — he'd called it the "Demon Eye Curse." Jagin's notes on the spell were now in Galders's possession. After returning to Stormwind, he should try asking him about it.

The other school of thought was Jagin's approach — treating summoned demons entirely as consumables, using them up and tossing them away, without cultivating any attachment.

This approach seemed more convenient on the surface. Whatever demon you needed… you'd just summon one, at most downing a mana potion on the spot to replenish yourself before dealing with the fight.

But this approach clearly worked fine for summoning lesser demons like imps or felhounds. Once you tried summoning a succubus or a more complex demon, the one you summoned would hardly obey you immediately… and that would multiply the trouble a hundredfold.

Better to imagine: you're in danger and need to summon a demon, only to find you have to fight the thing you just summoned? While your actual enemy seizes the chance to flank you.

The whole way, Jace walked with half-lidded eyes, gazing at the dark green woodland hills on either side of the road, the pumpkin farms blanketing the distant fields — many already ripe, a mass of golden-yellow dots from here.

Lower down, the wheat rippled in waves under the warm wind sweeping across Tirisfal. Even the forests on the hills at the edge of his vision, the white banners on the high watchtowers, swayed in rhythm.

As a Lordaeronian, spending time here made it easy to forget that this was "Tirisfal Glades" — the place full of wraiths and ghouls, with farmland gone to waste, great trees half-dead, and rotting, reanimated gnoll zombies shambling among them.

Thinking such thoughts, lost in the scenery of Tirisfal, Jace arrived in Brill almost on pure instinct. He'd walked this road countless times as a child — it had long since become muscle memory. He rounded a windmill on the hillside and saw the prosperous town in the lowlands beyond.

Brill was surrounded by the fields of several farms. In the earliest days it had actually been a large rural market. Later, as the capital to the south grew more prosperous, becoming the heart of all Tirisfal and the Lordaeron continent, Brill had developed into the handsome town it was today.

In the evening, Jace gazed out to the west, across the river. Up on the hilltop he could just make out a scattering of red-roofed houses — his mother's family farm. From here it didn't look like much, far smaller than the neighboring farms.

Of course, Jace barely knew anyone from his mother's side. He'd had little contact since childhood, even less as he grew up, and had never once visited.

The inn in Brill had a rather distinctive name — the "Journey's End Inn." At first glance it sounded a bit absurd, but on second thought, it actually made a certain sense.

Arrive here, stop your footsteps, rest a little — don't rush so hard…

He pushed open the door and caught the familiar scent of fried goat cheese mingled with the aroma of pumpkin wine.

He looked toward the counter from a distance. He knew the innkeeper — a kid he'd played with as a child. But ever since Jace had moved into the capital, the two had never seen each other again. By now the man probably didn't remember him at all.

"Boss, I'd like to book a room. Something out of the way. A week to start."

The young innkeeper stared at Jace for a moment, then said, "No problem, sir. By your accent, you're a local?"

Jace raised his eyes and looked at him. "I am. But I sold my house."

The innkeeper nodded. "Moved inside the walls, huh? There are too many people like that these days. A lot of folks say there are orcs in Tirisfal now, but I've never seen one."

"Don't orcs steal pumpkins?" Jace asked.

The innkeeper laughed. "I've heard it happen now and then, but I imagine they'd rather eat human meat. And you, sir — what can I get you?"

"Nothing for now."

Jace wanted to keep chatting, but in the end, to avoid being recognized and causing trouble for the inn, he cooled down, cut the conversation short, and hurried upstairs.
